I. The CIA Triad, Adversaries, and Controls

Every discipline of engineering rests on a small set of first principles that everything else derives from. In information security, that foundation is the CIA TriadCIA TriadThe three properties every security control ultimately tries to protect: Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability. Not the intelligence agency, a mnemonic for the three pillars of information assurance. Every attack degrades at least one of these three properties, and every defensive control exists to preserve at least one of them. Formalized in NIST SP 800-53 and ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A as the organizing model for security objectives. Sometimes extended to "AIC" to avoid confusion with the U.S. Central Intelligence Agency., a model formalized across decades of computer science and risk-management literature that identifies exactly three properties a secure system must preserve: confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Before a security professional can design a control, respond to an incident, or even describe an attack accurately, they must be able to state which of these three properties is under threat, because the answer changes what "success" looks like for the defender.

ConfidentialityConfidentialityEnsuring that information is only visible to the people authorized to see it, the opposite of a leak. A locked filing cabinet, an encrypted hard drive, and a "need to know" policy are all confidentiality controls. Enforced technically via access control lists (ACLs), encryption at rest/in transit, and role-based access control (RBAC). NIST SP 800-53 control family AC (Access Control) addresses confidentiality directly. is the property that unauthorized parties cannot read or infer protected data. IntegrityIntegrityEnsuring that data is accurate, complete, and has not been tampered with, either maliciously or accidentally. If someone can change your bank balance without authorization and without detection, integrity has failed even if confidentiality was never touched. Enforced via cryptographic hashing (SHA-256), digital signatures, checksums, version control, and write-access logging. NIST SP 800-53 control family SI (System and Information Integrity). is the property that data and systems remain accurate and unaltered except by authorized action, and that any unauthorized alteration is detectable. AvailabilityAvailabilityEnsuring that authorized users can access the systems and data they need, when they need them. A system that is perfectly confidential and perfectly accurate but offline during a hospital's shift-change is a security failure, patients cannot get care. Measured via uptime SLAs (e.g., "five nines" = 99.999% uptime), redundancy (RAID, failover clusters), and DDoS mitigation. NIST SP 800-53 control family CP (Contingency Planning). is the property that authorized users can reliably access systems and data when needed. These three properties are frequently in tension: encrypting a database (confidentiality) adds processing overhead that can slow legitimate access (availability); a strict lockout policy after failed logins (integrity of authentication) can itself become a denial-of-service vector if an attacker deliberately triggers lockouts on legitimate accounts.

The three properties pull against one another Think of a bank vault. Confidentiality is the vault door being closed so outsiders cannot see the cash. Integrity is the tamper-evident seals on the cash bundles so a teller can tell if someone swapped real bills for counterfeits. Availability is the vault opening on schedule every morning so tellers can actually do business. A vault that never opens is "secure" in the sense that nothing can be stolen, but it has failed its actual purpose. Security is never about maximizing lockdown; it's about balancing all three properties against the system's mission.

To reason precisely about the CIA triad, security professionals use a shared vocabulary. An assetAssetAnything of value that an organization needs to protect, a server, a database, a building, an employee's laptop, even a person's physical safety. Risk assessment always starts by inventorying assets, because you cannot protect what you haven't identified. NIST SP 800-30 defines asset identification as the first step of the Risk Management Framework (RMF) process. is anything of value worth protecting, hardware, data, personnel, or reputation. A vulnerabilityVulnerabilityA weakness in a system, process, or control that could be exploited to cause harm. An unlocked server-room door, an unpatched operating system, and an untrained receptionist who lets anyone "follow her in" are all vulnerabilities. Cataloged publicly in the CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) database for software; physical vulnerabilities are typically tracked internally via site security audits. is a weakness that could be exploited. A threatThreatAnything with the potential to exploit a vulnerability and cause harm, a person, a natural event, or a technical failure. A threat requires no vulnerability to exist (a burglar exists whether or not your door is locked) but causes no harm unless a vulnerability lets it act. NIST SP 800-30 distinguishes threat sources (who/what) from threat events (what specifically could happen). is any actor or event capable of exploiting a vulnerability. When a threat successfully exploits a vulnerability, the result is realized riskRiskThe potential for loss or damage when a threat exploits a vulnerability against an asset. Formally modeled as a function of likelihood and impact: Risk ≈ Threat × Vulnerability × Impact. Risk can never be reduced to zero, only reduced to an "acceptable" residual level. NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 formalizes qualitative and quantitative risk assessment methodologies, including risk matrices and Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE) calculations.. This chain, asset, vulnerability, threat, risk, is the analytical backbone of every security assessment covered in this unit, whether the "door" in question is a firewall rule or a literal door.

Social Engineering: Attacking the Decision, Not the Device

Every control described in this unit is ultimately operated by a person, and a person can be persuaded. Social engineeringSocial EngineeringManipulating a person into performing an action or disclosing information, rather than defeating a technical control directly. The target is the human decision procedure: who to trust, when to make an exception, whether to verify. Catalogued in MITRE ATT&CK under the Initial Access tactic (TA0001) as Phishing (T1566) and its sub-techniques, and under Reconnaissance as Phishing for Information (T1598). is the practice of manipulating a person into taking an action or revealing information, and it is the entry point for a large share of real intrusions precisely because it does not require a vulnerability in any product.

The recurring forms are worth naming precisely, because the defence differs by form.

  • Pretexting. The attacker invents a role and a reason for contact, an auditor needing a file, a courier needing a signature, an engineer from the lift maintenance company. The pretext supplies the target with a ready-made explanation, so the target never has to construct one, and constructing an explanation is exactly the moment at which suspicion would otherwise arise.
  • Phishing and its targeted variants. Mass phishing casts wide and cheaply; spear phishing is written for one named person using details drawn from public sources; whaling aims at an executive whose approval moves money or grants access. Vishing runs the same play over a phone call and smishing over SMS, both of which strip away the mail-security stack a defender has probably invested in.
  • Baiting. The attacker leaves something the target will want to use, a USB drive in a car park, a labelled disc in a lobby, and lets curiosity do the delivery step. A controlled study by Tischer and colleagues at the University of Illinois dropped 297 USB drives across a campus and found that 45 percent of them were not merely picked up but plugged into a computer and had files opened, with 98 percent of the drives removed from their drop points and the first one connected in under six minutes. The authors’ finding about motive is the more useful one for a defender: the dominant self-reported reason for connecting the drive was altruism, an attempt to identify the owner and return it (Tischer et al., Users Really Do Plug in USB Drives They Find, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy, 2016).
  • In-person approaches. Tailgating and piggybacking, treated in detail in Topic 2.2, are social engineering with a door attached. So is shoulder surfing a PIN pad and so is the impersonation of a delivery driver, the high-visibility vest is a pretext rendered in fabric.

What all of these exploit is not stupidity. It is a small set of ordinary and usually adaptive dispositions: deference to apparent authority, the reciprocity reflex that makes it awkward to refuse someone who has been helpful, the reluctance to cause a scene by challenging a stranger, and the compression of judgement that urgency produces. Robert Cialdini's catalogue of influence principles, authority, reciprocity, scarcity, commitment, liking, social proof, reads, in a security context, as a list of the levers an attacker will reach for. The practical consequence is that awareness training alone is a weak control, because it asks people to override dispositions under time pressure. Strong programmes pair training with procedures that remove the judgement call altogether: a verification callback to a directory number the caller did not supply, a payment-change process that cannot be completed by one person, a visitor policy in which escorting is the receptionist's job rather than the employee's improvisation.

Adversary Taxonomy

Attackers are not interchangeable. They differ in what they want, what they can afford to spend, and how much exposure they will tolerate, and those three variables predict which controls will actually stop them. Nation-state actorsNation-State ActorA hacking group funded, directed, or protected by a national government. These groups have the largest budgets, the most patience, and access to tools ordinary criminals don't, including zero-day exploits and insider recruitment. Their goals are usually espionage, sabotage, or geopolitical advantage rather than direct profit. Tracked by MITRE ATT&CK as named "Groups" (e.g., APT28, APT29, Lazarus Group). Often called Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) because of their long attack timelines. operate with government backing, near-unlimited resources, and long time horizons, they may sit inside a network undetected for years. Organized cybercriminal groupsOrganized Cybercriminal GroupA financially-motivated hacking operation, often run like a business, sometimes offering "as-a-service" tooling to other criminals (e.g., Ransomware-as-a-Service). Unlike nation-states, their objective is almost always direct monetary gain. Groups like Conti and LockBit have documented internal org charts with HR, developers, and negotiators. FBI IC3 tracks financial losses attributed to these groups annually. are financially motivated and highly efficient, often selling stolen access or data to other criminals. HacktivistsHacktivistAn attacker motivated by ideology or political protest rather than money, using hacking as a form of activism. Website defacement and denial-of-service against organizations they disagree with are common hacktivist tactics. Anonymous and its various offshoots are the most widely cited example. Hacktivist attacks tend to be high-visibility but lower in technical sophistication than nation-state operations. are ideologically driven and prioritize visibility over stealth. Insider threatsInsider ThreatA current or former employee, contractor, or partner who misuses their legitimate access to cause harm, either maliciously (a disgruntled employee stealing data on their way out) or accidentally (a well-meaning employee misconfiguring a system). Insiders are dangerous precisely because they already have the access an external attacker would have to work hard to obtain. Verizon's Data Breach Investigations Report (DBIR) consistently finds internal actors involved in roughly 20% of breaches, split between malicious and negligent categories. are uniquely dangerous because they bypass perimeter defenses entirely by virtue of already having legitimate access. Script kiddiesScript KiddieAn unskilled attacker who uses pre-built hacking tools and scripts written by others, without understanding the underlying mechanics. Low skill does not mean low impact, widely available tools can still cause real damage against unpatched, poorly defended targets. Contrast with "APT", script kiddies typically lack the patience or resources for long-duration, stealthy campaigns. represent the lowest end of the sophistication spectrum but remain a real threat because automated attack tools require little skill to deploy at scale.

Phases of an Attack

Two models dominate practice and they answer different questions. The Cyber Kill Chain, published by Hutchins, Cloppert and Amin at Lockheed Martin in 2011, is linear and coarse: seven stages, each of which the adversary must complete before the next, which is what licenses the central claim that breaking any one link defeats the whole campaign. MITRE ATT&CK is neither linear nor coarse. It organises observed adversary behaviour into tactics, the objective at a given moment, such as Initial Access or Persistence, and, beneath each, the specific techniques used to achieve it, documented with the detections and mitigations defenders have reported. An adversary in ATT&CK may revisit a tactic repeatedly, skip one entirely, or pursue two at once, which is closer to how intrusions actually unfold. The kill chain is the better instrument for arguing about layered controls; ATT&CK is the better working reference when you need to know what a specific technique looks like in a log.

Security professionals model attacks as sequential phases so that defenses can be mapped to the specific point in the chain where they are most effective, a framework popularized as the Cyber Kill ChainCyber Kill ChainA model developed by Lockheed Martin that breaks an intrusion into seven sequential phases: Reconnaissance, Weaponization, Delivery, Exploitation, Installation, Command & Control, and Actions on Objectives. The insight: breaking the chain at ANY single phase stops the whole attack, so defenders don't need to catch every single step, just one. Lockheed Martin (2011). Intelligence-Driven Computer Network Defense. Widely adopted alongside MITRE ATT&CK, which offers a more granular, non-linear alternative.. In the physical-security context of this unit, the same logic applies: reconnaissance (an intruder scouting building entrances and shift-change times), initial access (tailgating through a badge-controlled door), and actions on objective (stealing a server or planting a rogue device) are all discrete, defensible phases. The earlier in the chain a defender detects and interrupts the adversary, the lower the resulting impact.

A stolen laptop is a confidentiality failure Students sometimes assume the CIA triad only applies to networks and software. It does not. A stolen unencrypted laptop is a confidentiality breach delivered through a physical vector. A vandalized backup generator is an availability breach delivered through a physical vector. Every topic in this unit, tailgating, badge cloning, mantraps, CCTV, is simply the CIA triad applied to the physical world instead of the digital one. The rest of this unit is this chapter's vocabulary, reapplied.

Risk Assessment as a Formal Process

The reference procedure is NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1, Guide for Conducting Risk Assessments (2012), which structures the work in four steps: prepare for the assessment by fixing its scope, its assumptions, and the sources it will draw on; conduct it by identifying threat sources and threat events, the vulnerabilities and predisposing conditions that would let those events succeed, the likelihood of that succeeding, and the magnitude of the resulting harm; communicate the results to the people who will act on them; and maintain the assessment as the environment changes. Two features of that structure are easy to skip and expensive to skip. The first is the distinction between a threat source and a threat event: “a burglar” is a source and “forces the loading-dock door outside staffed hours” is an event, and only the event is specific enough to attach a likelihood or a control to. The second is that SP 800-30 splits likelihood into two questions, how likely is the event to be initiated or to occur, and, given that it occurs, how likely is it to result in adverse impact, because a control that stops an attempt and a control that limits the damage of a successful attempt are different investments.

SP 800-30 also expects assessments at three organisational tiers: the organisation as a whole, the mission and business processes it runs, and the individual information systems that support them. A risk that looks tolerable at the system tier can be intolerable at the mission tier once the dependency is visible, which is precisely the failure Colonial Pipeline demonstrates later in this chapter. Assessments are rated qualitatively (very low through very high), semi-quantitatively (bands mapped to numeric ranges so that scores can be ordered and compared), or quantitatively in currency, which is the form the following section works through. None of the three is inherently more rigorous; a quantitative figure derived from an invented exposure factor is a qualitative guess with false precision attached.

Reduced to its bones, the process is: (1) identify assets and assign them a value; (2) identify vulnerabilities and threats relevant to each asset; (3) estimate likelihood and impact to calculate risk; (4) decide on a risk response. Organizations do not defend everything equally; they defend proportionally to risk, and there are exactly four valid responses once a risk is understood: risk acceptanceRisk AcceptanceA formal decision to take no further action on a risk because the cost of mitigation exceeds the potential loss, or the risk is already within acceptable tolerance. This must be a documented, deliberate choice, not simply ignoring the problem. Requires sign-off from a risk owner in most governance frameworks (e.g., ISO 27005, NIST RMF Step 6, Authorize). (doing nothing further because the cost of mitigation exceeds the loss), risk mitigationRisk MitigationReducing either the likelihood or the impact of a risk by implementing a control, installing a lock, patching a vulnerability, training staff. This is the most common risk response and the primary subject of most security engineering work. NIST SP 800-30 distinguishes mitigation from elimination, mitigation reduces risk to an acceptable residual level, it rarely removes it entirely. (reducing likelihood or impact via a control), risk transferenceRisk TransferenceShifting the financial consequence of a risk to a third party, most commonly by purchasing cyber-insurance, or by outsourcing a risky function to a vendor who contractually assumes liability. The risk itself still exists; only the financial burden moves. Cyber-insurance premiums are increasingly tied to demonstrated security maturity (e.g., MFA enforcement) following a wave of ransomware payouts in the early 2020s. (shifting the cost via insurance or a vendor contract), and risk avoidanceRisk AvoidanceEliminating a risk entirely by discontinuing the activity or asset that creates it, for example, shutting down a legacy system rather than patching it indefinitely. The most drastic response, and the only one that reduces risk to zero, at the cost of the business function the asset provided. Often the correct answer for end-of-life systems where the cost of continued support exceeds the business value. (eliminating the activity that creates the risk entirely).

Acceptance is a decision, not an oversight Acceptance and neglect produce the same visible outcome, nothing is done, and are nevertheless different things. Acceptance is a named person recording that the residual risk has been measured, judged tolerable against a stated threshold, and assigned a review date; ISO/IEC 27005 and NIST SP 800-37 both require that signature, and its absence is what an auditor writes up. Transference is likewise narrower than it sounds. A cyber-insurance policy moves part of the financial consequence and moves none of the likelihood: the intrusion still happens, the data is still gone, the regulator still calls, and the insurer reimburses a capped fraction of the cost. The distinction matters at budget time, because a control that reduces ARO and a policy that reimburses SLE are not substitutes even when their annual prices match.

Categories of Security Controls

Controls are classified along two independent axes: their function and their category. By function, a control is preventivePreventive ControlA control designed to stop an incident before it happens, a locked door, a firewall rule, a badge reader. Preventive controls are the first line of defense but are never perfectly effective, which is why detective controls are also required. NIST SP 800-53 examples: AC-3 (Access Enforcement), PE-3 (Physical Access Control). (stops an incident before it occurs), detectiveDetective ControlA control designed to identify that an incident is happening or has happened, a security camera, a motion sensor, an audit log review. Detective controls don't stop the attack on their own; they generate the alert that triggers a response. NIST SP 800-53 examples: AU-6 (Audit Review), SI-4 (System Monitoring), PE-6 (Monitoring Physical Access). (identifies that an incident is occurring or occurred), correctiveCorrective ControlA control that limits damage after an incident and restores normal operations, restoring from backup, patching the exploited vulnerability, revoking a stolen badge. NIST SP 800-53 examples: CP-10 (System Recovery), IR-4 (Incident Handling). (limits damage and restores normal operation after an incident), deterrentDeterrent ControlA control that discourages an attacker from attempting an action in the first place, without physically stopping them, warning signage, visible security cameras, guard dogs. Deterrents work on attacker psychology, not physical or logical enforcement. Often overlaps visually with preventive controls (a locked gate deters AND prevents) but the distinguishing test is whether the control physically stops the action or merely discourages it. (discourages an attempt without physically stopping it), or compensatingCompensating ControlAn alternative control used when the primary recommended control cannot be implemented, providing equivalent protection through a different mechanism, e.g., increased manual log review when automated intrusion detection isn't available on legacy equipment. PCI-DSS formally defines compensating control criteria: the control must meet the intent of the original requirement and provide a comparable level of defense. (an alternative that provides equivalent protection when the ideal control cannot be deployed). By category, a control is technical (implemented via technology, like encryption or a card reader's cryptographic handshake), administrative/managerial (implemented via policy, training, and procedure, like a visitor sign-in policy), or physical (implemented via tangible barriers, like fences, locks, and lighting).

These two axes combine: a security camera is a physical, detective control. A locked server-room door is a physical, preventive control. A background-check policy is an administrative, preventive control. The two axes are genuinely independent: fixing one tells you nothing about the other, which is why a control register needs a column for each.

Defense-in-Depth

Defense-in-depthDefense-in-DepthA security strategy that layers multiple, independent controls so that if one fails, others still stand between the attacker and the asset, like a medieval castle's moat, wall, gate, and inner keep, each an obstacle in its own right. No single control is assumed to be perfect. Contrasted with "security through obscurity," which relies on secrecy of design rather than layered enforcement. NIST SP 800-53 explicitly recommends layered control implementation across the control families. is the organizing strategy that ties every control type together: no single control, however strong, is trusted as the sole line of defense. A data center might combine a perimeter fence (physical, deterrent/preventive), a badge reader (physical, preventive), a mantrap (physical, preventive), CCTV (physical, detective), an intrusion alarm (physical, detective), and a 24-hour security guard (administrative, preventive and detective) around the exact same asset. If an attacker defeats one layer, say, tailgating past the badge reader, the remaining layers still stand between them and the asset. This layered-redundancy principle is the connective thread running through every topic in this unit.

II. Quantifying Risk and Mapping Controls

While the CIA triad is often taught as a conceptual model, real security programs operationalize it through quantitative risk calculations and formal control mappings. This section works through the mathematics and documentation formats that translate the triad from theory into an auditable process.

Quantitative Risk Assessment

Formal risk assessment quantifies loss expectancy using a standard set of variables. The Single Loss Expectancy (SLE)Single Loss Expectancy (SLE)The expected monetary loss from a single occurrence of a risk event, calculated as Asset Value × Exposure Factor. If a $50,000 server has a 40% chance of total data loss during an incident, SLE = $50,000 × 0.40 = $20,000. A core variable in NIST SP 800-30 quantitative risk methodology, along with ARO and ALE. is calculated as Asset Value (AV) multiplied by the Exposure Factor (EF)Exposure Factor (EF)The percentage of an asset's value that would be lost if a specific risk event occurred, expressed as a decimal between 0 and 1. A fire that destroys 100% of a server room has an EF of 1.0; a fire that damages only some equipment might have an EF of 0.3. EF is typically estimated by subject matter experts or derived from historical incident data., the percentage of the asset's value expected to be lost in a single event. The Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO)Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO)The estimated number of times a specific risk event is expected to occur in one year. A flood expected once every 20 years has an ARO of 0.05. A phishing attempt expected 50 times a year has an ARO of 50. Derived from historical incident data, industry benchmarks (e.g., Verizon DBIR), or actuarial tables. estimates how often the event is expected to occur per year. Multiplying SLE by ARO yields the Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE)Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE)The total expected financial loss from a specific risk over one year, calculated as SLE × ARO. This is the number used to justify security spending: if a control costs $5,000/year and reduces ALE by $30,000/year, the control has a clear positive return. ALE is the central output metric of NIST SP 800-30 quantitative risk assessment, used directly in cost-benefit analysis for control selection., the number that directly justifies a security budget line item.

Risk Calculation Worksheet 
# Scenario: Unbadged tailgating leading to server theft at a branch office
Asset Value (AV)............... $80,000   (rack of production servers)
Exposure Factor (EF)........... 0.60      (60% of asset value lost per incident)
Single Loss Expectancy (SLE)... AV x EF = $80,000 x 0.60 = $48,000

Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO)... 0.25   (estimated once every 4 years)
Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE)...... SLE x ARO = $48,000 x 0.25 = $12,000

# Proposed control: mantrap + badge reader upgrade, $4,000/year amortized cost
# New ARO after control................ 0.05   (once every 20 years)
# New ALE............................... $48,000 x 0.05 = $2,400
# Risk reduction (ALE delta)............ $12,000 - $2,400 = $9,600/year
# Control cost-benefit................. $9,600 (savings) - $4,000 (cost) = +$5,600/year net benefit

This calculation is the quantitative backbone of every "should we buy this control" decision in a security budget. A control whose annual cost exceeds its ALE reduction is not economically justified, regardless of how effective it is technically, this is the analytical basis for the risk acceptance decision described in Section I.

Mapping Controls to the Triad and Control Matrices

Formal security programs document controls in a matrix that cross-references each control against the CIA property it protects, its function (preventive/detective/corrective/deterrent/compensating), and its category (technical/administrative/physical). This mapping is what an auditor reviews to verify that no CIA property is left with a single point of failure.

Control Register Excerpt 
Control ID  | Description                    | Category       | Function     | CIA Property
------------|--------------------------------|----------------|--------------|---------------
PE-3.1      | Badge reader at server room    | Physical       | Preventive   | Confidentiality
PE-6.1      | CCTV covering server room door | Physical       | Detective    | Confidentiality
PE-3.2      | Mantrap (interlocked doors)    | Physical       | Preventive   | Confidentiality
CP-9.1      | Nightly encrypted backups      | Technical      | Corrective   | Integrity, Availability

Rare and catastrophic outranks frequent and trivial Ranking risk by likelihood alone is the most common error in an immature risk register. A high-likelihood, low-impact event (an employee occasionally propping a door open for a smoke break) may represent lower overall risk than a low-likelihood, high-impact event (a targeted physical intrusion to steal a database server containing unencrypted customer PII). Risk is always the product of likelihood and impact, never either variable in isolation. This is why the ALE formula multiplies rather than adds SLE and ARO: a term near zero on either side drives the product toward zero, and neither term alone tells you anything about the product.

III. Case Studies: Target and Colonial Pipeline

Case Study 2.1.1: The 2013 Target Corporation Breach, A Failure Across All Three Properties

In November–December 2013, retail giant Target Corporation suffered a breach that compromised approximately 40 million credit and debit card records and 70 million customer records. The attackers gained initial network access using credentials stolen from Fazio Mechanical ServicesThird-Party Vendor RiskThe security risk introduced by external vendors, contractors, or partners who have been granted access into an organization's network or facilities. Even a small HVAC contractor can become the weak link in a much larger company's defense if their network access is poorly segmented. NIST SP 800-161 addresses supply chain and third-party risk management. The Target breach became the canonical case study for vendor risk in security curricula., a small HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) contractor with legitimate remote-access credentials to Target's vendor portal for billing and project management. Once inside, attackers moved laterally through a poorly segmented internal network to reach point-of-sale terminals, where they installed memory-scraping malware that captured card data as it was processed. This incident is an academically ideal case study because it demonstrates the failure of all three CIA properties simultaneously: confidentiality failed when card and personal data were exfiltrated; integrity failed when malware was installed on point-of-sale systems without authorization; and downstream availability impacts followed when Target had to take systems offline for remediation during its highest-revenue retail season.

The root-cause analysis pointed directly to a failure of defense-in-depth and network segmentation: a vendor account meant only for billing systems should never have had a network path to point-of-sale infrastructure. Target's own intrusion detection system (a detective control) had actually flagged the malware installation, but the security team did not act on the alert in time, illustrating that detective controls are worthless without a functioning response process behind them.

The alert fired and nobody acted on it Target's breach is often mischaracterized as "they had no detection." They did, the alert fired. The actual failure was procedural: no one triaged and escalated it in time. This is why every mature security program measures not just Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) but Mean Time to Respond (MTTR). A detective control that nobody watches is functionally identical to having no control at all.

Case Study 2.1.2: The 2021 Colonial Pipeline Ransomware Incident, An Availability Crisis

In May 2021, the criminal ransomware group DarkSideDarkSideA Russia-linked ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) criminal group that provided ransomware tooling to affiliate attackers in exchange for a cut of ransom payments. DarkSide targeted large organizations capable of paying substantial ransoms, a strategy sometimes called "big game hunting." The U.S. Treasury's OFAC sanctioned entities linked to DarkSide following the Colonial Pipeline incident. The group publicly announced it was disbanding shortly after, though its tooling reappeared under successor brands. compromised Colonial Pipeline, the largest fuel pipeline operator in the United States, using a single compromised VPN password found in a batch of leaked credentials, the account had no multi-factor authentication and was believed to be inactive. Although the ransomware itself infected only Colonial's IT (business) network, not its operational technology (OT) network that actually controls the pipeline, Colonial proactively shut down pipeline operations for nearly a week out of caution about the ransomware potentially spreading, and because their billing systems (needed to track fuel deliveries and invoice customers) were themselves compromised. The shutdown caused fuel shortages and panic-buying across the U.S. Southeast, demonstrating that an availability failure need not even involve the physical or operational system directly, a business-system disruption alone was sufficient to cascade into a real-world critical-infrastructure crisis. Colonial ultimately paid a ransom of 75 bitcoin (approximately $4.4 million at the time), a portion of which the FBI later recovered.

This case is frequently used to teach the concept of IT/OT convergence riskIT/OT Convergence RiskThe security risk created when Information Technology (IT) networks, email, business systems, billing, become interconnected with Operational Technology (OT) networks that control physical industrial processes, like pipeline valves or power grid relays. Historically these were "air-gapped" (physically separate), but modern efficiency demands have connected them, creating new attack paths from a corporate phishing email all the way to physical equipment. NIST SP 800-82 governs Industrial Control System (ICS) security specifically because of this convergence risk.: as business (IT) networks and physical control (OT) networks become more interconnected for efficiency, a purely digital compromise on the business side can force an availability shutdown of physical, real-world infrastructure, precisely the intersection of cybersecurity and physical security this unit addresses.

One password, no second factor Colonial Pipeline's entire crisis traces back to a single reused, non-MFA-protected VPN password. This is the same "single point of failure" concept the misconceptions in this chapter warn against: a firewall, a badge reader, or a VPN gateway is never sufficient alone. Defense-in-depth would have layered MFA, network segmentation between IT and OT, and anomaly-based monitoring on top of the VPN credential, any one of which likely would have stopped or contained this incident.

IV. Governance Frameworks: NIST RMF, ISO 27001, and Defense in Depth

Organizations formalize the CIA triad, risk assessment, and control taxonomy into governance frameworks so that protection decisions are consistent, auditable, and defensible.

1. The NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF)

NIST SP 800-37 defines a seven-step Risk Management Framework (RMF)NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF)A structured, repeatable seven-step process for identifying, assessing, and managing information security risk across an organization: Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize, Monitor. Required for U.S. federal information systems under FISMA, and widely adopted voluntarily elsewhere. NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2 (2018) is the current governing publication. that operationalizes everything covered in this chapter: categorizing systems by impact level, selecting controls from NIST SP 800-53's control catalog, implementing and assessing those controls, formally authorizing the system to operate, and continuously monitoring it thereafter. Every control discussed in this unit, badge readers, mantraps, CCTV, maps to a specific control family in NIST SP 800-53's "PE" (Physical and Environmental Protection) series.

2. ISO/IEC 27001 and the Information Security Management System (ISMS)

ISO/IEC 27001ISO/IEC 27001An international standard specifying requirements for establishing, implementing, and continually improving an Information Security Management System (ISMS). Organizations can be formally certified as ISO 27001-compliant by an accredited auditor, a widely recognized signal of security maturity to customers and partners. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 is the current revision. Annex A lists 93 controls across 4 themes, directly mirroring the CIA triad and control-category taxonomy taught in this chapter. requires organizations to run a continuous Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle around an Information Security Management System, explicitly grounded in confidentiality, integrity, and availability objectives for every asset in scope. Its Annex A control list is organized into organizational, people, physical, and technological control themes, a direct real-world parallel to the administrative/physical/technical taxonomy taught in Section I.

3. Building a Defense-in-Depth Control Matrix

Practically, hardening an asset means walking through every phase of the kill chain and every CIA property, and ensuring at least one preventive and one detective control exists at each layer, with a documented corrective response plan if both fail. For a facility housing sensitive servers, this typically means: perimeter fencing and lighting (deterrent/preventive), badge and biometric access control (preventive), a mantrap for the server room specifically (preventive), CCTV with retained footage (detective), an intrusion alarm tied to a monitored security operations center (detective), a documented incident response and disaster recovery plan (corrective), and a visitor escort policy with signed logs (administrative, preventive and detective).

Name the property before naming the control Naming the property under threat is not a rhetorical flourish; it constrains which controls are even candidates. A stolen laptop threatens confidentiality through data exposure and availability for the employee who lost their working machine, and it threatens integrity only if the attacker returns the device or its contents in altered form. Those three diagnoses lead to three different remedies, full-disk encryption, a spare-hardware and backup policy, and signed or hash-verified data respectively. An engineer who skips the diagnosis tends to buy the control they already had in mind.

Self-Check Review Questions

Question 1: Define the three properties of the CIA triad and explain, using the Target Corporation breach, how a single incident can compromise all three simultaneously.

The CIA triad defines confidentiality (data visible only to authorized parties), integrity (data and systems remain accurate and are altered only by authorized action), and availability (authorized users can access systems and data when needed). In the 2013 Target breach, confidentiality failed when attackers exfiltrated approximately 40 million card records and 70 million customer records that should have remained restricted. Integrity failed when attackers installed unauthorized memory-scraping malware on point-of-sale terminals, altering the software state of those systems without authorization. Availability was subsequently impacted when Target was forced to take systems offline during remediation, degrading the retailer's ability to process transactions during its highest-revenue period. This demonstrates that real-world incidents rarely compromise a single CIA property in isolation, lateral movement and cascading effects typically implicate all three.

Question 2: A company installs a $4,000/year mantrap upgrade to reduce the annualized rate of occurrence of server-room tailgating incidents from 0.25 to 0.05, where the Single Loss Expectancy per incident is $48,000. Calculate the Annualized Loss Expectancy before and after the control, and determine whether the control is economically justified.

Annualized Loss Expectancy is calculated as ALE = SLE × ARO. Before the control: ALE = $48,000 × 0.25 = $12,000 per year. After the control: ALE = $48,000 × 0.05 = $2,400 per year. Risk reduction (delta ALE) = $12,000 − $2,400 = $9,600 per year. Because the mantrap costs $4,000 per year and produces $9,600 per year in reduced expected loss, the net annual benefit is $9,600 − $4,000 = $5,600. Since the benefit exceeds the cost, the control is economically justified under a cost-benefit risk mitigation analysis, and the organization should proceed with mitigation rather than accepting, transferring, or avoiding the risk.

Question 3: Distinguish between a control's function (preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, compensating) and its category (technical, administrative, physical), and classify a "quarterly badge access review" along both axes.

A control's function describes what point in the incident lifecycle it acts upon: preventive controls stop an incident before it occurs, detective controls identify an incident occurring or having occurred, corrective controls limit damage and restore normal operation afterward, deterrent controls discourage an attempt without directly stopping it, and compensating controls substitute for a primary control that cannot be implemented. A control's category describes the mechanism used to implement it: technical (enforced through technology), administrative/managerial (enforced through policy and procedure), or physical (enforced through tangible barriers). A quarterly badge access review is an administrative, detective control. It is administrative because it is enforced through a documented policy and human process rather than a technical mechanism or physical barrier. It is detective, not preventive, because the review identifies stale, unauthorized, or excessive access grants after they were provisioned, rather than preventing the initial grant from occurring, though its findings typically drive a subsequent corrective action, such as revoking unneeded access.

Free Response 2 2.1.A 2.1.C 2.1.G, A visitor in a high-visibility vest carrying a ladder is admitted to a corporate lobby, is waved through a badged door by an employee who assumes he is expected, and spends twenty minutes alone in an unlocked wiring closet. (a) Identify the social engineering technique and the human tendency it relies on. (b) Place the incident on the stages of an intrusion, naming at least three stages and the specific action at each. (c) Propose three controls at three different stages, state each one’s function and category, and explain why three layered controls are a materially stronger answer than replacing the badge reader with a better one.

Answer Key

1. B2.1.A

The caller has invented a role and a reason for contact, a pretext, and delivered it by voice, and the deadline supplies the urgency that compresses the clerk’s judgement. The claimed auditor role is the authority lever. A describes baiting, which requires the target to pick up and use something the attacker left behind; nothing is left behind here. C requires physical presence at a controlled door, and the attack is remote. D requires the attacker to observe a credential being entered; no observation of a screen or keypad occurs. Note that the correct defence follows from the correct diagnosis: a callback to the supplier on a number taken from the vendor file, rather than a number the caller supplied, defeats the pretext without relying on the clerk’s scepticism.

2. C2.1.B

Two signals converge: the objective is intelligence, design documents rather than money, and the operation is sustained for years without monetisation, which implies a funder who is not paid in ransom. A is inconsistent with the profile; the defining limitation of a low-skill attacker is dependence on someone else’s tooling, which does not support multi-year stealth. B fails on visibility: hacktivism seeks publicity, and quiet document theft achieves none. D is unwarranted, since long dwell time is a property of the intrusion rather than evidence of employment; external actors routinely maintain persistence for extended periods.

3. B2.1.C

Dependence is what makes the model useful. If stage five cannot proceed without stage four, then a control that works at stage four is sufficient on its own, and the defender is not obliged to be right everywhere. A inverts the asymmetry: the adversary must succeed at every stage, the defender at only one. C overclaims, the model describes objectives, not tooling; MITRE ATT&CK catalogues techniques, and even it does not predict which will be used. D discards the dependency the model is built on and would leave every earlier stage uncontested.

4. B2.1.D

SLE = AV × EF = $80,000 × 0.60 = $48,000. ARO for “once every four years” is 0.25. ALE = SLE × ARO = $48,000 × 0.25 = $12,000. A is the SLE, the loss from one occurrence, reported as though it were annual. C applies the exposure factor to a quarter of the asset value, or equivalently confuses which factor multiplies which. D applies the 0.25 rate twice. The habitual check is dimensional: SLE is dollars per event, ARO is events per year, and only their product has the units of an annual budget line.

5. C2.1.E

Insurance moves a defined portion of the financial consequence to a third party. The breach remains equally likely and equally damaging; the policy reimburses after the fact, subject to limits and exclusions. A misclassifies it, since mitigation requires a change to likelihood or impact and no such change has been made. B would require discontinuing the activity, which the firm has not done. D conflates two responses: acceptance is a documented decision to bear the residual risk with no offsetting arrangement, and purchasing an offsetting arrangement is precisely what distinguishes transference from it.

6. A2.1.F

The camera is a tangible device installed at a physical boundary, so it is physical by category. Functionally it does two things: the monitored recording identifies an incident in progress or after the fact, which is detective, and the conspicuous mounting and signage discourage the attempt, which is deterrent. Controls commonly carry more than one function. B misassigns the category, the “technical” label is reserved for controls enforced through system logic, and misassigns the function besides. C describes policy instruments and post-incident restoration, neither of which a camera performs. D is the frequent error: a camera records an intrusion and does nothing to stop one, so it is not preventive, however strongly it may deter.

FR 12.1.D · 2.1.E

Model answer. (a) SLE = $120,000 × 0.35 = $42,000. ARO = 1/5 = 0.2. ALE = $42,000 × 0.2 = $8,400 per year. (b) New ARO = 1/25 = 0.04, so new ALE = $42,000 × 0.04 = $1,680. Risk reduction = $8,400 − $1,680 = $6,720 per year. Net benefit = $6,720 − $3,200 = $3,520 per year, so on these figures the control is justified; the residual $1,680 is then a candidate for documented acceptance. (c) Insurance is transference. The difference that survives equal pricing is that the control changes ARO and therefore reduces the number of events that happen at all, whereas the policy leaves the event rate untouched and reimburses a capped, excess-adjusted fraction of the loss afterwards. Consequences the policy does not reverse are worth naming: the outage while the cabinet is replaced, the loss of any data held on it, the regulatory exposure, and the reputational cost. A defensible answer may also note that insurers increasingly price premiums against demonstrated controls, so the two are complements rather than alternatives.
Look for: correct conversion of “once every n years” into ARO = 1/n; SLE and ALE distinguished rather than used interchangeably; the net benefit computed against the control’s cost rather than against the gross ALE; and, in (c), a difference stated in terms of which variable moves (ARO versus the financial consequence), not merely the assertion that insurance “does not prevent anything”.

FR 22.1.A · 2.1.C · 2.1.G

Model answer. (a) Pretexting carried out in person, with the vest and ladder as the physical props that make the invented maintenance role legible at a glance; the employee’s tendencies in play are deference to an apparently authorised worker and reluctance to challenge someone who appears to belong, reinforced by the assumption that reception has already vetted the visitor. (b) Reconnaissance: establishing that contractors are common on site and that the lobby is not staffed continuously. Delivery: arriving at the lobby in the guise that reconnaissance selected. Exploitation: passing the badged door on an employee’s credential rather than his own. Installation: twenty unobserved minutes in an unlocked wiring closet, which is where a rogue device would be placed. A response may also name command and control, if it argues the device would need an outbound path. (c) Three controls at three stages, for example: a staffed reception with a contractor-verification step against the day’s work order, administrative and preventive, acting at delivery; an interlocked door or turnstile admitting one person per credential, physical and preventive, acting at exploitation; a keyed and contact-alarmed wiring closet reporting to a monitored panel, physical, preventive in the lock and detective in the alarm, acting at installation. The argument for layering is that each control has a known failure mode and those failure modes are independent: a better badge reader does nothing about tailgating, because tailgating never challenges the reader; the receptionist can be deceived by a better pretext; the alarm can be ignored if nobody is monitoring. Three independent controls fail together only if three unrelated things go wrong at once, whereas a single strong control fails whenever its one weakness is found.
Look for: the technique named as pretexting or in-person impersonation rather than the generic label “social engineering”; at least three stages, each attached to a specific action from the scenario rather than a definition; three controls that act at genuinely different stages, each labelled on both axes; and an independence argument in part (c). A response that lists three controls all acting at the same door has not made the layering argument and should not receive full credit.
