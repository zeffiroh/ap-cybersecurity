# 4.2.d tutor notes

Notes for the pre- and post-lecture tutor posts.

## The intuition to hold onto
Walk into this skill with a single sharp question ready: if this account gets phished tomorrow, what's the worst thing the attacker inherits? The principle of least privilege isn't a compliance checkbox, it's a bet that you can't stop every credential from eventually leaking, so the only lever left is making sure each individual leak is cheap. A marketing intern's read-only drive access being compromised is an inconvenience; the same intern's account having write access to production is a company-wide incident, and the only difference between those two outcomes is a permission that never should have been granted in the first place.

## The big picture
The four access-control models (DAC, MAC, RBAC, ABAC) are easiest to keep straight if you ask who's making the decision and when: DAC lets the resource owner decide, case by case, which is why it scales badly, permissions sprawl with nobody enforcing consistency. MAC decides centrally with fixed labels, trading flexibility for near-total resistance to insider misconfiguration. RBAC decides by role, which is why it dominates enterprise IT, changing someone's job means reassigning a role, not hand-editing dozens of permissions. ABAC decides dynamically, at the moment of the request, using context like time of day or device posture, trading simplicity for real-time responsiveness. Notice that privilege creep is really the same silent-decay problem you saw with configuration drift already waiting for you in 4.3.a, access that was correct on day one degrades into a liability because revoking permissions requires someone to notice and act, while granting them is just a routine part of onboarding. That's exactly why periodic access review, separation of duties, and just-in-time access all exist: not to get the permission model right once, but to keep re-proving it's still right as roles quietly change underneath it.
