# 3.2.c tutor notes

Notes for the pre- and post-lecture tutor posts.

## The intuition to hold onto
Walk into this skill already knowing its punchline, because it's worth having ahead of time: every control this unit has covered so far, DNSSEC, ARP inspection, encryption, WPA3, an AUP, only stops *one specific kind* of attack, and none of them overlaps with any of the others. WPA3 does nothing whatsoever against DNS poisoning. DNSSEC does nothing whatsoever against a socially engineered password handover. That's not a weakness in any individual control; it's the reason defense in depth exists as a principle at all, and this skill is where all of Unit 3's separate pieces finally get organized into one picture instead of feeling like a list of unrelated topics.

## The big picture
The layer model here is worth memorizing by function, not just by name, because the exam wants you to *place* a novel control correctly, not recite the list: perimeter stops obviously bad traffic at the edge, network limits how far an attacker can move once they're past the edge, host protects the individual device assuming the network layer failed, application protects the logic running on that device, data protects the payload itself as the innermost and last line of defense, and human/managerial doesn't occupy a physical location at all, it spans and reinforces every other layer, which is exactly why 3.2.b's AUP and awareness training get filed here rather than off in their own silo. The genuinely important idea underneath all six layers is this: depth doesn't mean more security products, it means covering *non-overlapping* attack vectors, so that an attacker who defeats one layer's control still has to separately defeat the next layer's completely different control. Every remaining atom in this unit, VLANs, zero trust, firewalls, IDS/IPS, monitoring, incident response, is really just this skill's model getting one layer at a time filled in with concrete machinery.
