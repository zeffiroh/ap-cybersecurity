I. Attacking the Network Fabric

While Unit 1 examined the exploitation of human cognition, Unit 3 shifts the locus of attack to the network fabricNetwork FabricThe interconnected mesh of switches, routers, cabling, and protocols that carries data between devices. Just as a "fabric" is woven from many threads, a network fabric is woven from many interdependent links and protocols, each with its own trust assumptions. Modern enterprise fabrics are built on Ethernet (IEEE 802.3) at Layer 2 and IP (RFC 791 / RFC 8200) at Layer 3. itself, the wires, switches, and protocols that move packets from one host to another. Network-level exploits do not trick a human into clicking a link; they exploit design assumptions baked into protocols that were standardized decades ago, often in an era when the internet's user base was a small, trusted community of academic and government researchers. The foundational insight of this chapter is that many core networking protocols, ARPARP, Address Resolution ProtocolThe protocol that maps a known IP address to the physical (MAC) address of the device that owns it on a local network. Think of it as shouting "who has this apartment number?" in a building and waiting for someone to yell back their unit's exact location. Defined in RFC 826 (1982). Operates at the boundary of Layer 2 and Layer 3, and has no authentication whatsoever., TCPTCP, Transmission Control ProtocolThe protocol that provides reliable, ordered, connection-oriented delivery of data between two hosts. It uses sequence numbers, acknowledgments, and a handshake to guarantee that bytes arrive in order and without loss. Originally RFC 793 (1981), obsoleted and consolidated by RFC 9293 (2022)., and DNS, were engineered for functional correctness and resilience against random failure, not for resilience against an actively malicious participant on the same wire.

This distinction is often formalized in security curricula as the difference between a fail-safe designFail-Safe vs. Fail-OpenA fail-safe system defaults to a secure state when something goes wrong (e.g., a firewall that blocks traffic if its rule engine crashes). A fail-open system defaults to an available state (e.g., a network switch that keeps forwarding frames even if it cannot verify origin). Most legacy protocols are fail-open by design because availability was prioritized over confidentiality in early internet engineering. See Saltzer & Schroeder (1975), "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems," for the original articulation of fail-safe defaults as a security design principle. and a fail-open design. ARP, for instance, is fail-open: any device on a local segment can claim ownership of any IP address, and other devices will believe it without verification, because rejecting unverified claims would have historically caused more operational disruption than it prevented. This chapter examines how threat actors weaponize this trust-by-default architecture to intercept traffic, deny service, and propagate malicious code, the three canonical categories of network-level exploitation: eavesdroppingEavesdropping (Passive Interception)Silently capturing network traffic without altering it, in order to read confidential data such as credentials, session tokens, or proprietary information. The attacker does not need to be "in the path" of the traffic if they can place a sensor that sees a copy of it. Passive attacks are far harder to detect than active attacks because they leave no modified traffic behind, only anomalous physical/logical access., denial of service, and lateral propagation.

Understanding network-level exploits requires fluency in the OSI reference modelOSI Model, Open Systems InterconnectionA conceptual seven-layer framework (Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application) used to describe how data moves through a network stack. It is a teaching and design abstraction rather than a protocol itself, real-world stacks like TCP/IP compress several OSI layers together. Standardized by ISO/IEC 7498-1. Most network exploits discussed in this chapter operate at Layer 2 (Data Link, ARP), Layer 3 (Network, IP), or Layer 4 (Transport, TCP/UDP)., since the layer at which an attack occurs determines both its blast radius and its available defenses. An attack executed at Layer 2 (the local Ethernet segment) is inherently confined to that broadcast domain, whereas an attack executed at Layer 3 or above, such as a reflection-based denial of service attack, can traverse the entire routed internet and be launched from anywhere in the world. This layered thinking is the conceptual backbone of the entire chapter and reappears in every technical subsection below.

A protocol that trusts the wire is trusting whoever else is on it Early network engineers designed protocols under the assumption that if you had physical access to a cable or a switch port, you were already an authorized member of the organization, a security guard had already checked your badge at the door. That assumption collapsed with the rise of shared coffee-shop Wi-Fi, compromised IoT devices sitting on the same VLAN as finance workstations, and insider threats. Almost every exploit in this chapter is really just a modern attacker exploiting a 1980s assumption: "if you're on my wire, I trust you."

It is also useful to distinguish between active attacksActive AttackAn attack in which the adversary injects, modifies, or replays traffic, changing the state of the network or the victim's session. Active attacks are detectable in principle because they leave traces, unexpected ARP replies, malformed packets, unusual retransmissions. Contrast with passive attacks, which only observe. ARP spoofing, SYN floods, and worm propagation are all active attacks. and passive attacks in the network context. Passive attacks, such as simple packet sniffing on a shared medium, involve no interaction with the victim and are correspondingly difficult to detect. Active attacks, such as ARP cache poisoning or a SYN flood, require the attacker to inject forged or malformed traffic into the network, and therefore leave a detectable, if often subtle, footprint that intrusion detection systems can be tuned to recognize. The remainder of this chapter is organized around five specific exploit families: packet sniffing, ARP spoofing, man-in-the-middle interception, denial-of-service attacks, and network-borne malware propagation.

II. Sniffing, ARP Poisoning, DNS Poisoning, Hijacking, and Denial of Service

Packet Sniffing: Promiscuous Mode, Hubs, and Switches

A network interface card (NIC)Network Interface Card (NIC)The hardware component that connects a computer to a network, either physically (Ethernet) or wirelessly (Wi-Fi). Every NIC has a globally unique 48-bit MAC address burned into it at manufacture. By default, a NIC only accepts frames addressed to its own MAC address or to broadcast/multicast addresses, discarding all other traffic at the hardware level. normally discards any Ethernet frame not addressed to its own MAC addressMAC Address, Media Access Control AddressA 48-bit hardware identifier assigned to a network interface, typically written as six hex byte-pairs (e.g., 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E). It operates at Layer 2 and is used by switches to decide which physical port to forward a frame to. The first 24 bits (the OUI) identify the manufacturer; IEEE assigns OUI blocks. MAC addresses can be spoofed in software trivially, unlike IP addresses which require routing cooperation.. Placing the NIC into promiscuous modePromiscuous ModeA NIC operating state in which the card passes every frame it physically receives up to the operating system, regardless of destination MAC address, instead of silently discarding frames not addressed to it. This is the fundamental technical enabler of packet sniffing tools like Wireshark and tcpdump. On Linux, enabled via `ip link set eth0 promisc on`; on wireless adapters the equivalent capability is called "monitor mode," which additionally exposes 802.11 management/control frames. disables that filter, allowing a sniffing application such as WiresharkWiresharkThe most widely used open-source graphical packet analysis tool, capable of dissecting hundreds of protocols and reassembling full TCP streams for inspection. Built on the libpcap/WinPcap capture library. Originally released as "Ethereal" in 1998; renamed Wireshark in 2006 after a trademark dispute. tcpdump is its command-line, libpcap-based sibling. to capture and display every frame that physically reaches the interface. Whether promiscuous mode actually yields useful traffic, however, depends entirely on the physical topology of the network.

On a legacy hubNetwork HubAn obsolete Layer 1 device that electrically repeats every incoming signal out to all other ports simultaneously, with no awareness of MAC addresses or frame boundaries. A hub effectively turns an entire network segment into one giant collision domain. Hubs have been almost entirely replaced by switches since the early 2000s due to both performance and security concerns.-based network, every frame is physically replicated to every port, so an attacker plugged into any port receives a copy of all traffic on the segment "for free." Modern networks instead use switchesNetwork SwitchA Layer 2 device that learns which MAC address lives on which physical port (by observing source addresses) and then forwards each frame only out the port where the destination MAC was last seen, rather than broadcasting to every port. This dramatically improves performance and provides an incidental security benefit: a plain sniffer on one switch port cannot see unicast traffic destined for a different port. Switches maintain a CAM (Content Addressable Memory) table mapping MAC addresses to ports; if the table is full or the MAC is unknown, the switch floods the frame to all ports as a fallback., which forward unicast frames only to the specific port associated with the destination MAC address in the switch's CAM tableCAM Table, Content Addressable Memory TableThe switch's internal lookup table mapping learned MAC addresses to the physical port they were last observed on. It is populated dynamically by inspecting the source MAC of every incoming frame and has a finite size (commonly a few thousand to tens of thousands of entries). A "CAM table overflow attack" (MAC flooding) deliberately exhausts this table with forged source MACs, forcing the switch into fail-open flooding mode so the attacker's sniffer receives everything, a classic case of a fail-safe assumption becoming fail-open under resource exhaustion.. This means a plain sniffer on a switched network only sees broadcast traffic, multicast traffic, and unicast traffic explicitly addressed to its own port, a significant improvement over hubs, but far from a complete defense.

To legitimately capture traffic across an entire switched segment for monitoring or forensic purposes, network administrators configure port mirroringPort Mirroring / SPAN, Switched Port AnalyzerA switch feature that copies traffic seen on one or more source ports (or an entire VLAN) to a designated destination port, where a monitoring device such as an IDS sensor or packet capture appliance is attached. This provides visibility without disrupting normal traffic forwarding. Cisco calls this feature SPAN (local) or RSPAN/ERSPAN (remote, tunneled across Layer 2/3). It is the standard mechanism used by network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) like Zeek and Suricata to obtain a copy of live traffic., often called SPAN (Switched Port Analyzer) in Cisco terminology. An attacker without administrative access to the switch, however, cannot configure a SPAN session and must instead resort to Layer 2 manipulation, most commonly ARP spoofing, to coerce traffic to flow through their own machine.

ARP and ARP Cache Poisoning

The Address Resolution Protocol (ARP)ARP, Address Resolution ProtocolSee above. ARP resolves a known IPv4 address into the corresponding MAC address needed to actually deliver an Ethernet frame on the local segment. RFC 826, "An Ethernet Address Resolution Protocol" (November 1982). IPv6 replaces ARP with NDP (Neighbor Discovery Protocol, RFC 4861), which has its own analogous spoofing risks., standardized in RFC 826RFC 826The original 1982 IETF specification defining ARP. It predates virtually every modern security consideration and contains no authentication, sequencing, or integrity-protection mechanism of any kind. Plummer, D.C. (1982). An Ethernet Address Resolution Protocol. IETF RFC 826., operates through a simple broadcast request/unicast reply exchange. When Host A needs to send an IP packet to Host B on the same subnet but only knows B's IP address, A broadcasts an ARP Request ("Who has 192.168.1.20? Tell 192.168.1.10") to the Ethernet broadcast address FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF. Host B, recognizing its own IP in the request, unicasts an ARP Reply containing its MAC address directly back to A. Both hosts cache the resulting IP-to-MAC mapping locally in an ARP cacheARP CacheA local, dynamically populated table on each host mapping recently resolved IP addresses to MAC addresses, maintained to avoid repeating the broadcast request/reply exchange for every packet. Entries typically expire after a short timeout (commonly 60-1200 seconds depending on OS). View on Linux with `ip neigh show` or on Windows with `arp -a`. to avoid repeating the exchange for every packet.

The critical vulnerability is that ARP is entirely statelessStateless Protocol DesignA protocol in which each message is processed independently, without the receiver verifying it against any prior request or expected sequence. ARP replies are accepted whether or not the receiving host ever sent a corresponding request. Contrast with TCP, which uses sequence and acknowledgment numbers specifically to prevent unsolicited or out-of-order segments from being accepted. and unauthenticated: a host will accept and cache an ARP reply even if it never issued the corresponding request, and it has no cryptographic means to verify that the reply actually came from the legitimate MAC owner of that IP. This is compounded by gratuitous ARPGratuitous ARPAn ARP announcement a host sends unprompted, an ARP reply (or a request for its own IP) broadcast without anyone having asked, typically used legitimately to announce a new IP address, detect IP conflicts, or update peers after a failover event (e.g., in VRRP/HSRP). Because ARP has no authentication, this same mechanism is trivially abused to unilaterally overwrite every host's cache on the segment. Described informally in RFC 5227 (IPv4 Address Conflict Detection) though gratuitous ARP predates that RFC as an informal convention., a legitimate feature that lets a host unilaterally announce or update an IP-to-MAC mapping without being asked, precisely the mechanism an attacker abuses to poison neighboring caches.

An ARP spoofingARP Spoofing / ARP Cache PoisoningAn active Layer 2 attack in which the adversary sends forged ARP replies (or gratuitous ARP announcements) associating their own MAC address with the IP address of a legitimate host, typically the default gateway, causing victim hosts to send traffic intended for that IP to the attacker instead. Classified under MITRE ATT&CK T1557.002 (Adversary-in-the-Middle: ARP Cache Poisoning). attack chain proceeds in four stages: (1) the attacker, already present on the local segment, sends forged gratuitous ARP replies to the victim claiming that the attacker's MAC address owns the gateway's IP address; (2) simultaneously, the attacker sends forged ARP replies to the gateway claiming that the attacker's MAC address owns the victim's IP address; (3) both the victim and the gateway update their ARP caches with the false mapping, and all traffic between them now physically transits the attacker's machine; (4) the attacker enables IP forwarding on their own machine so traffic is relayed onward transparently, preserving connectivity while silently duplicating every packet for inspection or modification, a technique broadly known as an on-path attackOn-Path Attack (formerly "Man-in-the-Middle")An attack in which the adversary positions themselves on the actual data path between two communicating parties, able to observe and optionally modify traffic in both directions. ARP spoofing is the most common local-network mechanism for establishing this position. NIST and MITRE have increasingly adopted "adversary-in-the-middle" (AiTM) or "on-path attacker" terminology to avoid gendered language while preserving technical precision..

ARP poisoning requires a foothold on the segment, which bounds the damage A common student error is assuming ARP spoofing can be launched from anywhere on the internet. It cannot. ARP is a Layer 2 protocol confined to a single broadcast domain (a single VLAN/subnet). The attacker must already have a foothold, a compromised device, a rogue Wi-Fi client, or physical access, on the same local segment as the victim. This is exactly why network segmentation and VLANs are a first-line defense: they shrink the blast radius of any ARP-based attack to a small group of hosts rather than the entire enterprise.

Man-in-the-Middle Attacks and SSL Stripping

Once an attacker has achieved an on-path position via ARP poisoning (or via a rogue access point, discussed in Topic 3.2), they can passively log traffic or actively tamper with it. A particularly potent active technique is SSL strippingSSL StrippingA man-in-the-middle downgrade attack in which the attacker transparently intercepts a victim's initial plaintext HTTP request, establishes the encrypted HTTPS connection to the real server on the victim's behalf, but serves the content back to the victim over unencrypted HTTP, silently rewriting all HTTPS links in the page to HTTP so the victim's browser never actually negotiates its own TLS session. The victim sees a working page with no obvious padlock, and the attacker reads (and can modify) all "encrypted" traffic in plaintext. Publicly demonstrated by Moxy Marlinspike at Black Hat DC 2009 with the tool `sslstrip`. Largely mitigated today by HSTS (HTTP Strict Transport Security, RFC 6797) and browser HSTS preload lists., which exploits the fact that users typically type a bare domain name without specifying "https://", causing the browser to first request the insecure HTTP version of a site before any redirect to HTTPS occurs. The on-path attacker intercepts this first plaintext request and thereafter proxies the session, keeping the victim on HTTP while transparently maintaining the encrypted session with the legitimate server, capturing credentials and session cookies as they pass through in the clear.

DNS Cache Poisoning and the Kaminsky Attack

ARP forges an answer inside one broadcast domain. DNS forges an answer that a recursive resolver then hands to every client behind it, which is why a successful cache poisoning is a far larger event than a successful ARP spoof. The mechanism rests on the transport DNS was given in RFC 1035: a query goes out over UDP, and the resolver accepts the first response that matches on four things, source address, destination port, question section, and a sixteen-bit transaction identifier. Nothing in that list is a secret that an off-path adversary cannot guess or arrange to know.

The classical race is therefore straightforward. An adversary induces the resolver to look up a name, then floods it with forged answers bearing the authoritative server's source address, each guessing a different transaction identifier. If the real answer arrives first, the resolver caches it, and the adversary must wait for that record's time-to-live to expire before trying again, against a well-chosen TTL, hours. With a sixteen-bit identifier as the only entropy, one guess in 65,536 succeeds; but it was the TTL lock-out, far more than the entropy, that kept the attack impractical.

What Dan Kaminsky demonstrated in 2008, coordinated as CERT/CC Vulnerability Note VU#800113 and catalogued as CVE-2008-1447, published 8 July 2008, was that the lock-out could be removed entirely. Rather than attacking www.example.com, the adversary asks for a name that certainly is not cached: aaaa001.example.com, then aaaa002.example.com, and so on. Each miss forces a fresh outbound query and therefore a fresh race. The forged reply does not answer the nonsense name usefully. It carries, in its authority and additional sections, an in-bailiwick referral asserting that the nameserver for the zone is ns.example.com and that its address belongs to the adversary. A resolver that accepts glue for the zone it is querying caches that delegation, and from that moment the adversary is authoritative for the whole domain rather than for one hostname. Because the race can be re-run thousands of times per second, a one-in-65,536 chance becomes a near certainty within a minute.

The emergency fix bought entropy, not authenticity, and the difference matters The mitigation every resolver vendor shipped in July 2008 was source port randomization, later documented in RFC 5452. Instead of querying from one predictable UDP port, the resolver draws a fresh ephemeral port for each query, adding roughly sixteen more bits the adversary must guess and moving the search space from about 216 to about 232. That converts a one-minute attack into an impractical one. It does not make a forged answer detectable; it makes a forged answer improbable. The control that actually authenticates the answer is DNSSEC, which signs records so that a resolver can verify a chain from the root, and DNSSEC's deployment problem, that it protects nothing unless the zone signs and the resolver validates, is precisely why a probabilistic defence is still load-bearing more than fifteen years later.

TCP Session Hijacking and the Value of an Unguessable Sequence Number

TCP authenticates nothing. A segment is accepted into a connection when its addresses and ports match an existing socket pair and its sequence number falls within the receiver's current window. An adversary who can supply those values can inject data into somebody else's session. Steven Bellovin set this out in his 1989 survey Security Problems in the TCP/IP Protocol Suite, observing that implementations of the day advanced the initial sequence number by a fixed increment on a fixed timer, which made it predictable to an adversary who could not see the traffic at all.

The distinction that governs the defence is between an on-path and an off-path adversary. An on-path adversary, one who already holds the position in Figure 3.1.1, reads the sequence numbers directly, so hijacking becomes bookkeeping: emit a segment carrying the correct next sequence number and a plausible acknowledgement, and the receiver treats it as data. The usual follow-on is a reset sent to the party being cut out, so that the two ends do not desynchronize noisily and expose the intrusion. An off-path adversary must guess, and the modern countermeasure is to price the guess out of reach: RFC 6528 specifies that the initial sequence number be a timer value plus a keyed hash over the connection's four-tuple, so that sequence numbers are unpredictable across connections while still advancing monotonically within one. Ephemeral source port randomization removes further bits from the guess.

Two consequences carry into the risk assessment in Section IV. Sequence-number hardening is a control against off-path injection alone; it does nothing about an adversary holding a copy of the traffic, which is why link-layer controls and transport encryption are not substitutes for one another. And encryption above TCP protects the payload but not the connection: a forged reset with a plausible sequence number tears down a TLS session exactly as effectively as a plaintext one, which is why long-lived routing protocols such as BGP acquired a separate authentication option (RFC 5925) instead of relying on the transport to defend itself.

Denial of Service: SYN Floods, Volumetric Attacks, and Amplification

Denial-of-service attacks are typically classified into three layers: volumetric attacks, which simply saturate available bandwidth with high-throughput junk traffic (measured in Gbps/Tbps); protocol attacks, such as SYN floods, which exhaust finite state tables in network stacks rather than raw bandwidth (measured in packets per second); and application-layer attacks, such as HTTP floods that repeatedly request computationally expensive dynamic pages, exhausting web server or database resources with comparatively low traffic volume (measured in requests per second). IV. Layer 2 Hardening, DoS Resilience, and Patching

Defense against network-level exploits requires controls at multiple layers of the stack, since no single control addresses sniffing, ARP spoofing, denial of service, and worm propagation simultaneously.

1. Layer 2 Hardening: Dynamic ARP Inspection and Port Security

Network segmentation via VLANs further limits the blast radius of any successful Layer 2 attack to a single broadcast domain rather than the entire enterprise.

3. Patch Management and Protocol Deprecation to Prevent Worm Propagation

The WannaCry case study demonstrates that timely patch management remains the single highest-leverage defense against worm propagation exploiting known CVEs. Organizations should additionally disable legacy, high-risk protocols such as SMBv1 entirely wherever compatibility permits, restrict SMB/port 445 traffic at internal firewalls between network segments rather than only at the perimeter, and deploy network-based intrusion detection tuned to recognize anomalous internal lateral-scanning behavior, a strong indicator of an active worm regardless of the specific exploit used.

4. Documenting the Risk So That Somebody Else Can Act on It

Identifying an exposure is not the deliverable. The deliverable is a record that survives the analyst leaving the room, and NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 gives such a record its shape: a named threat source, the event that source would carry out, the vulnerability that makes the event possible, the likelihood of initiation and the likelihood of success, the impact if it succeeds, and the resulting risk, each on a scale the organisation agreed in advance rather than one invented per finding. The discipline the standard enforces is the separation of likelihood from impact. A flat guest VLAN that reaches the finance segment and a resolver that does not validate DNSSEC are not comparable until each has been decomposed that way.

A worked entry for the exposure in Figure 3.1.1 reads: threat source, an unprivileged party with access to the guest wireless segment; threat event, ARP cache poisoning to obtain an on-path position; vulnerability, guest and staff devices share one broadcast domain and Dynamic ARP Inspection is not configured on the access switches; likelihood of initiation, high, because the tooling is packaged and the segment is open to visitors; likelihood of success given initiation, high, because no control contests the forged reply; impact, moderate rather than high, because the traffic of interest is carried over TLS with HSTS, so the realistic loss is metadata and the reachability of exposed local services rather than credentials; risk, high; residual risk after enabling DHCP snooping and Dynamic ARP Inspection on the guest VLAN, low. That final clause is the one most often omitted and the one a decision-maker actually needs, because it is what separates a finding that has been closed from one that has merely been described.

No single control covers the whole chapter No single control in this chapter covers the whole attack surface, and the reason is structural rather than incidental: these exploits operate at different layers, so the defences have to as well. At Layer 2, dynamic ARP inspection, port security and VLAN segmentation answer ARP-based sniffing and on-path attacks, none of which a firewall higher in the stack ever sees, because the frames never reach it. At Layers 3 and 4, SYN cookies, ingress filtering (BCP 38) and upstream scrubbing answer volumetric and state-exhaustion denial of service. Neither group touches worm propagation, which is answered instead by patch management and protocol deprecation, removing SMBv1 rather than filtering it. The useful discipline is to name the layer a control operates at before naming the control, because a defence described without its layer is not yet a defence; it is a product name.

Self-Check Review Questions

Question 1: Explain the four-stage mechanics of an ARP cache poisoning attack and identify why Dynamic ARP Inspection is an effective countermeasure.

ARP cache poisoning exploits the fact that ARP, as defined in RFC 826, is a stateless and unauthenticated protocol, a host will accept and cache an ARP reply even if it never issued the corresponding request. The attack proceeds in four stages: first, the attacker sends forged gratuitous ARP replies to the victim host, falsely claiming that the attacker's MAC address is bound to the default gateway's IP address; second, the attacker simultaneously sends forged ARP replies to the gateway, falsely claiming ownership of the victim's IP address; third, both the victim and the gateway overwrite their legitimate ARP cache entries with these forged mappings, causing all traffic between them to be physically routed through the attacker's machine; fourth, the attacker enables IP forwarding to transparently relay the intercepted traffic onward, preserving apparent connectivity while enabling passive eavesdropping or active tampering (an on-path/man-in-the-middle position). Dynamic ARP Inspection is effective because it removes the trust-by-default assumption at the switch level. Rather than allowing any ARP packet to be forwarded unconditionally, DAI validates each ARP packet's claimed IP-to-MAC binding against a trusted binding table, which is populated automatically from DHCP snooping records reflecting legitimate DHCP lease assignments. Any ARP packet whose claimed binding does not match a known legitimate lease is dropped at the switch before it ever reaches its target, preventing the forged replies from ever poisoning a victim's cache in the first place.

Question 2: Compare and contrast a protocol-layer denial-of-service attack (SYN flood) with a volumetric reflection/amplification attack (DNS amplification), including the specific resource each exhausts.

A SYN flood is a protocol-layer attack that exploits the mechanics of the TCP three-way handshake as defined in RFC 9293. The attacker sends a high rate of SYN packets, often with spoofed source addresses, without completing the handshake with a final ACK. Each unacknowledged SYN causes the victim's operating system to allocate an entry in its finite connection backlog queue while awaiting the reply; once this table is exhausted, the server can no longer accept new legitimate connections. The resource exhausted is finite kernel connection-state memory, and the attack is typically measured in packets per second rather than raw bandwidth. This is mitigated primarily through SYN cookies, which defer state allocation until the handshake is cryptographically proven complete. A DNS amplification attack, by contrast, is a volumetric reflection attack that exploits connectionless UDP-based DNS resolvers. The attacker sends a small DNS query to an open recursive resolver with the source IP address spoofed to match the intended victim; the resolver, believing the victim originated the query, sends a disproportionately large response (for example, a large ANY or DNSSEC record set) directly to the victim's address. The resource exhausted here is the victim's inbound network bandwidth itself, and the attack is measured in aggregate throughput (Gbps/Tbps) rather than connection-state exhaustion. Mitigation relies on network-edge source address validation (BCP 38), which prevents the spoofed queries from ever being originated, and on disabling open recursive resolvers that serve as amplification vectors.

Question 3: Using the WannaCry/EternalBlue incident as a case study, explain how a network worm differs mechanically from a denial-of-service attack, and identify the primary organizational failure that enabled its scale.

A network worm, as exemplified by WannaCry, differs fundamentally from a denial-of-service attack in that it achieves actual remote code execution on the victim host rather than merely exhausting a finite resource. WannaCry propagated by exploiting EternalBlue (CVE-2017-0144), a remote code execution vulnerability in Microsoft's SMBv1 protocol implementation, to run arbitrary code with SYSTEM-level privileges on any reachable host running the vulnerable service, with zero user interaction required. After gaining execution, the worm installed the DoublePulsar backdoor implant, dropped its ransomware payload, and then autonomously scanned the local subnet for additional SMB-exposed hosts on TCP port 445, repeating the exploit chain to self-replicate across the network. Availability loss (encrypted, inaccessible files) was a downstream side effect of this code-execution-and-replication chain, not the direct mechanism of the attack itself, which is the key distinction from a DoS attack that never gains code execution at all. The primary organizational failure that enabled WannaCry's scale was not a novel zero-day but a widespread failure of patch management: Microsoft had released the MS17-010 security update patching this exact vulnerability a full month before the outbreak began. Organizations that had applied routine, timely patching were not susceptible to the worm's propagation mechanism, meaning the global scale of the incident is best explained by systemic gaps in vulnerability management practice across healthcare, logistics, and telecommunications sectors rather than by any inherent unpatchability of the flaw.

Answer Key

1. B3.1.A

A switch learns which hardware address sits behind which physical port by inspecting the source address of arriving frames, and thereafter forwards a unicast frame only out that one port. The capturing interface therefore never receives most of the segment's traffic, and no software setting on the capturing host can conjure frames the cable never carried. A is wrong because promiscuous mode governs what the interface passes up to the operating system, not what the switch delivers to the port; the observed result is exactly what a working promiscuous capture looks like on a switched network. C is wrong because ordinary wired Ethernet applies no link-layer encryption, had the frames arrived, they would have dissected normally. D inverts the mechanism: the restriction is imposed by the forwarding hardware, not by the operating system, which is why an attacker who can overflow the address table and force the switch to flood recovers visibility without touching the host at all.

2. C3.1.B

ARP as specified in RFC 826 is stateless and unauthenticated: a reply is cached on arrival whether or not a matching request was ever sent, and the protocol contains no signature, nonce, or credential against which the claimed address-to-hardware binding could be checked. The gratuitous announcement, a legitimate mechanism for failover and conflict detection, gives the adversary a supported way to overwrite a cache unprompted. A is a fabrication; ARP has no cryptography of any kind. B is a category error: ARP is not carried over UDP or IP at all but rides directly in an Ethernet frame with its own EtherType, which is also why the attack cannot be launched from outside the broadcast domain. D is false, Ethernet frames do carry a source address, but that address is set by software and can be written to any value, so its presence establishes nothing.

3. B3.1.B

The pre-2008 obstacle was not the sixteen-bit transaction identifier but the caching that followed a lost race: once the genuine answer was cached, the adversary had to wait out the record's time-to-live before another attempt. Querying names that cannot be in the cache removes that wait, so the race can be repeated continuously, and placing the forgery in the authority and additional sections as an in-bailiwick referral poisons the delegation for the whole zone rather than one hostname. A is false; the attack forges unsigned answers and involves no key recovery, which is exactly why DNSSEC is the control that would have defeated it. C misidentifies the class of flaw, CVE-2008-1447 is a protocol weakness affecting multiple independent implementations, not a memory-safety bug in one of them, which is why the coordinated response spanned every vendor. D is backwards: the identifier stayed sixteen bits, and the fix added roughly sixteen further bits of entropy through source port randomization.

4. B3.1.A, 3.1.B

Half-open connections filling a finite backlog while bandwidth remains modest is the signature of a protocol-state attack: the exhausted resource is kernel connection state measured in packets per second, not link capacity measured in bits per second. SYN cookies address it directly by encoding the connection state into the sequence number of the reply, so that no memory is committed until the client's final acknowledgement proves the handshake genuine. A misreads the evidence, 40 Mbit/s saturates nothing, and scrubbing capacity buys nothing against an attack that is not consuming bandwidth. C does not fit either: an application-layer flood produces completed connections and expensive requests, not half-open ones, and caching addresses request cost rather than handshake state. D is excluded because nothing here indicates code execution or propagation, only exhausted connection state.

5. B3.1.B

Unpredictable initial sequence numbers, computed as a timer plus a keyed hash over the four-tuple, deny an adversary who cannot see the connection any way to place a segment inside the receiver's window. That is the entire scope of the control. A is the case it does not help: an on-path adversary reads the sequence numbers off the wire, so their unpredictability to a stranger is irrelevant. C confuses layers, injecting or resetting a connection is not the same as reading the TLS records it carries, and sequence numbers have no bearing on the session keys. D is a different attack on a different resource: a SYN flood does not require guessing anything, since it never completes a handshake, and it is answered by SYN cookies rather than by sequence-number hardening.

6. B3.1.C

A register entry exists so that findings can be ranked and funded. Collapsing likelihood and impact into one undefined word makes ranking impossible, and omitting the residual position leaves a reader unable to tell whether anything already deployed reduces the exposure or what the exposure would be after remediation, the two facts a decision-maker needs. A is a real gap in a penetration-test report but not in a risk register, whose subject is the exposure rather than the tooling. C states a rule that does not exist: NIST SP 800-30 explicitly supports qualitative and semi-quantitative scales, and a fabricated numeric probability would be worse than an agreed qualitative band. D asserts an impact without assessing it, which is the same error the entry already commits; where the sensitive traffic is carried over TLS with HSTS, the realistic loss is metadata and local service exposure rather than credentials.

7. Free response3.1.B, 3.1.C

Model answer. In ARP the trusted item is the address-to-hardware binding asserted in a reply. The receiver caches it unsolicited and has nothing to check it against, so one forged frame redirects the segment's traffic. The control is Dynamic ARP Inspection backed by DHCP snooping: the switch holds an independently obtained table of legitimate bindings and drops any ARP packet on an untrusted port that contradicts it. In DNS the trusted item is a response that merely matches the query on source address, destination port, question, and a sixteen-bit identifier, and, in the Kaminsky variant, the delegation carried in the authority and additional sections of that response. The probabilistic control is source port randomization under RFC 5452, which widens the guess from roughly 216 to roughly 232; the authenticating control is DNSSEC, which signs records so the resolver verifies rather than guesses. In TCP the trusted item is a sequence number falling inside the receiver's window, and the control against an off-path adversary is unpredictable initial sequence numbers under RFC 6528, supported by ephemeral port randomization. The three are not interchangeable because each operates at the layer that owns the unverified field: Dynamic ARP Inspection is enforced by a switch inside one broadcast domain and can say nothing about a resolver's cache; DNSSEC authenticates records and does nothing about a forged reset; sequence-number hardening protects a connection and does nothing about the name that connection was opened to. Blast radius differs for the same structural reason. ARP is confined to a single broadcast domain and reaches the hosts on it. TCP injection reaches one connection, though it can be repeated. DNS cache poisoning reaches every client that consults the poisoned resolver, and in the delegation variant it captures an entire zone rather than one record, which is why it is the most serious of the three and why it warranted a coordinated multi-vendor release.

A grader should look for: (i) the specific unverified item named in each case, the cached binding, the four matching fields plus the in-bailiwick referral, the in-window sequence number, rather than a general statement that the protocol is insecure; (ii) a correct control paired with each, with DNSSEC distinguished from port randomization as authentication versus entropy; (iii) an explicit reason the controls do not substitute, framed by the layer at which enforcement happens; (iv) blast radius ordered with justification, ARP bounded by the broadcast domain and DNS unbounded by resolver population. An answer that lists the three attacks without identifying the trusted field has not met the objective.

Model answer. Threat event one: a visitor on the guest access point poisons ARP caches to obtain an on-path position between the record workstations and the gateway. Vulnerability: guests and clinical systems share one broadcast domain and no Dynamic ARP Inspection or DHCP snooping is configured. Likelihood of initiation high, likelihood of success high, impact moderate where record traffic is TLS-protected but higher where any internal service is plaintext; risk high. Threat event two: an adversary reaches the unpatchable imaging device over a vulnerable network service and executes code on it. Vulnerability: the device exposes a legacy service to every host in the same domain, including guest devices, and no compensating boundary exists; a vendor patch is unavailable, so the exposure is permanent rather than a scheduling problem. Likelihood of initiation moderate, likelihood of success high given reachability, impact high because the device is clinically load-bearing and cannot simply be rebuilt; risk high. Threat event three: malware on a guest laptop propagates autonomously to Windows workstations over a file-sharing service, as in the WannaCry pattern, with no user action anywhere in the chain. Vulnerability: an internal service is reachable across the whole domain and no internal filtering exists between the guest population and clinical systems. Likelihood moderate, impact high, risk high. The control to fund first is segmentation: place guest wireless on its own VLAN with no route to clinical systems, and place the imaging device in its own segment permitting only the specific flows its workflow requires. One change reduces all three events, because each depends on shared reachability rather than on any particular exploit. Residual risk after segmentation: the ARP event drops to low and is bounded to hosts inside a segment rather than eliminated, since ARP remains forgeable within any single broadcast domain; the imaging exposure drops to moderate, because reachability is now restricted to authorized clinical hosts, and it cannot fall further while the device remains unpatchable; the propagation event drops to low. The residual figures, not the gross ones, are the argument for the budget.

A grader should look for: (i) three genuinely distinct threat events, each with its enabling vulnerability stated as a property of the design rather than as a restatement of the attack; (ii) likelihood and impact assessed separately, with at least one impact justified by something other than the attack's name, clinical dependence, or the presence of transport encryption; (iii) a single prioritized control with an explicit reason it addresses more than one event; (iv) residual risk given per event, including a case where residual risk remains above low, and an acknowledgement that segmentation bounds rather than eliminates a link-layer attack. An answer that recommends every control in the chapter without prioritizing, or that omits residual risk, has answered a different question.
