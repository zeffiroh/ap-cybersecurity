# 3.5.b tutor notes

Notes for the pre- and post-lecture tutor posts.

## The intuition to hold onto
The habit to bring into this lecture, carried straight over from 3.5.a's anomaly-detection idea: you cannot know what's abnormal until you've actually measured what's normal first. That's not a throwaway line, it's the entire justification for baselining, and it reframes network monitoring as fundamentally a measurement discipline before it's ever a detection discipline. Skip the baseline and every alert is just a guess dressed up as a finding.

## The big picture
The three indicators worth being able to name cold, unusual port usage, beaconing, and volume spikes, share a single underlying idea worth remembering as one thing rather than three: none of them require reading a single byte of payload to be useful. A host beaconing to a command-and-control server at suspiciously regular intervals is detectable purely from *timing*, a data-exfiltration spike is detectable purely from *volume and direction*, and that's precisely why flow records (cheap, payload-free summaries) can catch what a full packet capture would only confirm after the fact at far greater storage cost. That's also the connective tissue linking this skill back to 3.1.d's packet-sniffing atom from earlier in the unit: encryption defeats an attacker trying to *read* your payload, but it does absolutely nothing to hide the *pattern* of your traffic, which is exactly the signal network monitoring is built to exploit on defense instead. Layer this skill on top of 3.5.a and 3.4's firewall atoms and you get the complete three-tier picture this unit has been building toward the whole time: the firewall decides what's allowed to start, the IDS matches what's already known to be bad, and monitoring catches everything left over, attacks using fully permitted ports and protocols, with no matching signature, that only give themselves away through volume, timing, or destination drifting off the baseline.
