# 5.6.c tutor notes

Notes for the pre- and post-lecture tutor posts.

## The intuition to hold onto
The habit worth building before this lecture, and really before the rest of your life in this field: an alert is a hypothesis, not a verdict. A burst of failed logins suggests brute-force, but it could just as easily be a broken script retrying a stale password, the analyst's job starts, not ends, when the alert fires, and jumping straight to a conclusion without checking the underlying evidence is the single most common way a real incident gets mishandled: wrong IP blocked, wrong password reset, real attack vector missed entirely.

## The big picture
As the final atom of the final unit, this is a genuinely fitting place to zoom out. Every detection tool this course has built, 5.6.a's log patterns, 5.6.b's DLP alerts, 5.2.c's lateral-movement flags, even 2.4's physical access-log anomalies from Unit 2, produces exactly the same kind of output: an indicator of compromise, a single observable fact that is rarely conclusive by itself. What separates a mature security program from an alert-driven panic is this skill's whole point, collecting multiple IOCs, weighing how strongly each one supports a given attack category, and reasoning to the best-supported classification rather than the first plausible one. Chain of custody matters for the same reason parameterized queries mattered back in 5.5.b: it's a structural guarantee (a hash taken at collection time, a documented access log) rather than a promise to be careful, which is exactly the pattern this whole course keeps returning to, trust the mechanism, not the good intentions. And notice the last thread this skill pulls taut: MITRE ATT&CK's shared vocabulary is what lets the risk-triage instinct from Unit 2's very first atom (name the threat, then reason about it) scale from one analyst's judgment call to an entire organization's, and an entire industry's, shared understanding of what actually happened.
