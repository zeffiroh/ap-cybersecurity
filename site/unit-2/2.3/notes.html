I. Locks, Credentials, Zones, and the Design of a Space

Where Topic 2.2 examined how attackers defeat physical perimeters, this chapter examines the managerial and technical controls organizations deploy to prevent those attacks in the first place. Physical access controlPhysical Access ControlThe set of mechanisms, mechanical, electronic, procedural, that restrict entry to a facility or space to only those individuals who are authorized. The physical-world equivalent of an authentication and authorization system, governing who may pass through a door rather than who may log into an account. NIST SP 800-53 control family PE (Physical and Environmental Protection), specifically controls PE-2 (Physical Access Authorizations) and PE-3 (Physical Access Control), governs this domain. systems exist to answer the same two questions asked by every digital access-control system: who are you (authentication), and are you allowed to be here (authorization), just applied to a door instead of a login prompt. This parallel is not a coincidence or a teaching device; the same principle of least privilegePrinciple of Least PrivilegeGranting a person only the minimum access necessary to perform their job function, and nothing more. A finance employee does not need access to the server room; a network administrator does not need access to the executive suite. Reduces both the attack surface and the potential damage from a single compromised credential. NIST SP 800-53 control AC-6 (Least Privilege) formally requires this for both logical and physical access under a unified access-control philosophy. that governs which files an employee's network account can touch also governs which doors their badge can open.

Locks and Mechanical Access Control

The oldest and most fundamental physical access control is the mechanical lock. Locks are typically classified by the mechanism they use to verify a key's authenticity: pin tumbler locksPin Tumbler LockThe most common mechanical lock design, using a series of spring-loaded pin stacks inside the lock cylinder. Only a key cut to the exact correct height at each position will push every pin to align precisely at the "shear line," allowing the cylinder to rotate. Vulnerable to lock-picking (manipulating pins individually) and "bumping" (a specially cut key that jars all pins simultaneously). Invented in its modern form by Linus Yale Jr. in the 1860s; still the dominant residential and light-commercial lock mechanism today. rely on a precisely cut key aligning a series of spring-loaded pins; electronic locksElectronic LockA lock actuated by an electronic signal rather than a purely mechanical key, commonly controlled by a badge reader, keypad, or centralized access-control system. Electronic locks enable centralized management: access can be granted or revoked instantly and remotely, and every open/close event can be logged, neither of which is possible with a traditional mechanical lock and key. Typically wired to a door strike or electromagnetic lock, controlled by an access-control panel that references a centralized authorization database. substitute a badge, PIN, or biometric read for the physical key, enabling centralized, revocable, and logged access decisions that a mechanical key can never provide. This distinction matters enormously from a managerial-control standpoint: a lost mechanical key typically requires re-keying the entire lock (and every other lock sharing that key's master system) to restore security, whereas a lost or stolen badge can be deactivated instantly in the access-control database without touching the physical hardware at all.

Credential Factors and Multi-Factor Physical Authentication

Just as digital authentication is classified into factors, physical access credentials fall into the same three categories: something you have (a physical key, a badge, a fob), something you know (a PIN code entered on a keypad), and something you are (a biometricBiometric AuthenticationVerifying identity using a unique, measurable physical or behavioral characteristic, fingerprint, iris pattern, facial geometry, voice, or even gait. Biometrics cannot be "forgotten" like a password and are harder to steal than a physical badge, but they also cannot be changed if compromised, and false-accept/false-reject rates must be carefully tuned. NIST SP 800-76 governs biometric data specifications for U.S. federal personal identity verification (PIV) systems. Measured using False Acceptance Rate (FAR) and False Rejection Rate (FRR). characteristic such as a fingerprint or iris scan). A facility relying on a badge alone is single-factor; requiring a badge plus a PIN, or a badge plus a fingerprint, constitutes true multi-factor physical authentication, directly mitigating the badge-cloning risk covered in Topic 2.2, because a cloned badge alone is no longer sufficient to gain entry.

The same two factors, applied to a door The structure of multi-factor authentication is identical whether the resource is an account or a room. A badge (something you have) plus a PIN (something you know) is exactly as conceptually sound as a password plus a TOTP code, and just as vulnerable if implemented poorly, such as writing the PIN on a sticky note taped to the reader.

Mantraps, Visitor Management, and Escort Policy

Beyond the point of entry, organizations layer additional managerial controls governing how non-employees move through a facility. A visitor logVisitor LogA recorded, typically time-stamped register of every non-employee who enters a facility, including their name, the person or department they are visiting, their time in and out, and often a signed acknowledgment of the facility's security policies. Serves both as a detective control (creating an audit trail) and a deterrent (visitors know their presence is recorded). NIST SP 800-53 control PE-8 (Visitor Access Records) requires retention of visitor access records for a defined period, commonly one to three years depending on regulatory context. creates an auditable record of every non-employee who enters, supporting both deterrence and post-incident investigation. An escort policyEscort PolicyA managerial control requiring that visitors, contractors, or other non-authorized individuals be accompanied by an authorized employee at all times while inside a facility, particularly in restricted areas. Prevents an otherwise-legitimate visitor (a delivery driver, an auditor) from wandering unsupervised into sensitive areas. A core control in ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A physical security controls and NIST SP 800-53 PE-2/PE-3. requires that a badge-carrying employee physically accompany any visitor for the duration of their presence, closing the gap between "authorized to be in the building" and "authorized to be anywhere in the building unsupervised." These are administrative, preventive/detective controls that complement, rather than replace, the technical and physical controls described above, a visitor log does nothing to physically stop an intruder; it only ensures a record exists if something goes wrong.

Policy is a control, and it fails in its own way The classification from Topic 2.1 applies unchanged here, and it has a practical use: it predicts what each control will not do. A badge reader is a physical, preventive, technical control. A visitor sign-in sheet is an administrative, detective control. Neither alone is sufficient, an organization with excellent badge readers but no visitor policy still has a gaping hole: anyone who can talk their way past a receptionist can move freely once inside, unbadged and unaccompanied.

The Managerial Layer: Policy, Procedure, and the Governance of Access

Hardware decides what a door will do; managerial controls decide what the organisation intends, who is accountable when intention and practice diverge, and how the divergence is discovered. NIST SP 800-53 sorts controls into managerial, operational and technical classes for exactly this reason, and its PE (Physical and Environmental Protection) family is largely a list of managerial obligations: authorise the people who may enter (PE-2), control and verify entry (PE-3), escort and record visitors (PE-6 and PE-8), and review the results. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 places the same material in its physical controls theme. The documents differ; the structure does not.

Four instruments carry most of the weight.

  • A physical security policy states the organisation’s position and delegates authority: which zones exist, who may authorise access to each, what the standard of proof is for identity, and who owns the decision. It is short and it changes rarely. Beneath it sit standards (every server-room door shall have a door-position contact reporting to the monitored panel) and procedures (how a contractor is booked in on a Tuesday). Conflating the three is the commonest drafting failure: a policy that specifies hardware becomes obsolete on the next procurement, and a procedure with no policy behind it has no authority when someone declines to follow it.
  • Credential lifecycle governance covers issuance, modification and, the step that actually fails, revocation. Access accumulates: a person transfers between departments and keeps both sets of rights, a contractor’s engagement ends and their badge does not. The controls are an authorisation record for every grant, a periodic recertification in which the manager who owns a space affirms each name on its list, and an offboarding procedure joined to the HR record rather than to anyone’s memory. A recertification that a manager can complete by approving the whole list in one action is a control in name only.
  • Visitor and contractor governance distinguishes being authorised to be in the building from being authorised to be anywhere in it unaccompanied. The substantive requirements are that a visit is booked in advance against a named host, that identity is verified against something the visitor did not produce from their own pocket, that the badge is visually distinct and time-limited, that escort is an assigned duty rather than a courtesy, and that the log is reconciled at the end of the day so that a visitor who never signed out is noticed the same evening. The Barclays branch intrusion in Topic 2.2 failed at the first of these: nobody checked the engineer against a work order, because there was no procedure requiring it.
  • Separation of duties and oversight. The person who approves an access request should not be the person who provisions the badge, and neither should be the person who audits the result. Where headcount makes that impossible, the compensating arrangement is an independent periodic review, and the fact that it is compensating should be recorded as such.

Designing the Space Itself: CPTED

Crime Prevention Through Environmental DesignCPTEDA design discipline holding that the physical arrangement of a space influences the behaviour that occurs in it, and that layout, sightlines, lighting and boundary treatment can reduce the opportunity for crime before any security hardware is installed. The term is C. Ray Jeffery’s (1971); the closely related concept of “defensible space” is Oscar Newman’s (1972). CPTED review is now routine in the planning process for many public buildings and campuses. is the managerial control that acts before the budget for hardware is spent, because it works on the layout rather than on the doors. Four principles recur in practice.

  • Natural surveillance. Arrange entrances, windows, parking and planting so that legitimate occupants can see the approaches without trying. A reception desk facing the door and a car park visible from occupied offices do work that a camera nobody watches does not.
  • Natural access control. Use the layout to make the intended route obvious and every other route awkward, a single defined visitor entrance, paths and planting that channel movement, service yards that are not a shortcut to anywhere.
  • Territorial reinforcement. Make the boundary between public and private legible, with changes of surface, low walls, signage and thresholds, so that a person standing in the wrong place is visibly in the wrong place and both they and any observer know it.
  • Maintenance. A site that is visibly cared for signals attention. Broken lighting, unrepaired fencing and graffiti signal the opposite, and the signal is read by the people the other three principles are meant to deter.

CPTED is worth its own heading because it is the cheapest mitigation available at the design stage and among the most expensive afterwards. Moving a reception desk to face the entrance costs very little on a drawing and a great deal once the building is occupied.

Choosing Among Mitigations

Topic 2.2 ended with a documented finding. This is the decision that follows it, and it is a comparison rather than a search for the best control in the abstract. Five questions discipline it.

  1. What exactly does this control remove? Name the vector it defeats, not the reassurance it provides. A deadlatch strike removes shimming and nothing else. An interlocked vestibule removes tailgating and nothing else. A control that cannot be described this precisely has not been understood.
  2. What survives it? Every mitigation leaves a residual, and the residual is where the next incident comes from. Migrating to cryptographic badges leaves tailgating exactly where it was.
  3. Does it change likelihood or impact? A vestibule reduces ARO; full-disk encryption on the laptops in the room leaves ARO untouched and collapses the consequence of a theft to the price of hardware. Both are legitimate, and a portfolio built entirely from the first kind fails badly on the day prevention fails.
  4. What does it cost across its life, including the part that is not on the invoice: monitoring, maintenance, the guard hours, the recertification effort, and the throughput cost at a busy entrance. A vestibule that makes the morning queue intolerable will be propped open by March, at which point its effective cost is its purchase price and its effective benefit is zero.
  5. Is it independent of what is already there? Two controls that fail for the same reason, both defeated by an unmonitored alarm, both bypassed by the same propped door, are one control bought twice.

The order in which the answers are applied matters. Eliminate the vector where it is cheap to eliminate (fit the deadlatch, move the skip inside the fence, remove the shortcut through the service yard). Reduce likelihood where elimination is impractical. Reduce impact where likelihood cannot be reduced further. Accept, explicitly and on the record, whatever remains, and set a date to look at it again. A control chosen without step five is the reason organisations own three detective controls on the same door and none on the loading dock.

Zoning and Layered Access

Mature access-control deployments do not treat a facility as a single security boundary; they divide it into concentric security zonesSecurity ZoningDividing a facility into concentric areas of increasing sensitivity, each requiring progressively stronger authorization to enter, a public lobby, a general employee area, a restricted server room, and a highly restricted data center cage, for example. Mirrors network segmentation, where a DMZ, an internal LAN, and a highly restricted database subnet require progressively stronger credentials to reach. A direct physical parallel to network security zoning and micro-segmentation strategies described in NIST SP 800-207 (Zero Trust Architecture)., a public lobby, a general office area, a restricted server room, and perhaps a highly restricted cage within the server room itself, each requiring progressively stronger credentials. This mirrors network segmentation almost exactly: just as a compromised guest Wi-Fi network should never have a direct path to a production database, a badge that opens the front lobby should not, by default, also open the data center.

II. Access Control Panels and Biometric Error Rates

This section examines the technical mechanisms underlying modern electronic access control, biometric matching, and centralized authorization systems.

The Access Control Panel and Authorization Flow

A modern electronic access-control deployment consists of readers (badge, keypad, or biometric sensors) wired to a local controller, which communicates with a centralized access control serverAccess Control Server / Physical Access Control System (PACS)The centralized software system that stores the authoritative database of which credentials are authorized for which doors, at which times, and logs every access event. Functions as the physical-world equivalent of an identity and access management (IAM) system. Enterprise PACS platforms (e.g., Lenel, Genetec, Software House) typically integrate with HR systems for automatic provisioning/deprovisioning tied to employment status. that holds the authoritative authorization database. When a credential is presented, the reader transmits the identifier to the local controller, which either makes an offline decision using a cached authorization list (for resilience during network outages) or queries the central server in real time. The authorization decision considers not just whether the credential is valid, but whether it is valid for that specific door and at that specific time, a badge might be authorized for the main lobby 24/7 but authorized for the server room only during business hours on weekdays.

Access Control Authorization Rule (Config Snippet) 
# PACS authorization rule -- door: SRVROOM-03, credential group: NETADMIN
[access_rule]
door_id        = SRVROOM-03
credential_grp = NETADMIN
schedule       = MON-FRI 07:00-19:00
factor_required = BADGE+PIN
antipassback   = TRUE
alarm_on_forced_open = TRUE
alarm_on_held_open_sec = 30

# Antipassback: rejects a second "in" scan for a badge that has not
# yet produced a matching "out" scan -- prevents a badge from being
# physically passed back out through a window to a second person
# waiting to tailgate into the same zone.

The antipassback parameter above enforces a subtle but important rule: a credential must alternate between "in" and "out" reads and cannot register two consecutive "in" events. This directly defends against a scenario where an authorized employee badges in, then passes their badge back through a window or gap to an accomplice waiting outside, who then attempts to badge in using the same credential, the system rejects the second "in" attempt because no corresponding "out" event occurred first.

Biometric Matching: False Acceptance and False Rejection

Biometric systems do not perform exact matching; they compute a similarity score between a live scan and an enrolled template, then apply a threshold to decide "match" or "no match." This threshold creates an unavoidable tradeoff between two error types: the False Acceptance Rate (FAR)False Acceptance Rate (FAR)The probability that a biometric system incorrectly matches an unauthorized individual's scan to someone else's enrolled template, granting access to the wrong person. Lower FAR means higher security but typically increases the False Rejection Rate. High-security deployments (e.g., data center biometric access) typically require FAR below 0.001% (1 in 100,000). NIST SP 800-76 defines testing methodologies for FAR/FRR evaluation., the probability the system incorrectly accepts an unauthorized individual, and the False Rejection Rate (FRR)False Rejection Rate (FRR)The probability that a biometric system incorrectly rejects a legitimate, enrolled individual's scan, denying them access despite being authorized. Lowering FAR (for tighter security) generally raises FRR (more false rejections), creating a security/usability tradeoff every biometric deployment must tune. The point where FAR and FRR curves intersect is called the Equal Error Rate (EER), commonly used as a single-number comparison metric between biometric systems., the probability the system incorrectly rejects a legitimate, enrolled individual. Tightening the match threshold to reduce FAR (fewer unauthorized people let in) inevitably increases FRR (more legitimate employees locked out and forced to re-scan or seek manual override), meaning organizations must explicitly choose a security posture appropriate to the sensitivity of the space being protected.

Biometric Threshold Tuning Log 
# Fingerprint reader threshold sweep -- 10,000 test scans
Threshold=0.60   FAR=0.850%   FRR=0.020%   (too permissive -- security risk)
Threshold=0.75   FAR=0.090%   FRR=0.310%   (balanced)
Threshold=0.90   FAR=0.001%   FRR=2.400%   (high security, data center standard)
Threshold=0.98   FAR=0.000%   FRR=9.100%   (unusable -- excessive lockouts)

# Data center cage deployment selected: Threshold=0.90
# Rationale: FAR of 0.001% (1-in-100,000) acceptable given asset
# sensitivity; FRR of 2.4% mitigated via badge+PIN fallback path.
A compromised fingerprint cannot be reissued A stolen badge or compromised PIN can be deactivated and reissued instantly. A compromised biometric template cannot be "changed" the way a password can, you cannot issue yourself a new fingerprint. This is why biometric template data at rest must be encrypted and, wherever possible, matched using irreversible transformed templates rather than raw scan storage, and why biometrics are recommended as one factor within a multi-factor scheme rather than a sole authenticator for high-value assets.

III. Case Studies: Data Center Access Models and Lifted Fingerprints

Case Study 2.3.1: Data Center Physical Security Standards Post-Y2K Colocation Boom

As enterprise reliance on third-party colocation data centersColocation Data CenterA facility where multiple, often unrelated organizations rent physical space (cages, cabinets, or rack units) to house their own servers, while the facility operator provides power, cooling, network connectivity, and physical security. Because multiple tenants' equipment shares the same building, robust physical access segmentation between tenants is essential. Industry certification frameworks such as the Uptime Institute's Tier Standard and SSAE 18 SOC 2 audits formally assess colocation physical security posture. grew through the 2000s and 2010s, the industry converged on a now-standard layered physical access model that remains the reference architecture taught in this chapter: a publicly accessible lobby with a staffed reception desk; a badge-and-PIN-controlled general facility floor; a mantrap at the entrance to the server hall itself, often combined with biometric verification; and individually locked, tenant-specific cages or cabinets within the server hall requiring a separate credential from the facility-wide badge. Independent audits under frameworks such as SSAE 18 SOC 2 Type II formally verify that these layers function as designed, including testing whether visitor escort policies and antipassback rules are actually enforced in practice rather than merely documented on paper. This layered model, publicly cited by major colocation providers in their published security documentation, is the direct real-world implementation of the security-zoning concept described in Section I, and demonstrates that "physical access control" in a professional context is rarely a single lock, but an audited, multi-layered system with independent verification at each tier.

An auditor tests the practice, not the document A SOC 2 physical security audit does not simply check whether a visitor-log policy document exists. Auditors typically request sample visitor logs, cross-reference badge system export data against those logs, and may conduct on-site walkthroughs attempting to test whether staff actually challenge unbadged individuals. This distinction, documented policy versus demonstrated enforcement, is a recurring theme across every compliance framework covered in this course.

Case Study 2.3.2: Lifted Prints and the Limits of a Biometric Factor

The best-documented demonstration of the problem is not a facility breach but a laboratory one, and it is more useful for being reproducible. In September 2013, within days of the release of Apple’s Touch ID sensor, members of the Chaos Computer Club published a method for defeating it: make a latent fingerprint left on a glass surface visible with graphite powder or cyanoacrylate fuming, photograph it at 2,400 ppi, laser-print the inverted image onto an overhead transparency so that the heavy toner forms a relief, and cast a thin film of latex or wood glue over that relief. The film is worn over a finger and presented to the sensor. Nothing about the technique requires the target’s cooperation or even their knowledge, because the raw material, the print, is left on every surface they touch. Later work by the same community reported comparable results against other consumer sensors, and vendors responded with liveness detection rather than with better matching.

Two conclusions follow, and only the second is about biometrics specifically. The first is that a fingerprint is a public identifier rather than a secret: it is distributed by its owner continuously and cannot be recalled. That is what makes the enrolment irrevocable in the way the callout below describes, and it is why a biometric is a reasonable claim of identity and a poor sole proof of authorisation. The second is the design conclusion. A sensor tuned toward convenience, a low false-rejection rate, so that staff are not turned away at a busy door, necessarily accepts a higher false-acceptance rate, and the two cannot be improved together except by improving the sensor itself. The remedy that survives contact with a real building is not a stricter threshold, which simply relocates the complaint from security to operations, but the second factor: a biometric that confirms the presenter of a credential the reader has independently validated, backed by liveness detection where the threat model warrants the cost. A facility that treats a fingerprint as sufficient on its own has built single-factor authentication out of a factor its users leave on the door handle.

Where the enforcement actually lives Managerial, used here interchangeably with administrative, and technical controls are separated by where the enforcement actually lives. A visitor log, an escort policy, and a background check required before a badge is issued are managerial controls: their enforcement is a person following a documented procedure, and their characteristic failure is that the procedure is skipped under pressure. A badge reader, a biometric sensor, and the interlock in a vestibule are technical and physical: their enforcement is mechanism, and their characteristic failure is that the mechanism is bypassed rather than disobeyed. The classification is worth making because it tells you what to test. You audit a managerial control by sampling whether it was followed; you test a technical control by attempting to defeat it.

IV. Running an Access Program: Lifecycle, Enforcement, and Audit

A mature physical access control program combines managerial policy, technical enforcement, and independent verification into a single governed lifecycle.

1. Credential Lifecycle Management

Every badge or biometric enrollment should be tied to a formal provisioning and deprovisioningProvisioning / DeprovisioningThe process of granting (provisioning) or revoking (deprovisioning) a user's access rights, ideally triggered automatically by an authoritative event such as HR onboarding or termination. Delayed deprovisioning, a former employee's badge remaining active for weeks after departure, is one of the most common physical access control failures found in audits. NIST SP 800-53 control AC-2 requires timely account/access management, formally including physical credentials under most control interpretations. process integrated with HR onboarding and offboarding systems, so that a terminated employee's badge is deactivated automatically and immediately rather than relying on a manual, easily-forgotten step. Access rights should be reviewed on a recurring schedule (commonly quarterly) to identify and revoke access that is no longer justified by an employee's current role, a direct application of the least-privilege principle from Section I.

2. Layered Technical Enforcement

Consistent with the zoning model, high-sensitivity areas should require multi-factor physical authentication (badge plus PIN or biometric), mantraps or interlocked vestibules at the transition into restricted zones, and antipassback logic to prevent credential-sharing exploits. Facilities should migrate away from cryptographically weak 125 kHz credentials toward ISO/IEC 14443-compliant smart cards, as discussed in Topic 2.2, closing the cloning vulnerability at its technical root rather than relying solely on procedural mitigation.

3. Independent Verification and Audit

Policy without enforcement testing is unreliable, as illustrated by both case studies above. Organizations should undergo recurring third-party physical security audits (SOC 2 Type II, ISO/IEC 27001 surveillance audits) that explicitly test enforcement, attempting tailgating, verifying visitor log accuracy against badge export data, and confirming deprovisioning timeliness, rather than accepting a written policy document as sufficient evidence of control effectiveness.

Layers chosen for independent failure modes A scheme for a sensitive facility is worth checking against all three categories, because each fails in a different way and a set drawn entirely from one category fails all at once. Technical controls, badge readers, biometric sensors, antipassback logic, fail by being bypassed. Administrative controls, visitor logs, escort policy, background checks, periodic access review, fail by being skipped under pressure. Physical controls, fencing, lighting, locked cages, staffed posts, fail by being forced or worn down. Labelling each proposed control on both axes is a check on whether it has been understood: a control whose category and function you cannot state is one whose failure mode you cannot predict, and therefore one you cannot pair sensibly with anything else.

Self-Check Review Questions

Question 1: Explain the antipassback mechanism in an access control panel and describe the specific physical attack it is designed to prevent.

Antipassback is a rule enforced by the access-control server requiring that a given credential's badge reads strictly alternate between "in" and "out" events; the system will reject a second consecutive "in" scan from the same badge if no corresponding "out" scan has occurred first. This mechanism directly prevents a scenario in which an authorized employee badges into a restricted area and then passes their physical badge back out, for example, through a window, gap, or to a waiting accomplice near the door, so that a second, unauthorized individual can use the same credential to badge into the same zone. Without antipassback, the access-control system would treat both entries as fully legitimate presentations of a valid credential, generating no anomaly. With antipassback enforced, the second "in" attempt is rejected because the system's internal state shows the credential as already "inside," directly closing this credential-sharing loophole.

Question 2: A data center biometric fingerprint reader is tuned to a match threshold of 0.90, producing FAR = 0.001% and FRR = 2.4%. Explain the tradeoff this threshold represents and why the facility should not rely on the biometric sensor as a sole authenticator.

False Acceptance Rate (FAR) and False Rejection Rate (FRR) exist in tension: raising the match threshold makes the system more selective, reducing the probability that an unauthorized individual's scan incorrectly matches an enrolled template (lower FAR), but simultaneously increasing the probability that a legitimate, enrolled individual's own scan fails to match closely enough to be accepted (higher FRR). At threshold 0.90, the facility has prioritized security (a 1-in-100,000 chance of incorrectly accepting an unauthorized individual) at the cost of usability (a 2.4% chance any given legitimate employee is incorrectly rejected and must re-scan or use a fallback method). The facility should not rely on the biometric sensor alone because, unlike a badge or PIN, a compromised biometric template cannot be revoked and reissued, an individual cannot generate a new fingerprint the way they can be issued a new badge. Biometric sensors, particularly lower-cost ones without liveness detection, can also be spoofed using lifted prints or synthetic replicas. Pairing the biometric factor with a badge or PIN as part of a multi-factor scheme ensures that a single point of failure in either factor (a spoofed print or a lost badge) is insufficient on its own to grant unauthorized access.

Question 3: Distinguish between managerial (administrative) and technical physical access controls, and classify the following: (a) a quarterly access rights review, (b) a mantrap at the server room entrance, (c) a mandatory visitor escort policy.

Managerial (administrative) controls are enforced through documented policy, procedure, and human process rather than a technological or physical mechanism. Technical controls are enforced through electronic or cryptographic mechanisms, readers, panels, and authentication protocols. Physical controls are enforced through tangible barriers and mechanisms that directly obstruct movement. (a) A quarterly access rights review is a managerial/administrative, detective control: it is carried out through a documented human review process rather than an automated technical enforcement mechanism, and it identifies (detects) excessive or stale access grants after the fact rather than preventing their initial provisioning. (b) A mantrap at the server room entrance is a physical, preventive control: it is a tangible interlocked structure that directly and mechanically prevents more than one person from passing through on a single valid credential presentation, stopping the tailgating attempt before it succeeds rather than merely detecting it afterward. (c) A mandatory visitor escort policy is a managerial/administrative control functioning as both preventive and detective: it is enforced through documented procedure and staff training (not a technical mechanism), and it works preventively by ensuring visitors never move unsupervised through restricted areas, while also functioning detectively in that a violation of the policy would be identified through audit or observation.

Practice Questions

Question 1 2.3.A, A site has a written physical security policy that specifies the model of badge reader to be fitted on every door. What is the drafting problem with this?
  1. None; naming the hardware makes the policy unambiguous and therefore enforceable.
  2. A policy states position and delegates authority and should outlive procurement; hardware selection belongs in a standard, so a policy that names a product is obsolete the next time the product is replaced.
  3. Policies may not mention technology of any kind, only people.
  4. The problem is legal rather than structural: naming a vendor in an internal document creates a contractual obligation.
Question 2 2.3.A, An access recertification is run quarterly. Each space owner receives a list of everyone holding access to their area and confirms it with a single “approve all” button, which most owners press within a minute of receiving the mail. What should an assessor conclude?
  1. The control is effective, since the recertification is performed on schedule and the evidence is retained.
  2. The control exists on paper only: the review produces an approval record without any per-name decision behind it, so stale access accumulates exactly as it would with no review at all.
  3. The control is a technical rather than a managerial one, so it should not appear in a physical security assessment.
  4. The frequency is the defect; the same process run monthly would be sound.
Question 3 2.3.A, Which arrangement best expresses separation of duties in credential management?
  1. The security manager approves the request, issues the badge, and reviews the audit log, so that one accountable person owns the whole process.
  2. The requesting employee approves their own access, provided their manager is informed afterwards.
  3. The space owner approves the request, a separate administrator provisions the credential, and a third party audits the resulting entitlements; where headcount prevents this, an independent periodic review is recorded as a compensating arrangement.
  4. Badges are issued by whoever is on the front desk, with approval reconstructed later from the visitor log.
Question 4 2.3.B, A documented finding records that a wiring-closet door with a plain spring latch was opened with a shim in ten seconds. Management proposes replacing every 125 kHz badge in the building with a cryptographic smart card. Evaluate the proposal against the finding.
  1. It is well matched: a stronger credential raises the difficulty of every physical attack proportionally.
  2. It is mismatched: the vector bypasses the reader entirely rather than defeating the credential, so the migration leaves this finding’s residual risk unchanged while consuming the budget a deadlatch strike would have needed.
  3. It is mismatched only on cost; technically the smart card does close the shimming vector.
  4. It is sound because the smart card generates a richer access log, which detects shimming after the fact.
Question 5 2.3.B, A busy main entrance is fitted with an interlocked vestibule that admits one person per credential. Within three months staff routinely prop the inner door during the morning peak. Which analysis of the mitigation choice is soundest?
  1. The staff are at fault; the remedy is a disciplinary policy and further awareness training.
  2. The lifetime cost of the control was underestimated: throughput at a busy entrance is part of what a control costs, and a control whose operational burden guarantees it will be defeated delivers its purchase price and none of its benefit.
  3. The vestibule was the wrong category of control; only administrative controls work at high-traffic entrances.
  4. The control is working as designed, since propping is itself detected by the interlock.
Question 6 2.3.B, Laptops in a monitored office are already covered by badge access and a door contact. The team adds full-disk encryption. In terms of the two risk variables, what has the addition changed?
  1. It reduces the annualised rate of occurrence, because encrypted machines are less attractive to thieves.
  2. It leaves likelihood untouched and reduces impact, collapsing the consequence of a theft from a confidentiality breach to the replacement cost of hardware, which is why a portfolio built only from likelihood-reducing controls fails badly the day prevention fails.
  3. It reduces both likelihood and impact, and therefore makes the door contact redundant.
  4. It changes neither, since encryption is a technical control and the finding is physical.
Free Response 1 2.3.A 2.3.B, A regional office admits contractors through a staffed reception. A contractor engaged for six weeks is given a permanent-style badge on day one, escorted only to the lift, and is still able to enter the building four months after the engagement ended. (a) Name three managerial controls that failed here and state, for each, what it was supposed to achieve. (b) Propose a mitigation for each failure, and for each say precisely what it removes and what survives it. (c) Explain why at least one of your three mitigations must be joined to an authoritative record such as the HR or vendor-management system rather than to a person’s diary.
Free Response 2 2.3.B, A new office is being fitted out. The budget allows two of the following four: an interlocked vestibule at the main entrance; deadlatch strikes and door-position contacts on all twelve internal doors; a CPTED review at the drawing stage that would relocate reception to face the entrance and move the service yard off the through-route; a biometric reader on the equipment room. (a) Choose two and justify the choice using the five questions from Section I, what each removes, what survives it, whether it acts on likelihood or impact, its lifetime cost, and its independence from the other controls present. (b) State explicitly which risks you are accepting by declining the other two, and set a review point. (c) Explain why the ranking would change if the building were already occupied.

Answer Key

1. B2.3.A

Policy, standard and procedure sit at different altitudes and change at different rates: the policy states the organisation’s position and who holds the authority, the standard specifies what must be fitted, and the procedure says how it is done on a given Tuesday. Pinning a product into the top layer forces a governance change every procurement cycle. A mistakes specificity for enforceability, the standard already supplies the specificity, and it can be revised without reopening the policy. C overcorrects; policies routinely set technical requirements in outcome terms. D invents a legal consequence that does not follow from an internal document.

2. B2.3.A

A recertification is a control only if it forces a per-name decision; a single blanket approval produces the artefact of review with none of its substance, and access continues to accumulate through transfers and expired engagements. The assessor’s finding is about the absence of a decision, not the absence of a record. A accepts evidence of activity as evidence of effectiveness, the precise substitution the chapter warns against. C misclassifies: the enforcement here is a human following a documented procedure, which is what makes it managerial. D changes the frequency of a process that does nothing, which multiplies the effort without adding a decision.

3. C2.3.A

The point of separating approval, provisioning and audit is that no single person can both grant access and conceal having done so; where headcount makes the split impossible, naming the independent review as a compensating arrangement keeps the gap visible to the next assessor rather than papering over it. A concentrates all three roles in one person, which is the condition separation of duties exists to prevent, and accountability is not a substitute for it. B is self-approval. D has no approval step at all; a log reconstructed afterwards records what happened, not what was authorised.

4. B2.3.B

The first of the five questions is what the control actually removes. A cryptographic credential removes duplication of the credential; the shim never touches the credential, the reader, or the panel, so the residual on this finding is exactly what it was before the money was spent. The matched mitigation is a deadlatch plunger, at a fraction of the cost. A assumes physical attacks scale with credential strength, which they do not, they have independent mechanisms. C concedes the technical claim that is false. D invents a detection benefit: a bypass that presents no credential generates no access-control event, whatever card technology is in use.

5. B2.3.B

Throughput is part of the lifetime cost of an entrance control, alongside monitoring and maintenance, and a control that makes the morning queue intolerable will be defeated by the people it protects. The design error was made when the cost was assessed as a purchase price. A treats a predictable operational response as an individual failing, and awareness training does not add capacity to a doorway. C overgeneralises: vestibules work well at high-traffic entrances when sized for the peak or paired with a second lane. D misreads the mechanism, a propped door defeats the interlock rather than reporting it, unless a separate door-position contact has been fitted.

6. B2.3.B

Encryption does not make a theft less likely; it changes what the theft costs, converting a confidentiality breach into a hardware replacement. That is the third of the five questions, and the reason a control portfolio needs both kinds. A attributes to an opportunistic thief a knowledge of the disk configuration they do not have at the moment of the theft. C both overstates the effect and draws the wrong conclusion: the door contact addresses a different vector on a different variable and is not made redundant by an impact control. D treats the control categories as separate problem domains, when the whole point of the classification is that controls of different categories act on the same risk.

FR 12.3.A · 2.3.B

Model answer. (a) Three failures. Credential issuance failed: the badge was neither visually distinct nor time-limited, so nothing about it signalled a contractor or expired on its own. Escort and zoning failed: escorting to the lift satisfies the letter of an escort policy while abandoning its purpose, which is that a person authorised to be in the building is not thereby authorised to be anywhere in it unaccompanied. Revocation failed: the engagement ended and the entitlement did not, which is the step that fails most often because it depends on somebody remembering a date after the work is finished. (b) Mitigations. Issue contractors a distinctly coloured badge with an expiry set to the engagement end date at the moment of issue, this removes indefinite validity and the ambiguity of appearance; what survives is everything inside the validity window, and tailgating, which no credential addresses. Make escort an assigned duty with a named host recorded against the visit and zone entitlements that do not include restricted areas, this removes unsupervised movement; what survives is a host who is complicit or careless, which is why the entitlement limit is set in the system rather than left to the host. Join deprovisioning to the vendor-management or HR record so the entitlement expires with the engagement, and reconcile the badge population against that record on a schedule, this removes accumulation; what survives is the interval between the end date and the reconciliation, which sets how long a stale credential can live. (c) A revocation tied to an individual’s memory or diary fails silently and at the worst moment, when the engagement has ended and the relationship is over. An authoritative record already changes state on the event that matters, so tying revocation to it makes the control fire without anyone deciding to fire it, and makes its failure auditable as a discrepancy between two records rather than invisible.
Look for: three distinct failures rather than three restatements of “the badge stayed active”; each mitigation paired with an explicit residual; and the part (c) argument grounded in silent failure and auditability rather than in general praise for automation.

FR 22.3.B

Model answer. (a) The strongest pairing is the CPTED review plus the deadlatch strikes and door-position contacts, and the argument runs through the five questions. The CPTED review costs almost nothing at the drawing stage and a great deal afterwards; it removes the opportunity created by an unsupervised approach and a service yard used as a through-route, acts on likelihood across every internal vector at once, and is independent of every piece of hardware in the building. The strikes and contacts remove shimming outright and report propping and forced entry on twelve doors rather than one; they act on likelihood for the shim vector and add detection where none exists, their lifetime cost is small and their maintenance minimal, and they fail independently of reception. The vestibule addresses one vector at one door, carries a real throughput cost, and is the control most likely to be defeated by its own users. The biometric reader improves the credential at a door whose likeliest compromise is the latch rather than the credential, and would be reasonable only after the doors themselves are sound. (b) Accepted risks, stated explicitly: tailgating at the main entrance is not addressed by hardware and is being carried on reception procedure and a challenge policy, and equipment-room entry remains single-factor. Both are recorded with an owner and reviewed at the twelve-month mark or on any change of tenancy or headcount. (c) In an occupied building the ranking inverts at the top: the CPTED changes become construction work rather than a drawing revision, so their cost rises by an order of magnitude while their benefit is unchanged, and the vestibule or the door hardware becomes the better purchase.
Look for: a choice defended by what each control removes and leaves rather than by which sounds strongest; likelihood and impact distinguished; lifetime cost including throughput and monitoring; an independence argument; acceptance stated on the record with an owner and a date; and part (c) turning on the cost of the intervention rather than on its effectiveness.
