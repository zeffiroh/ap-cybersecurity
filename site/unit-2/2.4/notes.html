Learning Objectives

  • Explain the sensing mechanism of each major detector class, passive infrared, microwave, dual-technology, acoustic glass-break, vibration and seismic, magnetic contacts, and beam, and state the specific failure mode each one carries.

I. Detection, Assessment, and Response

Topics 2.2 and 2.3 examined how attackers defeat physical barriers and how organizations authenticate and authorize entry through those barriers. This chapter addresses the layer that assumes both of those controls will, someday, fail: physical intrusion detectionPhysical Intrusion DetectionThe set of sensors, monitoring infrastructure, and alerting logic that identifies and reports unauthorized physical entry or movement, independent of whether access control was bypassed, defeated, or never present at that location. Functions as the physical-world equivalent of a network intrusion detection system (IDS). NIST SP 800-53 control family PE (Physical and Environmental Protection), specifically PE-6 (Monitoring Physical Access), formally requires this capability. exists precisely because no preventive control is perfect. A lock can be picked, a badge can be cloned, a mantrap can be defeated by an especially determined tailgater, detection is the layer that assumes failure is inevitable somewhere on a large enough perimeter and asks a different question: if prevention fails, how quickly do we find out, and where?

Detective vs. Preventive Controls, Revisited

Recall the control taxonomy introduced in Topic 2.1: controls are classified simultaneously by category (technical, physical, administrative) and by function (preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, compensating). A locked door and a mantrap are preventive, they stop an event before it happens. A motion sensor is detectiveDetective ControlA control designed to identify and record that a security event has already occurred, rather than to stop it from occurring in the first place. Detective controls do not reduce the probability of an initial breach; they reduce the time between the breach and its discovery, which in turn limits the attacker's dwell time and the resulting damage. Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) is the standard metric used to evaluate the effectiveness of a detective control program, physical or digital. by design, it does not stop an intruder from entering a room, but it ensures that entry does not go unnoticed. This is not a lesser or backup role: in a defense-in-depth model, detection is what limits an attacker's dwell timeDwell TimeThe duration between an attacker's initial unauthorized access and the moment that access is detected and remediated. A shorter dwell time directly limits how much damage, theft, or lateral movement an intruder can accomplish before being interrupted. Physical dwell time (minutes, in a well-monitored facility) is typically far shorter than average digital breach dwell time (historically measured in weeks per major industry breach reports), precisely because physical sensing is simpler and faster to correlate than network traffic analysis. once every preventive layer has already failed.

Detection, Assessment, Response: Three Functions, Routinely Confused

Practitioners in this field separate three things that students, and a good many procurement decisions, collapse into one.

  • Detection is a sensor changing state. It reports that something happened at a point in space and time. On its own it carries almost no information about what happened.
  • Assessment is the determination of what that something was. A camera view of the zone, a guard sent to look, or a second sensor of a different type all serve this function. Assessment converts an alarm into either an incident or a dismissal.
  • Response is the action taken once assessment concludes: a dispatch, a lockdown, a call to police, an entry in the log.

The three are joined by a timing relationship that decides whether the whole arrangement is worth anything. If the time from detection through assessment to an interrupting response exceeds the time the adversary needs to reach the asset and leave with it, the system will produce an excellent record of a completed theft. This is why the placement question in Section IV is not a matter of coverage percentages: a sensor on the cabinet detects an adversary who is already at the cabinet, and by then the remaining response margin is whatever it takes to cut a lock. A sensor at the perimeter fence detects the same adversary while several minutes of movement still separate them from the asset, and those minutes are the entire budget from which assessment and response must be paid.

A system that detects without assessing has a second problem, which is that it cannot dismiss anything. Every activation must either be treated as real, which is unaffordable, or dismissed on faith, which is the alarm-fatigue path described in Case Study 2.4.2. Assessment capability is therefore not a refinement added to a detection system once the budget allows; it is the component that makes the detection component usable.

The Unmonitored Perimeter Blind Spot

The single most common failure mode in physical detection deployments is the unmonitored blind spotUnmonitored Perimeter Blind SpotAny segment of a facility's physical boundary, a window, a loading-dock door, a roof access hatch, an unused side entrance, that lacks sensor coverage, camera coverage, or both, typically because it was overlooked during initial design or added later without a corresponding security review. Functionally identical in concept to an unpatched, unmonitored network port: attackers deliberately search out precisely these overlooked seams rather than attacking the well-defended front door. Physical penetration testers (a specialized subset of red-teaming) are frequently engaged specifically to locate blind spots that automated risk assessments miss, since blind-spot discovery generally requires physically walking a perimeter.: a facility with excellent badge readers and biometric mantraps at its main entrance can remain almost entirely undefended at a side window, an HVAC service door, or a rarely used loading dock, simply because sensor coverage was designed around the obvious, high-traffic entry points rather than the entire perimeter. Attackers who reconnaissance a target, a practice covered conceptually in Unit 1's discussion of social engineering and open-source intelligence, actively search for exactly these overlooked seams rather than attempting to defeat a facility's best-defended point of entry.

The perimeter is the whole envelope, not the doors somebody thought of A facility’s real perimeter includes every window, roof hatch, utility penetration, service riser and loading dock, and an adversary needs to find only the one opening that was never fitted with a sensor. The asymmetry is total: the defender must enumerate the entire envelope, the attacker must find one gap in it. This is the physical analogue of a firewall filtering inbound traffic on 80 and 443 while an unmonitored management port listens on the same host, and the remedy is the same in both cases, which is an enumerated inventory of openings rather than an intuition about where people come in.

Layered Sensing: The Detection Equivalent of Defense-in-Depth

Mature detection deployments do not rely on a single sensor type. Instead, they layer complementary sensing technologies so that a method of defeating one sensor type does not defeat the entire detection scheme, precisely the same logic that motivates layered network defenses (firewall, IDS, endpoint detection) rather than a single control. A facility might combine perimeter fence sensors, exterior motion detectors, interior PIR sensors, glass-break/vibration contacts on windows, and door/window contact switches, each covering a different physical mechanism of intrusion and each with a different, largely uncorrelated false-positive profile.

The layers have conventional names, and the names encode what each layer is for. Perimeter protection senses the crossing of the site or building boundary, fence-mounted strain or fibre sensors, buried seismic cable, beam pairs across an approach. It buys time, and it is the only layer that detects an adversary before they are inside anything. Interior or volumetric protection senses movement within a space, motion detectors covering a room or a corridor. It catches an adversary who entered by a route the perimeter layer did not cover, which is the ordinary case rather than the exceptional one. Point or object protection senses interference with a specific thing, a contact on a cabinet door, a vibration sensor on a safe, a tamper switch inside an enclosure. It is the last layer and the most specific, and it is the only one that survives an insider who was authorised to be in the room.

Two placement principles follow, and they are the substance of objective 2.4.B. The first is that detection belongs at the choke point rather than at the asset alone. A choke point is any place the geometry of the building forces an intruder to pass through: the single corridor serving a floor, the stair core, the lift lobby, the one door between the loading bay and the office floor. Sensors there are few, they are cheap, and every route to every asset behind them crosses one. Distributing the same money uniformly across an open floor buys more sensors, more false alarms, and no guarantee that any particular path is covered. The second is that a layer only adds detection if it can fail independently of the layers around it. Three detectors on one power circuit, reporting over one unmonitored line, to one panel with no tamper supervision, are one detector bought three times; and an adversary who cuts that line at the point where it leaves the building has defeated all three at once, which is why line supervision and tamper detection are treated below as part of the sensor rather than as accessories to it.

II. Intrusion Sensors and the Path from Signal to Alert

This section examines the physical sensing mechanisms underlying modern intrusion detection hardware and how their signals are aggregated into an actionable alert.

Passive Infrared (PIR) Motion Sensors

The most common interior motion sensor is the passive infrared (PIR) sensorPassive Infrared (PIR) SensorA motion detector that senses changes in infrared (heat) radiation within its field of view, rather than emitting any signal of its own (hence "passive"). A pyroelectric sensor element generates a small voltage change when the infrared radiation pattern across its surface changes abruptly, as happens when a warm human body moves across a room at ambient (cooler) temperature. PIR sensors are the dominant motion-detection technology in both commercial and residential security systems due to their low cost, low power consumption, and simplicity compared to active sensing alternatives., which detects motion by measuring changes in infrared radiation across a Fresnel-lensed field of view, rather than emitting any energy of its own. A human body at approximately 37°C emits infrared radiation distinctly different from an ambient-temperature room; as a person moves through the sensor's field of view, the lens segments the field into multiple zones, and motion causes a rapid change in detected infrared level as the person's heat signature crosses zone boundaries. The sensor's pyroelectric element converts this rapid change into a voltage spike, which the control circuitry interprets as motion.

# Note: pet-immunity filtering intentionally reduces sensitivity to
# small/low body-mass heat sources to avoid false alarms from pets in
# a residential deployment -- inappropriate for a server room zone
# where any detected motion after-hours is inherently suspicious.

Everything a PIR sensor can and cannot do follows from that one sentence: it detects a rate of change in the infrared pattern falling on a small pyroelectric element, and the element only sees a change when the warm body crosses a boundary between lens zones. Four consequences matter in the field.

  • Radial motion is its blind spot. A person walking across the detector’s field of view, tangentially, sweeps through zone after zone and produces a strong, fast signal. A person walking straight at the detector stays inside roughly the same zone the whole way; the incident radiation changes slowly, and the signal may never reach the trip threshold. This is not a manufacturing defect but a property of the geometry, and it dictates mounting: a PIR sensor is aimed across an approach, never down it. A detector mounted above a door looking straight along the corridor an intruder will walk is the single commonest installation error in the discipline.
  • Slow movement defeats it for the same reason, and needs no equipment at all, only patience. So does anything that flattens the thermal contrast: a room at body temperature, or an insulating covering, reduces the differential the element is measuring.
  • Common window glass is opaque to the wavelengths involved, which is usually stated as a limitation and is better read as a placement rule: an interior PIR does not see through a window, so it will not be triggered by a passer-by outside, and equally will not detect anything until the intruder is already inside the glazing.
  • Its false alarms have thermal causes. An HVAC diffuser blowing warm air across the field, sunlight tracking across a floor through the day, a radiant heater, or a rodent close to the lens all produce exactly the signal the sensor was built to notice. Aiming away from diffusers and glazing removes more false alarms than any adjustment of the sensitivity setting.

Active Sensing: Microwave, and Why Dual-Technology Units Use AND Logic

A microwave motion detectorMicrowave (Doppler) Motion DetectorAn active sensor that emits a continuous low-power microwave signal and measures the frequency shift of the reflection returning from a moving object, the Doppler effect. Because it detects motion through a change in reflected frequency rather than a change in heat, it is unaffected by ambient temperature and detects radial motion (toward or away from the unit) particularly well, which is precisely the geometry that defeats a PIR. Its characteristic weakness is the mirror image: microwave energy passes through plasterboard partitions, glazing and doors, so a unit can be triggered by movement in the adjoining room or outside the building entirely. is the complement of a PIR in almost every respect. It is active: it emits energy and measures the Doppler shift of what returns. Motion toward or away from the unit produces the largest frequency shift, so its strongest response is exactly where the PIR is weakest, and vice versa. Its weaknesses are its own: microwave energy penetrates plasterboard, glass and hollow doors, so a unit covering a store room will happily detect a forklift in the neighbouring bay, and it responds to moving metal, fan blades, and hanging signage.

A dual-technology detectorDual-Technology DetectorA single unit housing both a passive infrared and a microwave sensing channel, which signals an alarm only when both channels detect motion within a short common window, a logical AND. Because the two technologies have essentially unrelated false-alarm causes, requiring agreement suppresses the great majority of nuisance activations. The cost is a reduced probability of detection: a real intruder whom one channel misses is not reported at all, so a dual-technology unit is a false-alarm control and not a sensitivity improvement. puts both channels in one housing and alarms only when both agree within a short window. The reasoning is statistical rather than mechanical: the two channels have unrelated false-alarm causes, so the probability that a sunbeam and a forklift coincide within the same two seconds is very much smaller than the probability of either alone. The trade is real and is often glossed over. AND logic multiplies the probabilities of detection as well as of false alarm, so a dual-technology unit is strictly less likely to detect a given intruder than either channel by itself. It is chosen where nuisance alarms are the binding problem, a loading bay with roller doors and sunlight, and it is the wrong choice where the probability of detection is what must be maximised. Where both are needed, the answer is two separate units reporting on two zones with OR logic between them and a camera to assess, not one unit with the channels ANDed together.

Glass-Break and Vibration Contacts

Windows represent one of the most common unmonitored blind spots described in Section I, and are protected by two complementary sensor types. A glass-break acoustic sensorGlass-Break Acoustic SensorA microphone-based sensor tuned to recognize the specific acoustic frequency signature of breaking glass, typically a sharp high-frequency component (the initial shatter) followed by a lower-frequency component (glass fragments hitting the floor). Distinguished from ordinary background noise using frequency-domain signal analysis rather than simple volume thresholding, reducing false alarms from unrelated loud sounds. Acoustic glass-break sensors can typically cover an entire room from a single ceiling-mounted unit, unlike contact-based sensors which must be affixed to each individual pane. listens for this specific frequency signature rather than triggering on volume alone, distinguishing an actual break from a dropped object or a loud voice. A complementary vibration contact sensorVibration (Shock) Contact SensorA sensor physically affixed to a window pane or frame that detects the mechanical shock and vibration signature of an impact or cutting attempt, functioning independently of the acoustic sensor's microphone-based detection. Because it senses physical vibration transmitted through the glass or frame itself, it is not defeated by soundproofing or by cutting glass silently with a glass cutter (which produces vibration even without producing the acoustic "break" signature an acoustic sensor is tuned for). Placing both sensor types on the same high-value window is a direct application of layered, complementary sensing: an attack that defeats one sensing modality (silent cutting defeating acoustic detection) is still caught by the other (vibration detection)., physically affixed to the pane or frame, senses the mechanical shock of an impact or cutting attempt independent of the sound it produces, directly compensating for an attacker attempting to circumvent the acoustic sensor with quiet glass-cutting tools rather than a loud, obvious break.

The acoustic signature is worth stating precisely, because it explains both what the sensor catches and what it misses. Breaking a pane is a two-stage event. The pane first flexes and fails, radiating a low-frequency thump on the order of a few hundred hertz; the fracture and the fragments then produce a burst of high-frequency energy extending well into the kilohertz range. A modern sensor requires the pattern, the low-frequency flex followed within a short window by the high-frequency shatter, rather than either component alone, which is why a slammed door (low frequency, no shatter) and a dropped set of keys (high frequency, no flex) do not trip it. Three limits follow. The sensor is tuned to a glass type, and plate, tempered, laminated and wired glass fail with different signatures, so a unit calibrated for one may respond poorly to another. It needs an acoustic path, so it must be mounted with a line of sight to the glass and is degraded by heavy curtains or a partition. And an intruder who removes a pane by cutting and suction, or who defeats the frame rather than the glazing, never produces the signature at all, which is the entire reason the vibration contact is fitted alongside it rather than instead of it.

The same physics scales up. A seismic sensorSeismic (Structural Vibration) SensorA sensor fixed to a wall, floor, ceiling, safe, or vault that detects the vibration signature of attack on the structure itself, drilling, hammering, chiselling, thermal lancing, or the use of a core drill. Discriminating circuitry counts events above a threshold within a time window rather than alarming on any single knock, so that ordinary building noise does not trigger it. Used where the threat model includes an adversary who bypasses every opening and comes through the fabric of the building; also deployed as buried cable along a perimeter to detect footfall or digging. is fitted to a vault wall, a safe body, or an ATM enclosure to catch an adversary who attacks the structure rather than any of its openings, and buried seismic cable does the same job along a perimeter, detecting footfall or digging as a pattern of ground disturbance. In both cases the discriminating logic counts qualifying events within a window instead of alarming on a single knock, which is what makes the sensor survivable in a building where people also close doors.

Magnetic Contacts, and the Difference Between a Reed Switch and a Balanced One

The plain magnetic reed contactMagnetic Reed Contact SwitchTwo components, a magnet on the moving leaf and a sealed reed switch on the fixed frame, forming a circuit that is held closed only while the two are adjacent. Opening the door separates them and breaks the circuit. Cheap, reliable, and the oldest widely deployed intrusion sensor, but it responds to any sufficient magnetic field rather than specifically to its own magnet, which is the basis of the defeat described alongside. has a defeat that is old, well known, and still effective against a great deal of installed hardware. The switch closes in the presence of a magnetic field; it has no way to distinguish its own magnet from any other. An adversary who places a strong external magnet against the frame beside the switch holds it in its closed, supervisory state, and can then open the door indefinitely without the panel seeing a change. Nothing about the attack requires access to the wiring or to the panel.

The answer is the balanced magnetic switchBalanced Magnetic Switch (BMS)A high-security contact using a biased magnetic arrangement in which the switch is held in balance by its own magnet, so that an additional external field disturbs the balance and signals an alarm rather than holding the switch closed. Designed specifically to resist the external-magnet defeat that works against a plain reed contact. Underwriters Laboratories addresses these devices in UL 634, whose Level 2 classification imposes requirements for resistance to magnetic defeat and to mechanical attempts to hold the mechanism in its supervisory position; U.S. Intelligence Community facility standards under ICD 705 express a preference for Level 2 devices., which is engineered so that the presence of an extra field unbalances the arrangement and produces an alarm rather than suppressing one. Underwriters Laboratories treats these devices under UL 634, Connectors and Switches for Use with Burglar-Alarm Systems, whose Level 2 classification adds requirements for resistance to magnetic defeat and for withstanding attempts to hold the mechanism in its supervisory position by mechanical means; U.S. Intelligence Community facility standards under ICD 705 state a preference for Level 2 devices in accredited spaces. For a general commercial building the practical rule is narrower and cheap to apply: fit a plain reed contact where the contact is one of several sensors on a low-value opening, and specify a balanced switch on any door where the contact is the only thing standing between an adversary and the asset.

Beam and Photoelectric Detection

An active infrared beam pair projects a modulated beam from a transmitter to a receiver and alarms when the beam is interrupted. Its virtue is a very well-defined detection geometry: a line, rather than a volume with fuzzy edges, which makes it the natural sensor for a long boundary, a corridor, or the plane just inside a row of windows. Its limitations are equally geometric. A single beam can be crawled under or stepped over, so real deployments stack two or more beams at different heights and require simultaneous interruption. Fog, snow, heavy rain and accumulated dirt on the lens attenuate the beam, which is why outdoor units modulate the signal and monitor received strength, reporting a fault when the margin degrades rather than waiting to fail as an alarm. And the transmitter and receiver must hold alignment: a post that moves seasonally produces an intermittent fault that is usually diagnosed, wrongly, as a defective unit.

Tamper Detection and Line Supervision

Every sensor above shares a vulnerability that has nothing to do with its sensing principle: the adversary can attack the sensor instead of passing in front of it. Opening the housing, cutting the cable, shorting the pair, masking the lens with paint or a card, or simply removing power all convert a working detector into a quiet one. The countermeasures are treated as part of the device rather than as options.

  • Tamper switches in the housing and at the mounting report the cover being opened or the unit being removed from the wall, and they report on a circuit that is monitored whether or not the zone is armed, a tamper that is only supervised at night invites the adversary to prepare in the afternoon.
  • Line supervision puts an end-of-line resistor at the far end of the loop so that the panel measures a specific resistance rather than merely open-or-closed. A cut reads as infinite, a short reads as zero, and both are distinguishable from the normal value; the adversary can no longer defeat the zone by shorting the pair at the sensor. Higher-security installations use multiple resistance values or a digitally polled bus for the same reason.
  • Anti-masking in the detector itself emits a short-range probe and alarms when something is placed immediately in front of the lens.
  • Power and communications supervision report mains loss, low battery, and failure of the path to the monitoring centre. An alarm system whose only route to the outside world is a single telephone line has one cable that ends the entire arrangement, which is why a supervised secondary path is standard on anything above the lowest grade.

The general principle is that a detection system must detect attacks on itself, and must do so in a way that distinguishes attack from ordinary failure only after it has reported both. A device that fails silently is worse than no device, because the site believes it is covered.

Door/Window Contact Switches and the Detection-to-Alert Pipeline

The simplest and most reliable sensor type is the magnetic reed contact switchMagnetic Reed Contact SwitchA simple sensor consisting of two components, a magnet mounted on the moving part of a door or window, and a reed switch mounted on the fixed frame, that maintains a closed electrical circuit only while the magnet remains in close proximity to the switch. Opening the door or window separates the magnet from the switch, breaking the circuit and triggering an alarm state. Extremely reliable and low-cost, but only detects the door/window opening itself, not motion or presence beyond that single point. Reed switches are the oldest and most widely deployed physical intrusion sensor type, predating PIR and acoustic sensing by decades, and remain standard on essentially every commercial door and window sensor sold today., which detects only the physical opening of a monitored door or window, complementing motion and acoustic sensors that cover the space beyond that single point.

Every sensor type described above ultimately reports its state to a central alarm panelAlarm Panel / Physical Security Information Management (PSIM) SystemThe centralized hardware and software system that aggregates signals from every deployed sensor (PIR, glass-break, contact switches, cameras) across a facility, applies zone-based logic and arming schedules, and generates alerts routed to a monitoring station, security operations center, or local siren. Functions as the physical-world equivalent of a SIEM aggregating logs from many network sensors into a single correlated view. Enterprise-grade PSIM platforms can correlate signals across dozens of buildings and thousands of individual sensors into a single unified operator console., which applies zone-based arming logic (a given zone may be armed only after business hours, for instance) and escalates a triggered sensor into a routed alert, a local siren, a notification to a monitoring station, or a dispatch to an on-site or third-party security operations center (SOC). This aggregation-and-escalation pipeline is directly analogous to a SIEM correlating logs from many independent network sensors into a single actionable security event, a parallel this course will return to explicitly in Topic 4.4.

A triggered sensor is a question, not an answer A single PIR trip could be a genuine intruder, a facilities technician who forgot to disarm the zone, or a false positive from HVAC-driven air currents affecting the sensor. Mature detection programs correlate multiple independent signals, a door contact and a PIR trip in the same zone within a short window, for example, before escalating to an active dispatch, exactly as a SIEM correlates multiple weak signals into a single higher-confidence alert rather than paging an analyst for every individual log line.

Video as an Assessment Instrument

Cameras are usually procured as though they were detectors. They are not; they are the assessment layer, and the questions that decide whether a camera earns its place are all questions about whether a human or an algorithm can tell from the image what is happening.

  • Field of view against pixel density. A wide lens covering a whole yard yields an image in which a person is a smear, and a smear supports the statement “someone was there” and nothing else. Practitioners specify the task first, detect a presence, recognise a known person, identify a stranger, and then work backwards to the pixels-per-metre the task needs across the target’s working range, which fixes the lens and the number of cameras. A camera that covers everything usually resolves nothing, and this is the trade that decides how many cameras a yard actually needs.
  • Light. Sensitivity is quoted in lux, and the figure on a datasheet is measured under conditions the installed camera will rarely meet. The harder problem is dynamic range: a camera facing a glazed entrance at midday sees a silhouette against the glare, and a camera pointed at a car park at night sees headlights and black. Infrared illumination solves darkness at the cost of colour, which matters because a description that cannot include clothing colour is a much weaker piece of evidence.
  • Placement against the path, not against the room. The most useful camera in a building is usually not the one surveying the open floor but the one covering the choke point at head height, angled so that a person passing through must face it, positioned so that it also captures the door state. Assessment needs a view of the thing the sensor is reporting on, which is an argument for pairing each alarm zone with a camera whose view is that zone, so an operator receiving an activation is shown the relevant image without hunting for it.
  • Verification latency. A camera that assesses is one an operator can call up within seconds of the alarm, together with the recorded seconds before it. Pre-alarm buffering is what turns a live view of an empty corridor into an answer, because by the time the operator looks, whatever tripped the sensor has usually moved on.

Why Good Sensors Still Produce Alerts That Are Almost Never Real

The most commonly misread number in this field is the accuracy of a detector, and the misreading is a base-rate error. Consider a zone whose detector is genuinely good: presented with a real intruder it alarms 99 times in 100, and on any given night with no intruder it produces a nuisance alarm 1 per cent of the time. Now suppose an actual intrusion at this zone occurs about once in a thousand nights. Across a thousand nights the detector produces roughly one alarm from the single real event and roughly ten from the 999 quiet nights. The alarm is therefore genuine about one time in eleven, a little under 10 per cent, even though every quoted specification of the sensor is excellent. Nothing has gone wrong with the hardware. The rarity of the event, not the quality of the detector, dominates the answer.

Three practical conclusions follow, and they are the reason this chapter treats assessment as structural rather than optional. First, a monitoring operation cannot be designed on the assumption that alarms are usually real, because for rare events they never will be; it must be designed to dispose of the common case cheaply. Second, the way to raise the proportion of genuine alarms is to add an independent second signal rather than to make one sensor stricter, the correlation logic and the dual-technology AND both work by attacking the ten false alarms rather than the one real one. Third, the operational cost of false alarms is not a nuisance line item; it is the mechanism by which a detection programme dies. Published problem-oriented policing research puts the false rate for police-dispatched burglar alarms at roughly 94 to 98 per cent (Rana Sampson, False Burglar Alarms, 2nd ed., Problem-Oriented Guides for Police No. 5, 2007), and it was on that basis that Salt Lake City moved in 2000 to a verified-response policy under which police attend an alarm only once a private responder or a camera has confirmed a crime. A site that generates nuisance alarms at scale eventually finds that its alarms buy no response at all, which is the same outcome as having no detection, arrived at by a longer road.

Guard Tours, Anti-Passback, and Detection That Is Not a Sensor

Two detective controls sit outside the sensor inventory and are easy to overlook. A guard patrol is a mobile detector with an extremely low duty cycle but no fixed field of view: it sees the propped fire door, the ceiling tile pushed aside, the unfamiliar vehicle, and the cable that has been run somewhere it should not be, none of which any installed sensor reports. Its characteristic failure is that it becomes a fixed route walked at a fixed hour, at which point an adversary need only observe it once, so tours are recorded against checkpoints and randomised in order and timing. The checkpoint record is itself a detective control on the guard.

Anti-passback, introduced in Topic 2.3 as a preventive rule, does double duty here. Because the system maintains a belief about who is inside each zone, it can report the states that belief cannot reconcile: a credential presented for entry that the system already believes is inside, an exit with no matching entry, or a person still shown as present in a zone hours after their shift ended. These are detections produced by an access-control system with no sensor involved at all, and they catch credential sharing and tailgating, which is exactly the class of attack that leaves the door contacts and motion detectors entirely undisturbed.

Retention and the Handling of Recorded Evidence

Detection produces a record, and the record is frequently the only thing that survives the incident. Its usefulness depends on decisions taken long before.

  • Retention has to exceed the discovery interval. A fourteen-day loop is worthless against a theft discovered at the month-end stock count, and choosing the period is a question about how long this organisation typically takes to notice this class of loss, not a question about disk cost. Where personal data protection law applies, the same period is also a ceiling rather than only a floor, and the justification is expected to be written down.
  • Time must be right and common. Correlating a door contact against a badge event against a camera frame is impossible if the three systems disagree about the clock, so all of them synchronise to a common time source and the offsets are checked, not assumed.
  • Export, then preserve. The first action on a real incident is to place a legal hold and export the relevant footage before the loop overwrites it. More evidence is lost to a recorder quietly continuing to record than to any deliberate act.
  • Handling determines admissibility. An exported file needs a hash recorded at the moment of export, a documented chain of custody naming everyone who held it, and the original left untouched, with all work done on copies. Footage that cannot be shown to be unaltered is footage an opposing party will exclude, and the effort spent on the detection system converts to nothing at that point.

III. Case Studies: the Blind-Spot Loading Dock and False-Alarm Fatigue

Case Study 2.4.1: The Blind-Spot Loading Dock

A recurring pattern documented across numerous independent physical penetration-testing engagements involves loading docks and delivery entrances. These points are frequently exempted from standard perimeter sensor coverage because they are treated administratively as "controlled" through procedural means alone, a receiving log, a scheduled delivery window, rather than through the same sensor-based technical controls applied to the facility's front entrance. Testers repeatedly report successfully entering evaluated facilities through loading docks left propped open during active receiving hours, with no motion sensor, contact switch, or camera covering the opening, precisely the unmonitored-blind-spot failure mode described in Section I. The remediation pattern is consistent across these engagements: extending the same sensor layering standard applied to primary entrances (contact switches, motion coverage of the interior space immediately beyond the dock, and camera coverage) to every point of entry, regardless of whether that point is also governed by an administrative procedure.

A procedure is not coverage, because it fails on one distracted afternoon A receiving-log policy is a legitimate managerial/administrative control, but it only works if the humans following it never make a mistake, propping a door, stepping away momentarily, admitting an unbadged "delivery driver" who is actually a social engineer (recall Topic 1.1). Technical detective controls exist precisely because administrative controls alone are not resilient to a single human error.

Case Study 2.4.2: False-Alarm Fatigue and Reduced Response

A well-documented failure mode in commercial and institutional alarm monitoring is alarm fatigueAlarm Fatigue (Alert Fatigue)A phenomenon in which excessive false or low-value alerts desensitize monitoring personnel, leading them to respond more slowly, dismiss alerts without full investigation, or disable sensors entirely, thereby increasing the practical risk that a genuine event goes unaddressed. Directly analogous to SOC analyst alert fatigue from noisy network intrusion detection systems generating large volumes of low-fidelity alerts. Industry-reported statistics on commercial burglar alarm systems have repeatedly found that the overwhelming majority of dispatched alarm activations are false alarms, a rate high enough that some municipalities have adopted verified-response policies requiring additional confirmation before law enforcement dispatch., in which poorly tuned sensors (a PIR sensor triggered repeatedly by HVAC airflow, or a glass-break sensor mis-tuned to trigger on unrelated loud noises) generate enough false alarms that monitoring staff begin to treat all activations from a given zone with reduced urgency. The pattern is the physical-security analogue of SOC analysts desensitised to a noisy network IDS and missing a genuine alert buried among low-fidelity ones.

What makes the case usable rather than anecdotal is that the effect has been measured at municipal scale. The problem-oriented policing literature places the false rate for police-dispatched burglar alarms at roughly 94 to 98 per cent, and reports that of the thousands of alarm calls Salt Lake City police attended in 1999, around 0.3 per cent were found to arise from an actual crime (Sampson, False Burglar Alarms, 2nd ed., Problem-Oriented Guides for Police No. 5, 2007; see also the COPS Office edition of the same guide). In 2000 Salt Lake City adopted a verified-response policy: police attend an alarm only after a private responder or a visual confirmation establishes that a crime is in progress. The volume of police alarm response collapsed accordingly.

Read carefully, this is a lesson about the economics of assessment rather than about lazy operators. The alarm systems in question were largely functioning as designed; the systems that failed were the ones that could detect but could not assess, and so passed an undifferentiated stream of activations to a responder who had to treat all of them alike. Verified response did not improve any sensor. It relocated the assessment step to somebody who could actually perform it, a camera, or a private responder on site, before the expensive response was committed. The lesson generalises to any monitoring operation, physical or digital: when the base rate of real events is low, the scarce resource is not detection but the cheap disposal of the common case, and a programme that cannot dispose of the common case cheaply will eventually stop being answered.

Tuning is not maintenance; it is the control A facility can hold a complete sensor inventory and still be entered without anyone reacting, and the gap is never in the hardware list. A detective control consists of the sensor, the sensitivity and discrimination settings appropriate to that specific location, an assessment path that lets an operator find out what happened in seconds, and a response protocol that says who does what. Remove any one and the remaining three deliver nothing: an untuned sensor produces alarms nobody believes, an unassessed alarm cannot be dismissed or acted on, and an assessed intrusion with no response protocol produces a well-documented loss. The hardware is the cheapest of the four and the only one that appears on the invoice.

IV. Coverage, Tuning, and Escalation

A mature physical detection program combines complete sensor coverage, disciplined tuning, and a defined escalation and audit process.

1. Comprehensive Perimeter Coverage Review

Every physical opening in a facility's perimeter, doors, windows, roof hatches, utility penetrations, loading docks, should be explicitly enumerated and assigned at least one sensing technology, directly closing the unmonitored blind spot failure mode. This inventory should be revisited whenever the facility's physical structure changes (a new addition, a repurposed space) rather than treated as a one-time exercise, since blind spots are most often introduced by change rather than present from initial construction.

2. Layered, Complementary Sensing per Zone

High-value zones (server rooms, executive offices, evidence rooms) should combine at least two independent sensing modalities, a PIR sensor plus a door contact, or a glass-break sensor plus a vibration contact, so that a technique defeating one sensor type does not defeat detection entirely, mirroring the defense-in-depth logic applied throughout this unit.

3. Tuning and False-Positive Management

Sensor sensitivity should be actively tuned per deployment environment (pet-immunity settings, HVAC airflow considerations, glass-break frequency calibration) and reviewed on a recurring basis using false-alarm rate data, directly addressing the alarm-fatigue failure mode documented in Case Study 2.4.2. A sensor generating chronic false positives should be recalibrated or relocated, not simply tolerated or mentally discounted by monitoring staff.

4. Correlated Escalation Logic

Alarm panels and monitoring platforms should apply correlation logic, requiring multiple independent signals within a short window before escalating to a full dispatch response, reducing the operational burden of isolated sensor noise while still escalating genuinely correlated, high-confidence events immediately.

Detection is what prevention’s failure costs you, measured in minutes Detection does not substitute for the preventive and managerial controls of Topics 2.1 through 2.3; it is what determines the price of their eventual failure. A preventive control decides whether the event happens. A detective control decides how long it runs before somebody arrives, and every minute of that interval is damage the preventive control has already conceded. The two are therefore not alternatives competing for the same budget line but two different variables in the same risk expression, which is why the honest way to argue for detection is not that the locks might fail but that when they do, the loss is bounded by response time and by nothing else.

Self-Check Review Questions

Question 1: Explain why a facility with a strong badge-and-mantrap front entrance can still be compromised via a loading dock, and identify the concept that describes this failure mode.

This is the unmonitored perimeter blind spot failure mode: sensor and access-control investment is often concentrated on a facility's most visible, high-traffic entry point (the front door) while less-visible openings, loading docks, side windows, roof access, are left with weak or no sensor coverage, frequently because they are assumed to be adequately governed by an administrative procedure (a receiving log, a delivery schedule) alone. An attacker who reconnaissances the facility specifically searches for these overlooked seams rather than attempting to defeat the well-defended front entrance, since the loading dock offers an equivalent path into the facility with dramatically less resistance.

Question 2: A server room window is protected by both an acoustic glass-break sensor and a vibration contact sensor. Explain why both are deployed rather than just one.

The two sensors use independent physical detection mechanisms and are therefore defeated by different attack techniques. The acoustic sensor listens for the specific frequency signature of a loud, sudden glass break; an attacker using a slow, quiet glass-cutting technique may avoid triggering it. The vibration contact senses mechanical shock transmitted through the glass or frame directly, and is not dependent on the sound produced, so it still triggers on a quiet cutting attempt. Deploying both sensors is a direct application of layered, complementary sensing: a technique that defeats one sensing modality does not defeat the other, closing the single-point-of-failure gap that either sensor alone would leave.

Question 3: A monitoring center reports that a particular alarm zone generates false alarms so frequently that staff have started assuming every activation from that zone is a false alarm. Identify this phenomenon and explain the security risk it creates.

This is alarm fatigue (alert fatigue): a high volume of low-value or false alerts from a specific source desensitizes monitoring personnel, causing them to respond more slowly or dismiss future activations from that same source without full investigation. The security risk is that a genuine intrusion occurring in that chronically noisy zone is likely to be dismissed along with the routine false alarms, effectively neutralizing the detective control's value even though the sensor itself is functioning and correctly triggering. The correct remediation is to recalibrate or relocate the noisy sensor to reduce its false-positive rate, rather than relying on staff to somehow maintain vigilance despite the noise, directly paralleling how a SOC must tune a noisy network IDS rather than simply expecting analysts to catch a genuine alert buried among thousands of low-fidelity ones.

Practice Questions

Question 1 2.4.A, A passive infrared detector is mounted above a doorway, aimed straight down the corridor that an intruder would walk. Why is this placement poor?
  1. The detector will be saturated by the doorway’s ambient heat and will report a permanent fault.
  2. A person walking directly toward the unit stays within roughly one lens zone, so the incident infrared changes slowly and may never reach the trip threshold; a PIR should be aimed across an approach rather than along it.
  3. Passive infrared detectors cannot be ceiling- or door-mounted and must be fitted at skirting height.
  4. The placement is sound; the limitation is only that the detector cannot see through the closed door.
Question 2 2.4.A, A loading bay suffers frequent nuisance alarms from sunlight and moving vehicles, and a dual-technology PIR/microwave detector is fitted in place of the PIR. What has this changed?
  1. Both the false-alarm rate and the probability of detection have improved, since two sensors see more than one.
  2. Requiring both channels to agree suppresses most nuisance alarms, because their causes are unrelated, but the same AND logic also lowers the probability that a real intruder is reported, so the unit is a false-alarm control rather than a sensitivity improvement.
  3. Nothing of substance; the microwave channel simply duplicates the infrared one.
  4. The detection probability has improved and the false-alarm rate is unchanged, since the microwave channel adds coverage without adding noise.
Question 3 2.4.A, A store-room door carries a plain magnetic reed contact as its only sensor. An adversary opens the door repeatedly over several weeks and the panel never reports it. What is the most likely explanation, and what is the appropriate remedy?
  1. The panel’s arming schedule excluded the zone; the remedy is to arm it around the clock.
  2. An external magnet held against the frame keeps the switch in its supervisory state, because the reed responds to any sufficient field rather than to its own magnet; the remedy is a balanced magnetic switch, whose balance an extra field disturbs.
  3. The reed switch has failed closed through age; the remedy is a maintenance schedule.
  4. The adversary shorted the loop at the sensor; the remedy is a stronger magnet on the door leaf.
Question 4 2.4.B, A site has budget for two interior detectors on a floor whose equipment room is reached only by a single corridor from the stair core. Where should they go, and on what reasoning?
  1. Both inside the equipment room, since that is where the asset of value actually sits.
  2. One in the corridor choke point that every route to the room must cross, with an assessment camera on the same view, and one in the equipment room as point protection, because the choke point detects the intruder while enough time remains for assessment and response, and the room covers the case of an insider already past it.
  3. Spread evenly across the open floor so that no square metre is uncovered.
  4. Both at the building entrance, since detection is only meaningful at the outermost boundary.
Question 5 2.4.B, A zone’s detector alarms on 99 per cent of genuine intrusions and produces a nuisance alarm on 1 per cent of quiet nights. Genuine intrusions at this zone occur about once in a thousand nights. Roughly what share of alarms from this zone are genuine, and what follows?
  1. About 99 per cent, since that is the detector’s accuracy; the monitoring centre should dispatch on every activation.
  2. About 50 per cent, because the two error rates offset one another.
  3. Under 10 per cent, roughly one alarm in eleven, because the rarity of real intrusions dominates; the remedy is an independent second signal and a cheap assessment path, not a stricter threshold on the same sensor.
  4. The question cannot be answered without knowing the sensor’s manufacturer and grade.
Question 6 2.4.C, A theft from a comms cabinet is discovered at the month-end audit. The recorder holds fourteen days. Which single change would most improve the site’s ability to investigate this class of incident?
  1. Increase camera resolution across the site, so that any future footage is sharper.
  2. Set the retention period from the interval in which this organisation actually discovers this class of loss, here at least a full audit cycle, since footage that has already been overwritten cannot be improved by any other measure.
  3. Add a second siren so that a future intrusion is more likely to be interrupted.
  4. Move the recorder into the comms cabinet so that footage and asset are stored together.

Answer Key

1. B2.4.A

A pyroelectric element responds to the rate at which the infrared pattern falling on it changes, and that change is produced by the target crossing boundaries between lens zones. Radial approach keeps the target in roughly one zone, so the signal rises too slowly to trip; tangential crossing sweeps zone after zone and produces a strong one. The correction is aim, not sensitivity. A invents a saturation failure the technology does not exhibit at room temperatures. C is false: PIR units are routinely mounted high, and mounting height is not the defect here. D identifies a true but irrelevant property, the aiming error remains once the door is open, which is exactly when detection is wanted.

2. B2.4.A

AND logic between two channels with unrelated noise sources multiplies the false-alarm probabilities, which is a large reduction; it multiplies the detection probabilities too, which is a reduction the buyer rarely notices. That trade is the right one in a bay full of sunlight and forklifts and the wrong one where probability of detection is what must be maximised, in which case two units on separate zones with OR logic and a camera to assess is the better arrangement. A and D both assert an improvement in detection probability that AND logic cannot produce. C denies the complementarity that motivates the design: microwave is strongest on radial motion, where PIR is weakest.

3. B2.4.A

A reed switch closes in the presence of a sufficient magnetic field and cannot distinguish its own magnet from an introduced one, so an external magnet held at the frame pins it in the supervisory state while the door swings freely. The balanced magnetic switch is engineered so that an additional field unbalances rather than holds the mechanism, and UL 634 Level 2 exists to identify devices tested against exactly this attack. A would produce no record either, but a repeatable weeks-long pattern with no fault and no alarm points to defeat rather than to scheduling, and the remedy proposed does not address the mechanism. C would normally surface as an intermittent fault on a supervised loop. D misattributes the technique and then proposes a change that makes the magnetic defeat easier, not harder.

4. B2.4.B

The choke point is where the building’s geometry does the defender’s work: one sensor covers every route to the asset, and it fires while enough of the intruder’s path remains that assessment and response can still fit inside it. The second detector goes on the asset because point protection covers the case the choke point cannot, someone already inside who was entitled to be. A detects an adversary who has arrived, leaving a margin measured in seconds. C converts a geometric advantage into a coverage exercise, buying more sensors, more nuisance alarms, and no guaranteed path coverage. D abandons the interior entirely and fails against anyone who entered by an uncovered opening, which is the ordinary case.

5. C2.4.B

Over a thousand nights the zone yields about one alarm from the single real event and about ten from the 999 quiet ones, so roughly one alarm in eleven is genuine, a base-rate result, not a defect in the hardware. Tightening the threshold trades away the detection probability on the one event that matters while barely touching the ten; adding an independent second signal, and an assessment path cheap enough to dispose of the common case, attacks the ten instead. A is the base-rate error itself, reading a conditional accuracy as a posterior probability. B averages two numbers that do not combine that way. D treats an arithmetic question as a procurement one; the figures given are sufficient.

6. B2.4.C

Retention is chosen from the organisation’s own discovery interval for the loss type in question. A fourteen-day loop against a month-end audit guarantees that the relevant footage is gone before anyone knows to look for it, and no other improvement recovers it. Where data protection law applies the same period is also a ceiling, and the reasoning is expected to be written down. A improves footage that will not exist. C is a response measure and does nothing for an incident discovered weeks later. D places the evidence inside the target, so the same intrusion removes both.
