2.1 Activities

Security Foundations: CIA Triad · 3

Skill 2.1.a · pairs · 20 min · Offline: yes

CIA triad: confidentiality, integrity, availability

Goal

Students classify real-ish security scenarios by which CIA pillar(s) are violated and propose at least one countermeasure for each. Ties to LO 2.1.A (identify which CIA property a threat targets) and the course skill Analyze Risk.

Materials

Scenario card (print or project)

# Incident description Pillar(s) violated One countermeasure
1 A disgruntled employee emails the customer list to a competitor. ? ?
2 A web server is flooded with fake traffic and goes down for six hours. ? ?
3 An attacker intercepts an unencrypted login and reads the password. ? ?
4 A backup database is found to have corrupted records after a botched migration script. ? ?
5 Ransomware encrypts all files on a school network; teachers cannot access lesson plans. ? ?
6 A developer accidentally pushes a config file with an API key to a public repository. ? ?

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. (5 min) Each pair reads scenarios 1-3 independently and writes their pillar classification and countermeasure in the table. Partners do not discuss yet.
  2. (5 min) Partners compare answers. Where they disagree, they must each defend their classification with one sentence citing the lesson definition. They settle on a consensus answer.
  3. (5 min) Repeat for scenarios 4-6.
  4. (5 min) Each pair opens the simulation and verifies their answers against the sim's scoring. For any scenario they got wrong, they revise their countermeasure.

Collaborate

Assign roles: one partner is the "security analyst" (defends the classification), the other is the "devil's advocate" (argues whether a second pillar is also violated). For scenarios that clearly violate multiple pillars (e.g., #5), they must rank which pillar is the primary violation and explain why. Swap roles halfway through.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student writes one sentence: "Scenario X primarily violates [pillar] because [definition match], and [countermeasure] would reduce the risk by [mechanism]."

Skill 2.1.b · small group · 25 min · Offline: yes

Threat actors and their motivations

Goal

Students analyze incident reports to determine the most likely threat actor profile, their motivation, and select the highest-leverage defensive controls. Ties to LO 2.1.B and the Analyze Risk skill.

Materials

Incident Profiles

Profile Technical observations Impact
A Port scan followed by exploitation of a 3-year-old FTP vulnerability. Attacker tried to install a public coin-miner script. Server CPU went to 100%.
B An employee who was passed over for a promotion downloaded the client list to a USB drive right before their exit interview. Data leaked on a forum.
C Custom stealth rootkit installed on active directories; data copied out slowly over a secure channel to a foreign server IP. High-value IP exfiltrated.
D Web portal flooded with HTTP requests accompanied by a public social media post criticizing the company's environmental record. Website offline.

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. (5 min) Brainstorming: In groups of 3-4, students read each profile (A-D) and match it to one of: Script Kiddie, Insider Threat, Nation-State/APT, or Hacktivist.
  2. (10 min) Defensive Alignment: For each profile, students choose the best mitigation from the following list and explain why:
  3. Least privilege access control + logging
  4. Web application firewall (WAF) + rate limiting
  5. Prompt patching program + disabling unused services
  6. Network segmentation + EDR anomaly detection
  7. (5 min) Peer Review: Two groups swap answers and review each other's actor classifications and controls justification.
  8. (5 min) Class debrief: Review why matching defense to actor motivation saves resources.

Collaborate

Assign roles in each group: - Threat Intelligence Lead: Argues who the threat actor is based on technical indicators. - Defensive Architect: Proposes which control provides the best mitigation. - Finance Director: Questions whether the cost of the proposed control is justified based on the threat actor's motivation and resources.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits a written card: "To defend against a [Threat Actor Category], the most cost-effective control is [Control] because their motivation is [Motivation], making that control highly disruptive to their objective."

Skill 2.1.c · small group · 25 min · Offline: yes

Risk language: likelihood, impact, and security goals

Goal

Students practice analyzing threats, calculating risk scores, comparing control costs against asset values, and selecting and justifying the most appropriate risk treatment strategy (Mitigate, Accept, Transfer, Avoid).

Materials

Risk Scenarios

Scenario Asset & Value Threat & Vulnerability Likelihood (1-5) Impact (1-5) Control & Cost
1 Student email server ($20,000 value) Server fails due to hardware crash. Single server with no backup. 3 4 Mitigation: Backup server ($4,000 cost)
2 Guest Wi-Fi portal ($2,000 value) Portal offline for 2 hours. Web server is low-priority. 4 1 Mitigation: Enterprise redundant server ($5,000 cost)
3 E-Commerce store database ($50,000 value) SQL Injection breach of credit card data. Database stored on-premise. 4 5 Transference: Cyber Insurance policy ($5,000/yr); or Avoidance: Outsource payment processing ($0 cost, but removes store control)
4 Legacy FTP server ($5,000 value) Sensitive trade secrets leaked. Server runs outdated vulnerable firmware. 5 5 Avoidance: Shut down FTP server and use modern secure file-sharing tool.

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. (5 min) Risk Calculation: For each scenario (1-4), calculate the raw Risk Score: $$\text{Risk Score} = \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Impact}$$
  2. (10 min) Treatment Decisions: For each scenario, choose the optimal risk treatment (Mitigate, Accept, Transfer, or Avoid) based on these constraints:
  3. If the Risk Score is high (>= 15), you must act.
  4. If a control costs more than 20% of the asset value, mitigation is not cost-effective.
  5. If a risk cannot be mitigated cost-effectively, consider Transfer or Avoid.
  6. If the Risk Score is low (< 5), consider Acceptance.
  7. (5 min) Peer Presentation: Groups present their recommendations for one scenario to the class, justifying their choice based on the math.
  8. (5 min) Class Review: Discuss why "Avoidance" is a valid option when the risk is high and mitigation costs exceed the budget.

Collaborate

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student writes down one scenario and their recommended strategy: "For Scenario [X], we choose [Strategy] because the Risk Score is [Score] and the control cost of [Cost] relative to the asset value of [Value] makes this the most business-appropriate decision."
