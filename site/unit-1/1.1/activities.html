1.1 Activities

Social Engineering · 3

Skill 1.1.a · pairs · 20 min · Offline: yes

Psychological tactics (intimidation, urgency, authority, scarcity)

Goal

Students learn to name the psychological tactic(s) behind a message and justify a defensive response. Ties to LO 1.1.A (identify indicators) and 1.1.B (explain how tactics influence victims).

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. Each pair opens the simulation and works through the message stream, agreeing on the tactic tag(s) for each before submitting. They must reach the target score.
  2. For any message they got wrong, they write one sentence explaining why the tactic works psychologically (what feeling it engineers).
  3. Pairs draft one original message of their own that stacks at least two tactics, then trade with another pair to classify.

Collaborate

Assign roles: one partner is the "attacker" (defends why their crafted message is convincing), the other is the "analyst" (names the tactics and the defense). Swap roles for the second message.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits: one real-feeling message they wrote + the tactics it uses + the correct defensive action.

Skill 1.1.b · pairs · 25 min · Offline: yes

Elicitation; malicious links and files

Goal

Students practice two skills: (1) recognizing when a conversation is extracting information through elicitation gambits, and (2) reading URLs and filenames methodically to flag malicious indicators. Ties to LO 1.1.A (identify attack indicators) and 1.1.C (describe delivery vectors for social-engineering attacks).

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.

Part A, URL/file inspection (15 min)

  1. Each pair opens the simulation and works through the message set together, agreeing on every indicator before submitting each entry. They must reach the target score.
  2. For any message they got wrong, each partner writes one sentence explaining the rule they missed, why that indicator applies.

Part B, Elicitation role-play (10 min)

  1. Partner A plays a social engineer trying to find out the name of the company's backup server using only indirect questions (no direct "what is the server called?"). Partner B plays a receptionist who will answer natural conversational questions but should refuse direct requests.
  2. After 4 minutes, partners swap roles. The new attacker tries a different gambit (flattery, wrong-statement, or mutual disclosure).
  3. After both rounds, both partners write: which gambit was used, what information was extracted, and what the receptionist should have done differently.

Collaborate

After Part B, each pair teams up with another pair. Share the gambits used and compare what information leaked. Discuss: is it possible to have a normal helpful conversation AND protect sensitive details? What is the minimum a receptionist should reveal to a stranger?

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits: one URL they find in their (personal, non-school) email that contains at least one indicator from the lesson, or "none found", plus which indicator type and why. (If using school devices, the instructor provides a printed set of sample URLs instead.)

Skill 1.1.c · pairs · 25 min · Offline: yes

Email, text, and social media attacks

Goal

Students practice identifying channel-specific phishing indicators across email, SMS, and social media messages, and explain why each channel's trust model makes it exploitable. Ties to LO 1.1.A (identify indicators) and 1.1.C (distinguish attack channels and their characteristics).

Materials

Message set (print and cut into cards)

Card A, Email

From: security-alert@amaz0n-security.net Subject: Your Amazon account has been locked Body: "We detected unusual activity on your Amazon account. Verify your account immediately or your access will be permanently suspended. Click the link below." Link: http://bit.ly/amz-verify

Card B, SMS

From: +1-888-555-0134 "USPS: Your package cannot be delivered. Reroute at http://192.168.0.22/usps or it will be returned to sender within 24 hours."

Card C, Social media DM

From: @NetflixHelpCenter_ (note trailing underscore) "Hi! Your Netflix subscription expires tonight. Confirm your payment info at the link below to keep streaming." Link: https://cutt.ly/netflix-renew

Card D, Email

From: ceo@yourcompany-finance.net Subject: Urgent, wire transfer needed today Body: "I am in a meeting and cannot talk. Please wire $8,500 to the vendor below immediately. Do not loop in accounting. I will explain when I return."

Card E, SMS

From: MOM "Hey, dinner is at 7pm tonight. Can you pick up bread on the way?"

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. Each pair reads all five cards and assigns every applicable indicator label to each card. Use these labels: lookalike-domain, urgent-money-request, credential-request, mismatched-sender, shortened-link. A card may have zero, one, or several labels. Write the labels directly on the card (or on a sticky note attached to it).
  2. For each label they assign, pairs write one sentence explaining WHY that indicator matters: what trust assumption does the attacker exploit on this specific channel?
  3. For cards A, B, and C (multi-indicator cards), pairs identify which single indicator they would notice FIRST if they received this message on their own device, and discuss whether a different channel would have made the same indicator harder to spot.

Collaborate

Assign roles: one partner is the "analyst" (names and defends each label), the other is the "attacker" (argues why the message looks convincing and why a victim might miss that indicator). After completing cards A-E, swap roles and draft one original phishing message of their own, choosing a channel, a brand, and at least two indicators, then trade with another pair to classify.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits in writing: 1. Which card was hardest to classify and why. 2. One original phishing message they drafted, with the channel they chose and every indicator label it uses. 3. The single defensive action a recipient should take upon receiving any of these messages.
