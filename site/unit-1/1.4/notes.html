    Account for the ways an adversary reaches for machine-learning tooling to make an existing attack cheaper, faster, or more believable, synthesising a person's voice or likeness from material already published, drafting fluent phishing text in a language the attacker does not speak, mining public sources for reconnaissance, coaxing sensitive material back out of a language model through crafted prompts, seeding the open web with false claims a model will later repeat, and using code-generation tools to build or adapt malicious software.

I. How AI Changes the Attacker's Toolkit

The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML)AI vs ML vs Deep LearningNested concepts. Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the broad goal: machines that behave intelligently. Machine Learning (ML) is one approach: machines learn from data rather than being explicitly programmed. Deep Learning is a subset of ML using neural networks with many layers ("deep" = many layers). For cybersecurity attacks: GANs and voice cloning are deep learning. Credential stuffing scripts are ML (statistical pattern matching). Simple phishing bots are just AI-scripted. The attacker's toolkit 2024: Large Language Models (LLMs) for spear-phishing text generation, GANs for deepfakes, RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) to personalize messages using LinkedIn/public data. GPT-4-class models can generate convincing, error-free phishing emails in any language, at any scale, for $0.002 per email. architectures has transformed the threat landscape by enabling adversaries to automate and scale highly personalized social engineering attacks. Historically, high-success social engineering campaigns were constrained by the availability of human resources. Crafting personalized spear-phishing emails or conducting interactive voice-based social engineering required human operators, limiting the frequency and scope of attacks. Today, generative artificial intelligence enables threat actors to execute targeted, high-fidelity operations at scale.

A primary vector in AI-driven social engineering is synthetic media, commonly referred to as deepfakesDeepfakeSynthetic media, video, audio, or images, generated by deep neural networks that convincingly impersonates a real person. The term combines "deep learning" and "fake." First popularized in 2017 when a Reddit user ("deepfakes") used ML to superimpose celebrity faces onto other videos. Now weaponized for fraud: CEO voice cloning, video call imposters, fake government ID documents. Deepfake detection: Adobe's "Content Authenticity" (C2PA), Microsoft's Video Authenticator, Sensity AI. Detection features: unnatural blinking patterns, facial edge artifacts, audio-visual sync mismatches, abnormal skin texture. The 2019 UK energy heist and 2024 Hong Kong $25M heist are case studies in this section. Reference: Tolosana et al. (2020). DeepFakes and Beyond: A Survey. Information Fusion.. Deepfakes utilize deep generative neural networksDeep Generative ModelA neural network that learns to generate new data resembling its training data, not just classify existing data. Two main types: GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks) and VAEs (Variational Autoencoders). Also: Diffusion Models (used by DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, Sora). For deepfakes: GANs train a Generator to produce synthetic faces/voices indistinguishable from real ones. For LLM-generated phishing: Transformer models (GPT-4) generate convincing text. Goodfellow et al. introduced GANs in 2014 (NeurIPS). Diffusion models outperformed GANs for image quality by 2022 (DALL-E 2, Stable Diffusion). Voice: ElevenLabs, OpenAI Voice can clone a voice from 3 seconds of sample audio. to synthesize realistic images, video, and audio that replicate the physical appearance and voice of specific individuals. This technology undermines authentication frameworks that rely on biometricsBiometrics, Statistical, Not BinaryUnlike passwords (exact match: "password123" == "password123" → TRUE), biometrics use statistical similarity. A fingerprint scanner measures ridge patterns and computes a match score (0-100%). If score ≥ 85%, identity confirmed. This threshold creates two failure modes: False Accept Rate (FAR), a fake/wrong biometric exceeds threshold; False Reject Rate (FRR), a genuine biometric falls below threshold. Deepfakes exploit FAR: AI generates a synthetic biometric that scores above the threshold. ISO/IEC 19795 defines biometric testing standards. ISO/IEC 30107 defines Presentation Attack Detection (PAD). The threshold is configurable: higher threshold = fewer false accepts (more security) but more false rejects (less convenience). NIST FRVT (Face Recognition Vendor Test) benchmarks facial recognition systems..

Unlike knowledge-based passwords, which are checked using exact binary matching, biometrics are verified using statistical similarity matching. A biometric scanner measures features (such as facial geometry or voice frequency profiles) and calculates a similarity score. If the score exceeds a configured threshold, identity is verified. This statistical natureWhy Statistics Makes Biometrics SpoofableThere is no single "correct" face scan the way there is a correct password. Every time you scan your face, lighting, angle, and facial muscle position vary slightly, so the system uses a threshold. This threshold is the attack surface. A deepfake doesn't need to perfectly replicate your face; it only needs to produce a face that scores above the threshold on the classifier. The classifier was trained on real face data, a GAN trained on the same distribution can generate faces that fool it. This is fundamentally different from passwords. A GAN that produces faces "close to" a target's face can statistically exceed the acceptance threshold even without a perfect replica. PAD (ISO/IEC 30107) adds liveness detection on top of identity detection to catch this class of attack. makes biometrics vulnerable to cloning. By using generative models, attackers can produce synthetic biometric features that match the statistical profile of a target, bypassing authentication systems. These synthetic media tools enable new forms of Business Email Compromise (BEC)BEC, Business Email CompromiseA category of fraud where attackers impersonate executives or trusted partners to trick employees into transferring money or sensitive data. Historically: an attacker spoofs "ceo@corp.com" via email. Now: AI-enhanced BEC uses voice cloning or deepfake video to make the fraud undetectable. The FBI's IC3 reports BEC has cost businesses over $51 billion globally (2023). FBI IC3 2023 Report: BEC losses $2.9B/year in the US alone. Enhanced BEC with voice cloning: FBI PSA October 2023. The attack uses urgency and authority (Cialdini) plus the realism of voice/video to bypass executive approval workflows. Defense: out-of-band verification (call the CEO on a known number), two-person authorization for large transfers. and social engineering, where attackers clone the voices of executives to order fraudulent financial transactions.

The enrolment cost has collapsed Early neural text-to-speech required hours of studio recordings of the target speaker, which meant voice cloning was a project, not an opportunistic attack. Zero-shot systems inverted that economics. Microsoft's VALL-E, described in Wang et al. (2023), arXiv:2301.02111, synthesises speech in an unseen speaker's voice from a three-second enrolment recording, because the model was pre-trained on roughly sixty thousand hours of multi-speaker English and therefore already encodes the space of human voices; the enrolment clip only has to locate a point inside it. Any executive who has spoken on an earnings call, a conference panel, or a recruiting video has published enough audio. The AP framework's phrasing of this risk is deliberately broad, existing voice and image samples are sufficient to build a digital avatar, and the broad phrasing is correct, because the required sample is now measured in seconds.

Language as a lost detection signal

For two decades, awareness training taught employees to read phishing messages for linguistic error: mismatched register, article dropping, calques from the writer's first language, the odd tense. That heuristic was never a statement about attacker skill; it was a statement about attacker labour markets. Campaigns were written by operators working in a second or third language, and the resulting texture was a durable, cheap signal. A general-purpose large language modelLarge Language Model (LLM)A transformer neural network trained on very large text corpora to predict the next token in a sequence, then typically fine-tuned to follow instructions. Because fluency is a property of the training distribution rather than of the operator, an LLM writes idiomatic text in any well-represented language regardless of what language its user speaks. Vaswani et al. (2017), "Attention Is All You Need," arXiv:1706.03762, introduced the transformer architecture. The security consequence is asymmetric: fluency is cheap to buy and expensive to detect, so a defence built on detecting disfluency degrades to nothing while costing the defender the same to run. removes that labour constraint. Fluency is now a property of the tool, not of the writer, and a defence that keys on grammar has been silently downgraded to a coin flip. Personalisation follows the same curve: an adversary can retrieve a target's public posts, conference bios, and corporate filings and condition the message on them, so the pretext references real projects and real colleagues.

The corollary matters more than the attack itself. If linguistic quality no longer separates legitimate from hostile mail, the surviving discriminators are structural rather than stylistic: does the sending domain authenticate, does the request follow the organisation's normal approval path, does the link resolve to a domain the organisation controls, and does the message ask its recipient to bypass a control. Those signals are unaffected by how well the message is written, which is precisely why they are the ones worth training on.

Attacks on the model itself

Generative systems are not only instruments of attack; they are targets. Four classes recur, and NIST catalogues them in NIST AI 100-2 E2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations (March 2025).

  • Evasion. An input is perturbed at inference time so a correctly trained classifier crosses its decision boundary, the malware sample that scores benign, the face that matches the wrong identity. The model is not modified; its input is.
  • Poisoning. The training data is contaminated so the defect is compiled into the weights. The AP framework's example is the direct one: publish or edit web content containing false claims, wait for it to be crawled into a training corpus, and the model repeats it. A backdoor is the targeted variant, a rare trigger pattern is bound to an attacker-chosen output while accuracy on ordinary inputs stays normal, so routine evaluation reveals nothing.
  • Privacy extraction. Model inversion and membership inference recover training material, or confirm that a particular record was in the training set, using nothing but query access. Carlini et al. (2021), arXiv:2012.07805, extracted verbatim sequences including names, phone numbers, and email addresses from a production language model. This is the mechanism behind the framework's warning against putting confidential data into a chatbot: text submitted to a service that retrains on user input may be recoverable by a later, unrelated user.
  • Prompt injection. A model consuming retrieved content cannot separate instructions from data, because both arrive as tokens in one context window. Text embedded in a fetched web page or attached document is therefore executable by default against any agent that reads it. The direct form, a user talking the model past its own restrictions, is the less serious case; the indirect form, where the injected text is planted where an automated pipeline will read it, is the one that scales.

II. How Synthetic Media Is Made: GANs, Diffusion, and Voice Cloning

1. Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) Architecture

Deepfake generation relies on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)GAN, Generative Adversarial NetworkA deep learning architecture invented by Ian Goodfellow et al. (2014) where two neural networks compete: a Generator tries to create fake data, and a Discriminator tries to detect fakes. Like a forger and an art detective in a race: the forger gets better at faking, the detective gets better at detecting, and both improve until the fakes are indistinguishable from real. Goodfellow, I. et al. (2014). Generative Adversarial Nets. NeurIPS. Variants: DCGAN (images), StyleGAN2 (faces, Karras et al. 2020), CycleGAN (unpaired image translation), Pix2Pix. Applications beyond deepfakes: drug discovery (molecular generation), data augmentation, artwork generation, voice synthesis.. A GAN consists of two neural networks trained simultaneously: a Generator ($G$)Generator (GAN)The neural network that creates synthetic data from random noise. Input: a random vector z (sampled from a noise distribution, e.g., Gaussian). Output: a synthetic image/audio/video designed to fool the Discriminator. Early in training: produces blurry, obviously fake output. After thousands of iterations: produces photorealistic output. For face deepfakes: the Generator is a convolutional neural network (CNN) that learns to map noise vectors to face pixel arrays. Architecture: typically a deconvolutional (transposed convolution) network that upsamples noise to image resolution. StyleGAN2 uses a style-based architecture that separates coarse structure (pose, face shape) from fine details (skin texture, lighting), enabling extremely realistic face synthesis. and a Discriminator ($D$)Discriminator (GAN)The neural network that classifies inputs as real (from training data) or fake (from the Generator). Input: either a real image from the training dataset or a fake image from G. Output: probability score 0-1 (0 = definitely fake, 1 = definitely real). Simultaneously trains alongside G: as G improves, D must improve to keep up. When training converges: D outputs 0.5 for all inputs (cannot tell real from fake), this is the Nash Equilibrium. Architecture: a standard convolutional classifier (similar to VGG or ResNet). The Discriminator's gradients flow backward through the loss function to update the Generator's weights via backpropagation. This adversarial gradient signal is what drives G to produce increasingly realistic outputs.. The two models engage in a minimax gameMinimax Game (Game Theory)A mathematical framework where two players have opposing objectives: one minimizes a value function while the other maximizes it. In a GAN: Discriminator D maximizes its ability to detect fakes (maximize log D(x) + log(1-D(G(z)))). Generator G minimizes the probability of being detected as fake (minimize log(1-D(G(z)))). At Nash Equilibrium: neither player can improve unilaterally, G has learned to perfectly mimic the real data distribution. Nash, J.F. (1950). Equilibrium points in n-person games. PNAS. Von Neumann & Morgenstern (1944). Theory of Games and Economic Behavior. In practice: GANs rarely reach true Nash Equilibrium, they can suffer from "mode collapse" (Generator produces only a few types of outputs)., defined by the loss function:

$$\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}(x)}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log (1 - D(G(z)))]$$

Where:

  • $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ is the distribution of real target data (e.g., authentic images of a target's face).
  • $p_z(z)$ is the distribution of input noise vectors used to seed the Generator.
  • $D(x)$ represents the probability that $x$ came from the real training data rather than the Generator.
  • $G(z)$ is the synthetic sample produced by the Generator from noise $z$.

During training, the Discriminator ($D$) is optimized to maximize the probability of correctly identifying real vs. synthetic samples. Concurrently, the Generator ($G$) is trained to minimize the probability that the Discriminator flags its outputs as fake. Over thousands of iterations, the Generator learns to produce highly realistic synthetic faces that can bypass both human observation and automated facial recognition systems.

Reading the GAN loss function The loss function has two terms. Term 1: $\mathbb{E}[\log D(x)]$ — D is rewarded for correctly classifying real data (x ~ real distribution) as real. Term 2: $\mathbb{E}[\log(1-D(G(z)))]$ — D is rewarded for classifying G's output as fake; G is penalized for this (G minimizes it, D maximizes it). Nash Equilibrium: when $p_G = p_{data}$ (Generator's distribution matches real data), the optimal Discriminator outputs $D(x)=\frac{1}{2}$ for all inputs, it genuinely cannot tell real from fake. At this point: training stops improving. The GAN has "won."

2. Diffusion Models: A Different Route to the Same Distribution

The GAN objective is a competition, and competitions can be unstable. If the discriminator becomes too strong its gradients vanish and the generator stops learning; if the generator finds one output that reliably fools the discriminator it can collapse onto that mode, producing convincing but nearly identical samples. Practitioners spend a great deal of effort on architectural tricks to keep the two networks balanced.

Diffusion modelsDenoising Diffusion Probabilistic ModelsA generative family that replaces adversarial training with a regression problem. A fixed forward process adds Gaussian noise to a training image over many steps until it is indistinguishable from noise; a network is then trained to predict the noise that was added at each step. Sampling reverses the chain from pure noise. Because the training target is known exactly, the loss is a plain mean-squared error and there is no second network to balance. Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020), "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models," arXiv:2006.11239. Dhariwal and Nichol (2021), "Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis," arXiv:2105.05233, established the quality crossover. Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022, arXiv:2112.10752) moved the process into a compressed latent space, which is what made high-resolution generation cheap enough to run on consumer hardware. reach the same goal by a different road. A fixed forward process progressively adds Gaussian noise to a real sample until only noise remains. A network is then trained to predict, at each noise level, the noise that was added, a supervised regression problem with an exact target and no adversary. Generation runs the chain backwards: start from noise and denoise, step by step, into a sample. Dhariwal and Nichol (2021), arXiv:2105.05233, showed this family overtaking GANs on image quality, and latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022, arXiv:2112.10752) reduced the cost enough for the method to run on consumer graphics hardware.

The security consequence of that architectural shift is one of accessibility rather than capability. GAN training demanded curated per-target datasets and careful tuning; a diffusion model with text conditioning generalises across subjects, needs no per-target training run, and is distributed as downloadable weights. The barrier to producing convincing synthetic imagery moved from “can train a neural network” to “can install software.”

3. Voice Cloning: Speaker Embedding and Neural Vocoding

Classical text-to-speech built one model per voice. The modern pipeline separates who is speaking from what is said, and that separation is the whole reason a few seconds of audio suffices. A speaker encoderSpeaker Encoder and Speaker EmbeddingA network pre-trained on thousands of speakers to map an arbitrary utterance to a fixed-length vector, typically 192 to 512 dimensions, that captures speaker identity while discarding the words spoken. Two recordings of the same person land near each other in this space; recordings of different people land far apart. Jia et al. (2018), "Transfer Learning from Speaker Verification to Multispeaker Text-To-Speech Synthesis," arXiv:1806.04558, demonstrated the transfer: the encoder trained for speaker verification supplies the conditioning vector for synthesis. The same embedding technology underpins voice-biometric authentication, which is why a system that verifies voices and a system that forges them are trained on the same objective., pre-trained on thousands of unrelated voices, maps a short reference clip to a fixed-length vector encoding vocal-tract geometry and timbre. An acoustic model converts text to a mel spectrogram, conditioned on that vector. A neural vocoderNeural VocoderThe component that converts a mel spectrogram, a compact time-frequency representation, roughly 80 values per frame, into a raw waveform at 16,000 to 24,000 samples per second. The spectrogram discards phase, so the vocoder must invent it plausibly; classical signal-processing vocoders produce the characteristic "robotic" artefacts, and neural vocoders do not. van den Oord et al. (2016), "WaveNet: A Generative Model for Raw Audio," arXiv:1609.03499, was autoregressive and far too slow for real time. HiFi-GAN (Kong et al., 2020, arXiv:2010.05646) reached comparable quality with a feed-forward generator running faster than real time on a single GPU, which is the change that made live conversational deepfakes practical rather than merely offline ones. then turns the spectrogram into a waveform, inventing the phase information the spectrogram discarded.

Two properties of this design matter defensively. First, no training run targets the victim, so there is no observable preparation to detect and no dataset to seize. Second, feed-forward vocoders such as HiFi-GAN (Kong et al., 2020, arXiv:2010.05646) synthesise faster than real time, which is what turns a pre-rendered recording into an interactive conversation that can answer an unexpected question. Telephony compounds the problem: a narrowband voice channel bandlimits the signal to roughly 300–3400 Hz and applies lossy compression, destroying much of the high-frequency detail on which artefact-based detectors depend. The channel that carries the attack also launders it.

  1. Feature Extraction: The target audio is broken into short frames. MFCCs: take the Mel-scaled spectrum → apply discrete cosine transform → result is 13-40 coefficients that compactly represent the voice's spectral character. Attackers can harvest these, photograph them at high resolution, enhance in software, and use them to create spoofing artifacts. III. Case Studies: the UK Voice-Clone Theft and the Hong Kong Deepfake Call

Case Study 1.4.1: The 2019 UK Energy Firm Voice Clone Theft

In March 2019, the chief executive of a UK-based energy firm was targeted in a social engineering fraud that is generally cited as the first publicly documented use of synthetic voice in a corporate theft. The account below comes from the firm's insurer, Euler Hermes, whose fraud expert Rüdiger Kirsch described the case to the Wall Street Journal in Stupp (2019), “Fraudsters Used AI to Mimic CEO's Voice in Unusual Cybercrime Case.” Neither the victim firm nor its parent has been publicly named, and the specific software used was never identified, the insurer's assessment was that AI-based voice software had been used, not a demonstrated forensic finding. The executive believed he was speaking with his boss, the chief executive of the German parent company; he later described recognising the German accent and, tellingly, the “melody” of the man's speech.

The caller applied urgency and authorityCialdini's Principles in AI AttacksThe 2019 voice clone attack weaponized two of Cialdini's six principles simultaneously: Authority (it was the CEO's voice, ultimate authority) and Urgency/Scarcity (penalties if not transferred within one hour). AI voice cloning removes the "something felt off about that call" gut-check humans rely on, the voice was indistinguishable from the real CEO. This is why AI-enhanced social engineering is categorically more dangerous than human-operated attacks: it defeats intuitive System 1 skepticism. Cialdini, R. (2007). Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion. The 2019 case is the first publicly documented AI voice clone fraud. Reported by WSJ: "Fraudsters Used AI to Mimic CEO's Voice in Unusual Cybercrime Case" (Stupp, 2019). (directing the UK executive to transfer €220,000, roughly US$243,000 at the time, to a Hungarian supplier within the hour). The executive complied; the money moved to an account in Hungary and onward to Mexico before dispersing. The caller then telephoned twice more: once to claim falsely that the sum had been reimbursed, and once to request a further payment. That third call is where the attack failed. The promised reimbursement had not arrived, and the call displayed an Austrian number rather than the German one the executive expected. Both discrepancies were structural facts about the transaction and the channel, neither had anything to do with how the voice sounded, and together they were enough to stop the second payment. The attackers are believed to have used commercially available voice cloning softwareCommercial Voice Cloning Availability (2024)Voice cloning is no longer research-only. Publicly accessible tools: ElevenLabs (free tier), Microsoft VALL-E, Respeecher, Resemble AI, Descript. ElevenLabs can clone a voice from 1 minute of sample audio. VALL-E (Microsoft, 2023) achieves high-fidelity cloning from just 3 seconds. Pricing: free to $5/month for most tools. This puts highly realistic voice fraud within reach of any attacker with a computer and internet access. FBI PSA October 2023: "Malicious Actors Manipulating Photos and Videos to Create Explicit Content and Sextortion Schemes." CISA Fact Sheet 2023: "Defending Against AI-Generated Deepfake Fraud." The attack surface: any executive who has appeared in a company video, podcast, news interview, or public speech has provided sufficient audio for a voice clone.. The source recordings were never recovered, so the training material is not documented; the plausible corpus for any senior executive is the ordinary public record of interviews, conference sessions and investor calls.

What actually stopped it It is worth being precise about which signal broke the attack, because the popular retelling gets it backwards. The voice was never doubted, the executive found it convincing on the third call as much as the first. What failed the attackers was verifiable state outside the conversation: a reimbursement that could be checked and had not arrived, and a caller ID from the wrong country. Both are facts about the world, not judgements about the audio. This is the general shape of the problem. Synthetic media attacks the perceptual channel, so a defence sited in that channel, does this sound right, does this look right, is being tested on exactly the axis the attacker optimised. Defences sited outside the channel are not.

Hong Kong Police disclosed this case at a press briefing in February 2024, initially without naming the victim. An employee in the firm's Hong Kong office received an email, apparently from a UK-based senior finance officer, concerning a confidential transaction. The employee was suspicious of the message. He was then invited to a video conference.

On the call he saw and heard what appeared to be the finance officer and several colleagues. The fraud surfaced through ordinary follow-up: when the employee later raised the “confidential transaction” with head office, no such transaction existed. Arup has stated that its own systems were not breached; the technical infrastructure worked exactly as designed, and the compromise was entirely of the authorisation decision.

That detail is the one worth dwelling on. The email alone did not succeed, the employee doubted it, which is what awareness training is supposed to produce. The video call overturned that doubt. A control that depends on a human being sceptical was defeated by supplying the specific evidence humans use to resolve scepticism, which means the control was never as strong as its designers believed; it had simply never been tested by an adversary who could manufacture faces.

Synthetic media as an amplifier, not a new attack Strip out the generative component and this is a business email compromise of a kind the FBI has catalogued for a decade: impersonate an authority figure, invoke confidentiality to suppress verification, invoke urgency to suppress reflection, and split the sum across multiple accounts to complicate recovery. The IC3's 2024 Internet Crime Report puts US losses to that pattern at roughly $2.8 billion in a single year, the overwhelming majority of it with no synthetic media involved at all. What the deepfake supplied was not a new attack path but the removal of the last obstacle on an old one, the employee's residual doubt. Reading the case as “a deepfake attack” misplaces the defensive investment; the exploitable weakness was a payment workflow in which a single person, persuaded, could move HK$200 million. That weakness predates the technology and is fixable without reference to it.

IV. Defending Against Synthetic Media: Liveness, Provenance, and Out-of-Band Checks

Organizations must secure authorization workflows against synthetic media and biometric cloning by implementing the following layered guidelines:

1. Standardizing ISO/IEC 30107 Biometric Liveness Auditing

Ensure that all biometric readers comply with ISO/IEC 30107ISO/IEC 30107, Three Levels of PAD ComplianceLevel 1: Basic liveness detection, detects static photos and basic video replay. Most consumer devices (Face ID, Windows Hello) implement Level 1-2. Testing lab: IDEMIA, Aware Inc., certify ISO/IEC 30107-3 PAD compliance. NIST FRVT MORPH (2024) specifically tests deepfake face morphing attack detection, most commercial systems still fail at Level 3 deepfakes. Defense evolution: generative models and PAD systems are in an ongoing arms race. standards for Presentation Attack Detection (PAD). Biometric verification portals must mandate multi-spectral imagingMulti-Spectral BiometricsUsing multiple light wavelengths (visible light, near-infrared, short-wave infrared) to capture biometric data that reveals properties impossible to fake with surface materials. Visible light: surface features. Near-infrared: vein patterns beneath skin. Short-wave IR: subsurface tissue structure. The standard's value lies in making the defence measurable rather than rhetorical. ISO/IEC 30107-3 defines two error rates that must be reported together: APCER, the proportion of presentation attacks accepted as genuine, and BPCER, the proportion of legitimate users rejected. Quoting one without the other is meaningless, since either can be driven to zero by sacrificing the other. NIST IR 8387, Practical Considerations for Biometric Presentation Attack Detection (2021), is the readable companion for procurement.

Two limits bound what this buys. First, PAD is evaluated against an enumerated set of attack instruments; a novel instrument is, by construction, outside the tested set, and published error rates say nothing about it. Second, and more consequential for the scenarios in this chapter, PAD protects a sensor the defender controls. Neither the 2019 telephone call nor the 2024 conference call involved one. Liveness detection secures a physical door or a supervised enrolment terminal; it has no purchase on an ordinary voice or video call, where the defender's only input is a compressed media stream chosen by the attacker.

What C2PA does and does not prove. A valid manifest establishes that a specific signer asserted a specific processing history for content whose hash matches. It does not establish truth. Nothing prevents an attacker from photographing a screen displaying synthetic video with a C2PA-enabled camera, producing a genuinely signed capture of a fake. More prosaically, provenance data is fragile: a screenshot, a re-encode, or a platform that strips metadata on upload removes the manifest entirely, and absence of a manifest is not evidence of forgery, because most authentic content in circulation has never been signed. C2PA is therefore useful in the affirmative direction, this asset came from this signer, unaltered since, and close to useless as a detector. Treat it as a mechanism for establishing the provenance of content you publish, not for adjudicating content you receive.

3. Multi-Channel Administrative Signing Policies

Enforce strict operational policies for high-value financial transfers and credential updates:

  • Mandate out-of-band verificationOut-of-Band (OOB) VerificationVerifying a transaction through a completely separate communication channel from the one requesting it. If the request came via video call, verify by calling the requestor's registered mobile phone number (a different channel). If the request came via email, verify by calling their desk phone. The "band" is the communication channel, out-of-band means using a different one. Defeats deepfake attacks: the attacker controls the deepfake channel but not the out-of-band channel. SWIFT CSP (Customer Security Programme) requires OOB verification for all high-value wire transfers > $1M. NIST SP 800-63B §5.1.3 uses OOB for transaction signing. The Hong Kong deepfake employee had one simple defense available: call the real CFO on his registered mobile number before executing any transfer. That one phone call would have stopped the $25M fraud. (verify transactions using two independent communication channels, such as email approval combined with a pre-registered callback number).
  • Implement a two-person authorization ruleDual Control / Two-Person Integrity (TPI)A security principle requiring two authorized individuals to independently verify and sign off on sensitive operations, neither can complete the transaction alone. Like a bank vault requiring two keys turned simultaneously. Prevents both fraud (one attacker would need to compromise two independent people) and insider threats.   • Designate secret verification passphrasesVoice Authentication Passphrases (Challenge-Response)Pre-agreed, short secret words or phrases exchanged at the START of a call to verify the caller's identity, before discussing any sensitive information. These three controls share a property that explains their effectiveness: none of them requires anyone to judge whether a voice or a face is genuine. Out-of-band verification works because the attacker who controls the inbound channel does not thereby control the number in the corporate directory; its failure mode is a callback number supplied by the caller, or a target whose mobile number has been ported away in a SIM-swap, which is why the number must come from an independently maintained record. Dual control works because it converts a single act of persuasion into two, against people who should be reached separately; it fails when the second approver rubber-stamps, when both approvers sit on the same compromised call, or when the same social pressure reaches both. A shared passphrase works because a generative model can reproduce a voice but cannot reproduce a secret it has never observed, this is the control the AP framework highlights for personal and family use, and it is genuinely the cheapest defence available. Its failure mode is disclosure: a passphrase sent over email or stored in a document that is later breached is no longer a secret, and one that is never rotated eventually leaks by attrition.

4. Phishing-Resistant Authentication (FIDO2 / WebAuthn)

Where an account is protected by voice biometrics or by a one-time code the user can be talked into reading aloud, the authenticator itself is the weak point. FIDO2 / WebAuthnFIDO2 and WebAuthnAn authentication standard in which the user's device holds a private key and the relying party holds only the corresponding public key. At registration the authenticator generates a key pair scoped to one origin. At sign-in the server sends a random challenge; the authenticator signs it along with the origin the browser is actually connected to, and releases a signature only for the origin the credential was registered against. W3C Web Authentication Level 2 is the browser-facing API; FIDO CTAP2 governs the authenticator. NIST SP 800-63B classifies such authenticators as verifier-impersonation-resistant. Because the private key never leaves the authenticator and never appears on screen, there is nothing for a user to read out to a caller and nothing for a proxy site to replay. removes that weak point structurally. The private key never leaves the authenticator, the signature is bound to the origin the browser actually reached, and no value crosses the user's field of vision that could be dictated over a telephone. A perfectly convincing synthetic executive can demand a code and receive nothing, because no code exists. NIST SP 800-63B classifies these authenticators as resistant to verifier impersonation for precisely this reason.

The limits are real and worth stating. Origin binding defends the authentication event; it does nothing about what a legitimately authenticated user is then persuaded to do, so FIDO2 would not have prevented either case study in this chapter, both victims were authorised users acting within their permissions. Account-recovery paths are the usual practical weakness: an organisation that deploys hardware-backed credentials and retains a helpdesk that resets them on the strength of a phone call has moved the attack rather than removed it, and that helpdesk call is now the one being placed in a cloned voice.

5. Handling Generative Tools as an Untrusted Boundary

The framework's remaining guidance concerns the organisation's own use of AI tools, and it follows from the extraction and poisoning mechanisms described in Section I. Data submitted to a service whose terms permit training on user input should be treated as disclosed, because model inversion and memorisation can surface it to unrelated users later; the operative rule is that a prompt is a transmission to a third party, not a private scratchpad. Correspondingly, output from a generative system carries no evidentiary weight. A model that has ingested poisoned web content will restate the planted claim fluently and without hedging, since fluency is a property of the decoder rather than a signal of grounding. Anything consequential, a security advisory, a configuration change, a CVE identifier, a legal citation, should be confirmed against a stable primary source that is not itself model-generated.

Defending at three levels Defenses against AI-powered attacks operate at three levels: (1) Technical, ISO/IEC 30107 PAD for biometrics; C2PA content credentials for media; FIDO2 WebAuthn (phishing-proof MFA, also defeats voice cloning attacks on login systems). (2) Procedural, dual-control authorization; out-of-band verification; secret passphrases; no single-employee wire authorization. (3) Human, Security awareness training with specific deepfake drills; policy that ANY employee can halt a suspicious transfer without career consequences ("the freeze policy"). Most organizations have only layer 3. Effective defense requires all three.

Self-Check Review Questions

Question 1: Define the optimization game played between a GAN Generator and Discriminator, and explain how a trained Generator bypasses biometric verification.

A GAN Generator (G) and Discriminator (D) engage in a minimax game defined by the value function V(D, G). The Discriminator is trained to maximize the probability of assigning the correct label to both real training data and synthetic data from the Generator (maximizing log D(x) and minimizing log(1 - D(G(z)))). The Generator is trained to minimize the probability that the Discriminator flags its outputs as fake (minimizing log(1 - D(G(z)))). As G is optimized, its generated data distribution converges toward the target data distribution. When G is trained on a specific target's biometric features (such as facial features or vocal patterns), the output mimics the target's physical geometry or spectral characteristics. Because biometric verification systems utilize statistical similarity thresholds rather than binary equality, the high-fidelity output generated by G exceeds the threshold, bypassing authentication.

Question 2: Explain the role of Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) in voice cloning pipelines, and why standard audio recording is insufficient for high-fidelity cloning.

MFCCs represent the power spectrum of a sound signal mapped to the Mel scale, which models the logarithmic frequency perception of the human auditory system. This mapping isolates the resonant characteristics of the speaker's vocal tract (formants), which dictate accent, pronunciation, and timbre. Raw audio recordings capture composite waveforms that include physical acoustic echoes, phase variations, and environmental noise. Standard raw audio contains too much irrelevant information for a model to learn clean vocal profiles. Extracting MFCCs isolates the speaker's vocal characteristics from environmental noise, allowing generative models to learn and replicate their voice profile.

Question 3: What defenses are defined in the ISO/IEC 30107 standard to detect biometric presentation attacks?

ISO/IEC 30107 defines standards for Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) to detect spoofing attempts using artifacts like photos, video playback, or silicone masks. Defenses include liveness detection algorithms that analyze features like micro-vein patterns, skin impedance, capillary blood flow, thermal signatures, eye blinking patterns, and pupil dilation response to light, distinguishing living human tissue from synthetic replicas.

8. Free response1.4.B

Model answer. The first risk is disclosure of sensitive data through the prompt. A model that has ingested poisoned or simply incorrect source material restates it fluently, because fluency is a property of the decoder and carries no information about grounding, a fabricated drug interaction reads exactly like a correct one. Controls: require every clinical or factual assertion to be confirmed against a stable primary source that is not model-generated, restrict use to drafting and summarising material the clinician already knows, and retain a named human accountable for anything entering the record.

Look for: both risks named, input disclosure and unreliable output; a mechanism for each rather than an assertion, specifically memorisation or extraction for the first and the absence of any grounding guarantee for the second; at least one control per risk; and credit for noting that fluency and confidence of phrasing are not evidence of correctness. Answers proposing only staff training should be marked down: the framework's guidance is that sensitive data is not entered at all, which is a rule enforceable by tooling rather than a matter of care.
