1.4 Activities

AI-Augmented Cyberattacks · 5

Skill 1.4.a · pairs · 25 min · Offline: yes

Voice/image cloning for impersonation

Goal

Students apply the classification rules from the lesson to realistic caller scenarios and design a verification procedure for their fictional organization. Ties to LO 1.4.A (identify deepfake indicators) and AP skill "Analyze Risk."

Materials

Scenario cards (use if the simulation is unavailable)

Card A. A call arrives on an internal extension from "the CFO." The voice sounds correct but the CFO asks to wire $45,000 before end of day and specifically says "don't use email, just call me back on this line." The shared code word is not offered when you ask. - Expected classification: suspect (no_shared_secret + refused_callback pattern).

Card B. A video call from your manager. The lighting on her face looks slightly different from the background and her lips seem a fraction out of sync with the audio. - Expected classification: suspect (video_face_mismatch).

Card C. A call from the IT help desk on the official help-desk number asking you to confirm your employee ID for a directory update. No urgency, no financial request. - Expected classification: ok.

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. Each pair works through the the simulation verify-the-caller game. They must reach the target score (5 correct) before moving to step 2. One partner narrates the reasoning aloud while the other drives.
  2. Using the rules in problem/interface.md, each pair writes a one-paragraph "Caller Verification Policy" for a fictional company (Meridian Health). The policy must specify which signals trigger a "suspect" flag and what the employee should do next.
  3. Pairs swap policies with another pair and critique: does the swapped policy cover all seven signal types? Does it tell an employee what action to take on a "suspect" call?

Collaborate

Assign roles in step 3: one partner is the "security auditor" (finds gaps in the swapped policy), the other is the "policy author" (defends or revises). After critique, both agree on a final merged policy.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student writes: one signal they would add to the policy that is NOT in the default list, and explains why it would help detect a cloned call.

Skill 1.4.b · pairs · 20 min · Offline: yes

Generative-AI phishing; prompt injection against LLM apps

Goal

Students interact with the scripted SecureBot in the simulation, systematically probe all five known injection-pattern families, and then analyze why each defense responded the way it did. They produce one original injection string, label its pattern type, and describe a concrete operator-side patch. Ties to LO 1.4.A (identify AI-based attack techniques) and AP skills "Analyze Risk" and "Mitigate Risk."

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.

  1. Open the simulation and read the objective panel (3 min). One partner reads the objective aloud while the other reads the list of five patterns to probe. Agree on who types and who records.

  2. Probe all five patterns (10 min). Working as a pair, take turns typing messages into SecureBot's chat box. Your goal is to trigger each of the five pattern families:

  3. "ignore previous instructions" (or "ignore prior")
  4. "reveal" combined with "system"
  5. "you are now" or "pretend you are"
  6. "disregard"
  7. "system:" as a prefix

For each triggered pattern: note what you typed, what SecureBot replied, and whether the response blocked, deflected, or redirected you. The recorder writes these down.

  1. Read the objective-met analysis (3 min). When all five patterns are probed, the simulation shows a summary panel explaining each defense. Read it together. Flag any explanation that surprises you.

  2. Design a patch (4 min). Choose ONE of the five patterns. Write a one-sentence description of a structural control an operator could deploy to stop that pattern from reaching the LLM at all. Your patch must go beyond "tell the model to ignore it", it should be a system-level or pipeline-level control (e.g., a preprocessing filter, a permission boundary, a human approval gate).

Collaborate

After step 4, swap your patch description with the adjacent pair. Each pair critiques the swap: does the described patch actually prevent the injection, or could an attacker rephrase to bypass it? Return the critique in writing. Authors revise if they agree with the critique.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits individually:

  1. One injection string they personally typed that triggered a SecureBot pattern.
  2. The name of the pattern family it belongs to (from the five listed above).
  3. One operator-side control that would block that string before it reaches the LLM, and a one-sentence explanation of why that control is harder for an attacker to bypass than a natural-language instruction in the system prompt.
Skill 1.4.c · Pairs · 20 min · Offline: yes

Prompt extraction of sensitive info

Goal

Students play the "Redact the Leak" simulator in pairs, then connect each leak type to a concrete real-world harm. Each pair produces one exit ticket entry per leak class they observed.

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.

  1. Play (8 min). Both partners sit at one machine. Take turns being the "filter operator" (clicking the answer buttons) while the other person reads the snippet aloud and tries to spot the leak before the operator answers. Play through all 6 snippets. Record your score.

  2. Debrief the types (7 min). For each leak type you encountered (API Key, System Prompt, Email Address, Password), discuss:

  3. What could an attacker do with this specific piece of data?
  4. Who is harmed, the operator, the end user, or both?
  5. What single control would have prevented the leak from appearing in the LLM output?

Use concrete terms. For example: "A leaked API key for the fraud-detection service means an attacker can call that service using the bank's account, potentially exhausting rate limits or accessing fraud flags on real accounts."

  1. Exit ticket (5 min). Each student individually writes four rows in their notebook, one per leak class, following this format:
Leak class Real-world harm (1 sentence) One defense
API key ... ...
System prompt ... ...
Email address ... ...
Password ... ...

Collaborate

Assign roles within each pair: one student argues from the attacker's perspective ("here is what I can do with this leak") and the other argues from the defender's perspective ("here is the control I would deploy"). Switch roles between leak types. The goal is to make sure both students can articulate both sides.

If time permits, compare your exit tickets with an adjacent pair and identify any defense they listed that you did not, or vice versa.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits their four-row table (leak class / harm / defense) before leaving. One-sentence answers are sufficient. The instructor checks that each defense is specific, "use an output filter" alone is too vague; "use an output filter with a regex matching sk-[A-Za-z0-9]{8,}" or "never embed live API keys in the system prompt" are acceptable.

Skill 1.4.d · pairs · 20 min · Offline: yes

False information in LLM training sets (data poisoning)

Goal

By the end of the activity, each pair will have (1) correctly tagged all three poisoned rows in the the simulation dataset, (2) classified each poisoned row by attack goal (availability or integrity/backdoor), and (3) produced one original poisoned row of their own with a written classification.

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.

  1. Inspect (8 min). Open the simulation. Each partner reads all 8 rows independently and marks the ones they suspect are poisoned. Do not discuss yet.

  2. Compare and submit (5 min). Compare your selections with your partner. Where you disagree, argue your case using evidence from the content and label. Agree on a final set of checked boxes and click Submit. Read the feedback for each poisoned row.

  3. Classify by attack goal (5 min). For each of the three poisoned rows your sim identified, answer in writing:

  4. Which attack goal does this serve -- availability attack (degrade overall accuracy) or integrity/backdoor attack (specific targeted misclassification)?
  5. How would a deployed model behave differently because of this poisoned sample?
  6. Row 2 (label/content mismatch, negative content labeled positive): integrity attack -- the classifier learns to suppress negative-sentiment alerts.
  7. Row 3 (label/content mismatch, positive content labeled negative): integrity attack -- positive reviews are flagged incorrectly.

  8. Row 6 (trigger word outlier): backdoor -- the trigger token causes forced misclassification on any future input containing it.

  9. Exit ticket (2 min). Each student individually writes one new poisoned row they could add to a sentiment-analysis training dataset. The row must include a label, content text, and a written answer to: "Is this a mismatch-type or outlier-type poisoning indicator, and why?"

Collaborate

Assign roles for step 1: Partner A focuses on label/content agreement and Partner B focuses on statistical anomalies (unusual length, repeated tokens, uncommon words). Bring both perspectives to the step 2 discussion. Neither partner should defer without a reason -- if you disagree, state which detection rule you are applying.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits (on paper or typed): one invented poisoned row with label, content, and a one-sentence classification as mismatch-type or outlier-type with justification. Example of an acceptable answer: "Label='positive', content='This device overheated and ruined my desk.' -- Mismatch-type: the label is positive but the content describes product failure, which would register as negative in any honest annotation."

Skill 1.4.e · Pairs · 25 min · Offline: yes

AI reconnaissance and enhanced malware coding; defenses (shared secrets, MFA, verify output)

Goal

Students triage the Meridian Bank server log in the simulation, identify which entries indicate AI-assisted reconnaissance, map each flagged entry to the recon technique it represents, and name the specific defense control that would limit that technique. Each pair produces a completed analysis table and one original log entry of their own as an exit ticket.

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.

  1. Triage the sim (8 min). One partner drives the simulation; the other narrates. Work through all 8 log entries. Check boxes for entries you believe are recon indicators. Do not submit yet, discuss each selection before clicking Submit.

  2. Submit and record results (3 min). Click Submit. Note your score. For each entry the sim marks as incorrect, read the explanation and update your understanding.

  3. Complete the analysis table (10 min). For each of the 3 confirmed recon entries, fill in the table below. Use the defense names from problem/prompt.md (exact strings matter for the coding problem).

Line Log text (abbreviated) Recon technique Defense control
2 Port scan 1-65535 in 4s
4 DNS burst 847 subdomain queries in 2s
6 Login attempts: 312 usernames in 8s
  1. Exit ticket, write your own log entry (4 min). Each partner independently writes one new log line that would signal AI-assisted reconnaissance (different from the three already seen). Write the defense that would address it. Examples are permitted to use subdomain enumeration, user-agent cycling, CVE scanning, or any other technique from the atom.

Collaborate

Assign roles before Step 1: one person is the Security Analyst (decides which entries to flag and explains why), the other is the Defense Architect (names the control for each flagged entry). Swap roles for the exit ticket. If you disagree on a classification, write both positions and the reasoning, the sim's explanation will arbitrate.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits individually: - One original log entry simulating AI-assisted reconnaissance (written by that student, not copied from the sim). - The name of the defense control that would limit the technique shown in their entry. - One sentence explaining why the entry indicates automated rather than human activity.
