Endpoint detection and response (EDR)

Unit 4 · Securing Devices · 4.4 Endpoint Threat Auditing

Nice work today. Here's what to lock in:

  • Recap handout: post-lecture-handout.docx
  • Simulation: SimLab
  • Self-check quiz: due before next class

The big picture: The Meridian scenario, Office spawning PowerShell, PowerShell making an unusual outbound connection, a new executable landing in a temp folder, is worth remembering as a worked example of "living off the land," and it's the cleanest illustration in this unit of why signature-based defense (4.3.b) structurally cannot catch everything: PowerShell itself is completely legitimate software that ships with Windows, so no signature will ever flag it, and the only way to catch this attack chain is to notice the sequence is abnormal even though every individual step used trusted tools. That's the same behavioral-baseline logic from endpoint protection, just extended across four different telemetry streams, process, file, registry, network, instead of one, and it directly sets up the next two atoms: EDR gives you a live, single-device view, log analysis (4.4.b) extends that same pattern-recognition instinct across many systems and across time, and indicators of compromise (4.4.c) formalizes how you combine clues from both into a confident verdict. Keep the response half in mind too, collect-detect-respond in one agent is specifically what separates EDR from a SIEM (which correlates but can't act) and from antivirus (which detects but rarely isolates), and isolation is worth remembering precisely: it cuts network access to stop lateral movement, it does not wipe or destroy anything.

Key terms to lock in:

  • Endpoint: any device (laptop, server, workstation) that is an origin or destination for network traffic and hosts a security agent.
  • EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response): security software that collects device telemetry, detects malicious behavior using signatures and behavioral models, and can respond automatically to contain threats.
  • Telemetry: the stream of raw event data (process, file, network, registry events) that the EDR agent reports from the device.
  • Behavioral detection: flagging activity that deviates from a learned baseline, even when no matching signature exists.
  • Isolation: the EDR response action that cuts a device's network connections to prevent lateral movement while preserving remote analyst access.
  • Living off the land: attacker technique of using legitimate OS tools (PowerShell, WMI, certutil) to carry out malicious actions, making detection harder for signature-based tools.

Common misconceptions to watch for:

  • "EDR is just better antivirus." -> Antivirus primarily checks files against known signatures at rest. EDR monitors continuous runtime behavior across process, file, network, and registry streams and can respond by isolating, killing, or quarantining, not just scanning.
  • "If nothing alerts, the device is clean." -> EDR relies on behavioral baselines and signatures that may not cover every possible attack. No detection tool guarantees completeness; layered defenses and regular threat-hunting are still required.
  • "Isolation means the device is wiped." -> Isolation cuts network access but does not destroy data. The analyst can still connect to the isolated device through the EDR console to investigate and recover.
