Before class today. Antivirus and firewalls (4.3.a, 4.3.b) defend a device from the outside, they watch for known-bad files and traffic. But many of the most damaging breaches in history did not require tricking a user or evading a signature at all. They exploited a known, published vulnerability in software that the victim organization had simply not yet fixed.

The intuition to hold onto: The sentence worth sitting with before the lecture: publishing a patch also publishes a roadmap of exactly what was broken, because attackers can reverse-engineer the diff between the old code and the new code to see precisely what the vendor just fixed. That single fact is why "patch promptly" isn't just good hygiene, it's a race that starts the moment the fix ships, and it reframes the whole atom around a genuinely surprising idea: the vulnerability becomes more dangerous, not less, in the hours right after it gets patched, because that's exactly when weaponized exploit code starts circulating against everyone who hasn't updated yet.

A myth worth busting before we start: "Zero-days are the main way organizations get breached." → Most real-world breaches exploit n-day vulnerabilities, known flaws with an available patch that simply was not applied in time. Zero-days are rare and expensive for attackers by comparison.
