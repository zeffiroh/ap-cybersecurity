Device hardening

Unit 4 · Securing Devices · 4.3 Endpoint Hardening Controls

Before class today. Every device shipped from the factory arrives with a default configuration designed to maximize compatibility and ease of setup, not security. Default administrator passwords are identical across thousands of units. Unneeded services run because the manufacturer assumed the buyer might want them.

The intuition to hold onto: The image worth carrying into this skill: every device ships from the factory optimized for the easiest possible setup, not for security, which means "default configuration" and "maximally exploitable configuration" are often the same thing. Hardening isn't adding new protection so much as it's removing invitations that were switched on by default, the open port nobody uses, the guest account nobody disabled, the admin password that's identical across ten thousand identical devices sitting on shelves. Before you can defend a device, you have to first stop advertising it as an easy target.

A myth worth busting before we start: "Only servers need hardening." → Every device with a network connection, laptops, printers, phones, cameras, has an attack surface and must be hardened.

Today's goal: Describe the core device-hardening controls, apply a hardening checklist to a sample configuration, and explain why reducing a device's attack surface lowers organizational risk. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
