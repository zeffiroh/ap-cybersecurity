4.2.b, Multi-factor authentication

Before class today. A password is a single piece of evidence: if an attacker learns it, through phishing, a data breach, or a guessed weak password, they can log in exactly as the real user, with no further obstacle. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) closes this gap by requiring proof from two or more independent categories of evidence before granting access.

The intuition to hold onto: Before the lecture, fix this trap in your head so it doesn't catch you on the exam: two passwords is not two-factor authentication, no matter how many boxes you type into. What makes something MFA is that the factors come from different categories, something you know, something you have, something you are, because the whole point is forcing an attacker to succeed at two structurally different kinds of theft simultaneously, not just guess twice. A stolen password plus a stolen second password is still just one skill (guessing or phishing secrets) executed twice.

A myth worth busting before we start: "Entering two different passwords is two-factor authentication." → Both are "something you know", the same factor category twice. True MFA requires two different categories.

Today's goal: Explain why single-factor (password-only) login is insufficient, classify a described authentication method into its factor category, judge whether a login attempt satisfies true multi-factor authentication, and identify which MFA methods are phishing-resistant versus phishable. breach, or a guessed weak password, they can log in exactly as the real user, with no further

independent *categories* of evidence before granting access.

A stolen password plus a stolen second password is still just one skill (guessing or phishing secrets) executed twice.

**A myth worth busting before we start:** "Entering two different passwords is two-factor authentication." → Both are "something you know", the same factor category twice. True MFA requires two *different* categories.
