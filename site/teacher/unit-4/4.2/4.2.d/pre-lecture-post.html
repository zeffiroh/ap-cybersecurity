4.2.d

Least privilege and access control models

Unit 4 · Securing Devices · 4.2 Device Authentication Hardening

Before class today. Every account, process, and service on a system needs some level of access to do its job, a help-desk technician needs to reset passwords, a database service needs to read certain tables, a web server needs to write to a log directory. The principle of least privilege (PoLP) says that every subject should be granted the minimum access required to perform its function, and nothing more.

The intuition to hold onto: Walk into this skill with a single sharp question ready: if this account gets phished tomorrow, what's the worst thing the attacker inherits? The principle of least privilege isn't a compliance checkbox, it's a bet that you can't stop every credential from eventually leaking, so the only lever left is making sure each individual leak is cheap. A marketing intern's read-only drive access being compromised is an inconvenience; the same intern's account having write access to production is a company-wide incident, and the only difference between those two outcomes is a permission that never should have been granted in the first place.

A myth worth busting before we start: "Giving admin rights to everyone is more convenient and low-risk if you trust your employees." → Trust does not shrink blast radius. A trusted employee's account can still be phished or infected by malware, and malware inherits the privileges of whoever runs it, over-provisioning turns any single compromise into an organization-wide one.

Today's goal: Explain the principle of least privilege, describe why over-privileged accounts increase risk, distinguish DAC, MAC, RBAC, and ABAC access control models, and recognize privilege creep and the controls that mitigate it. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
