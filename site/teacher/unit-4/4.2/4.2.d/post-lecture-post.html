Least privilege and access control models

Unit 4 · Securing Devices · 4.2 Device Authentication Hardening

Nice work today. Here's what to lock in:

  • Recap handout: post-lecture-handout.docx
  • Simulation: SimLab
  • Self-check quiz: due before next class

The big picture: The four access-control models (DAC, MAC, RBAC, ABAC) are easiest to keep straight if you ask who's making the decision and when: DAC lets the resource owner decide, case by case, which is why it scales badly, permissions sprawl with nobody enforcing consistency. MAC decides centrally with fixed labels, trading flexibility for near-total resistance to insider misconfiguration. RBAC decides by role, which is why it dominates enterprise IT, changing someone's job means reassigning a role, not hand-editing dozens of permissions. ABAC decides dynamically, at the moment of the request, using context like time of day or device posture, trading simplicity for real-time responsiveness. Notice that privilege creep is really the same silent-decay problem you saw with configuration drift already waiting for you in 4.3.a, access that was correct on day one degrades into a liability because revoking permissions requires someone to notice and act, while granting them is just a routine part of onboarding. That's exactly why periodic access review, separation of duties, and just-in-time access all exist: not to get the permission model right once, but to keep re-proving it's still right as roles quietly change underneath it.

Key terms to lock in:

  • Least privilege (PoLP): grant a subject only the minimum access needed to perform its function, nothing more.
  • DAC (Discretionary Access Control): resource owner decides and grants access; e.g. Unix file permissions.
  • MAC (Mandatory Access Control): access decided centrally via fixed classification labels; e.g. government/military systems, SELinux.
  • RBAC (Role-Based Access Control): permissions attached to roles; users assigned to roles. Most common model in enterprise IT.
  • ABAC (Attribute-Based Access Control): access decided dynamically from attributes of user, resource, and environment.
  • Privilege creep: the silent accumulation of unnecessary access over time as roles change.
  • Separation of duties: structuring a sensitive process so no single person controls it end-to-end.
  • Access review: a periodic recertification where unused or unjustified access is revoked.
  • Just-in-time (JIT) access: temporary, time-bound elevated access granted only for the duration it is needed.

Common misconceptions to watch for:

  • "Giving admin rights to everyone is more convenient and low-risk if you trust your employees." → Trust does not shrink blast radius. A trusted employee's account can still be phished or infected by malware, and malware inherits the privileges of whoever runs it, over-provisioning turns any single compromise into an organization-wide one.
  • "RBAC and ABAC are the same thing." → RBAC assigns static permissions to a role and a user inherits that role's access; ABAC evaluates dynamic attributes (user, resource, environment) at the moment of the request. RBAC answers "what role are you," ABAC answers "do current conditions satisfy the policy."
  • "Once access is granted correctly, it stays correct." → Access correctness decays over time as people change roles (privilege creep). Least privilege requires ongoing periodic access review, not a one-time setup.

Learning objective(s): 4.2.B, 4.2.C
