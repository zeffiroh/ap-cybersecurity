The big picture: The Mirai botnet is worth remembering not as a scary anecdote but as proof of a genuinely counterintuitive idea: individually worthless devices become collectively catastrophic purely through scale, with no sophistication required at all, just a scanner trying the same three or four factory passwords against every IP address on the internet. That should reshape how you think about "shadow IoT" as a risk category: it's not dangerous because any one unapproved smart plug is powerful, it's dangerous because attack surface is additive and invisible devices can't be hardened by a team that doesn't know they exist. Hold onto that connection into 4.3.a, where device hardening turns out to be the direct answer to exactly the first two bullet points here, change every default credential, close every unnecessary port, applied as a discipline to every device, not just the traditional laptops and servers a security team is used to thinking about.

Key terms to lock in: - IoT (Internet of Things), everyday physical devices that connect to a network/internet (cameras, sensors, appliances, controllers) beyond traditional computers and phones. - Default credentials, the factory-set username/password shipped on a device, often never changed by the owner, and widely known/scanned for by attackers. - Firmware, the low-level software embedded in a device that controls its core operation; often lacks an automatic update mechanism on IoT hardware. - Attack surface, the complete set of points where an attacker could try to get into or extract data from a system; more exposed devices/services/ports means a larger attack surface. - Botnet, a network of compromised devices remotely controlled by an attacker as a group, often used for large-scale DDoS attacks. - Mirai, a 2016 botnet malware that hijacked IoT devices via default Telnet credentials and launched record-setting DDoS attacks; the canonical real-world IoT botnet example. - UPnP (Universal Plug and Play), a protocol that lets devices automatically open ports on a router, which can unintentionally expose management interfaces to the internet. - Shadow IoT, devices connected to a network without IT's knowledge, approval, or security oversight, silently expanding the attack surface. - EOL (end-of-life) device, a device whose manufacturer has stopped issuing updates or support, leaving any undiscovered or unpatched vulnerabilities permanently exploitable.

Common misconceptions -- don't fall for these: - "IoT devices are too small or simple to be worth attacking." → False. Attackers rarely care about the device itself; they want it as a botnet node, a pivot point into the rest of the network, or a source of sensitive data (camera feeds, sensor logs). Scale, not sophistication, is what makes IoT attractive. - "If a device isn't connected to the internet, it's safe." → A device on a local network can still be reached by anything else already on that network (a compromised laptop, a malicious guest device), and many "local-only" devices still phone home to a cloud API. Network placement reduces but does not eliminate risk. - "Changing the Wi-Fi password protects every device on the network." → A Wi-Fi password controls who can join the network, not whether devices on that network trust each other. A device with default admin credentials or an exposed Telnet port is just as exploitable by another device already inside the network (or through UPnP-punched holes from outside it).

Learning objective(s): 4.1.A, 4.1.C
