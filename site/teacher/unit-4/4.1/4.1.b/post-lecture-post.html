The big picture: What makes the USB Rubber Ducky and the hardware keylogger worth remembering together is that they attack from opposite directions of the exact same blind spot: operating systems are built to implicitly trust anything that looks like a keyboard, and antivirus software only ever inspects files and running code, never raw electrical signals arriving before the OS gets involved. Neither attack is a software bug; both are a category of threat that pure software defense literally cannot see, which is exactly why the fixes in this skill (BIOS passwords, disabled boot-from-USB, port blockers) are physical and firmware-level rather than another antivirus signature. Full-disk encryption deserves special attention here because it's your first real preview of Unit 4.2's authentication material: FDE bound to a TPM is really a possession-based unlock gate for an entire drive, and the cold-boot attack is a reminder, one you'll see again with session tokens in 4.2.c, that a cryptographically sound control can still fail because of exactly when it's active, not because the crypto itself is weak.

Key terms to lock in: - Device tampering, unauthorized physical modification or interception of a device to compromise its security. - USB Rubber Ducky, a keystroke injection tool disguised as a standard USB flash drive that executes pre-programmed commands by mimicking a keyboard. - Hardware keylogger, a physical device connected between a keyboard and computer that records all keystrokes. - Full Disk Encryption (FDE), a cryptographic method that encrypts all data on a storage drive, including the operating system, protecting it from offline reading if the drive is stolen. - Trusted Platform Module (TPM), a dedicated microchip on the motherboard designed to secure hardware by integrating cryptographic keys and verifying boot sequence integrity. - Port blockers, physical inserts placed into unused external ports (like USB or Ethernet) to prevent unauthorized devices from being plugged in. - Chassis intrusion detection, a switch inside a device's case that logs or alerts when the case is opened, providing after-the-fact evidence of physical tampering. - Tamper-evident seal, a sticker or label placed over a device's case or port that visibly tears, discolors, or breaks if removed, letting staff visually confirm a device has not been opened.

Common misconceptions -- don't fall for these: - "Operating system passwords protect my data if my laptop is stolen." → Bypassed. An attacker can remove the hard drive and read it on another system, or boot from a Linux USB to browse files, unless Full Disk Encryption (FDE) is enabled. - "Antivirus software will block malicious USB keyboard emulators." → Antivirus scans files, but a USB Rubber Ducky acts as a keyboard. The OS trusts it as a typing human, meaning it bypasses software-level malware scans unless specific USB device whitelisting is active. - "A locked office building means the devices inside are physically secure." → Building access controls only stop outsiders; they do nothing against a malicious insider, a visitor left unattended, or a device sitting in an unlocked room within an otherwise secure building. Physical security requires layered controls at the building, room, and device level.
