4.1.b, Device tampering and physical compromise

Before class today. The fundamental rule of hardware security is simple: if an attacker has physical access to your device, it is no longer your device. Logical controls, such as passwords, firewalls, and network access lists, assume that the physical chassis of the machine is secure. If an adversary can touch a computer, plug a device into it, or open its case, they can bypass almost every software-based defense.

The intuition to hold onto: The sentence to actually internalize here is blunt on purpose: if an attacker has physical access to your device, it is no longer your device. Everything you've learned so far about strong passwords and clever authentication schemes quietly assumes the chassis in front of you is trustworthy, that nobody has opened the case, swapped the RAM, or plugged something into a port while you weren't looking. Unit 2 spent a lot of time on physical security for buildings; this skill is the same argument scaled down to a single laptop, and the punchline is identical: no software control, however well designed, survives an attacker who can touch the hardware it runs on.

A myth worth busting before we start: "Operating system passwords protect my data if my laptop is stolen." → Bypassed. An attacker can remove the hard drive and read it on another system, or boot from a Linux USB to browse files, unless Full Disk Encryption (FDE) is enabled.

Today's goal: Identify physical security threats (including hardware keyloggers, malicious USB drop attacks, and unauthorized physical access), explain how physical compromise bypasses logical network defenses, and analyze strategies to mitigate device tampering. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
