The big picture: The three indicators worth being able to name cold, unusual port usage, beaconing, and volume spikes, share a single underlying idea worth remembering as one thing rather than three: none of them require reading a single byte of payload to be useful. A host beaconing to a command-and-control server at suspiciously regular intervals is detectable purely from timing, a data-exfiltration spike is detectable purely from volume and direction, and that's precisely why flow records (cheap, payload-free summaries) can catch what a full packet capture would only confirm after the fact at far greater storage cost. That's also the connective tissue linking this skill back to 3.1.d's packet-sniffing atom from earlier in the unit: encryption defeats an attacker trying to read your payload, but it does absolutely nothing to hide the pattern of your traffic, which is exactly the signal network monitoring is built to exploit on defense instead. Layer this skill on top of 3.5.a and 3.4's firewall atoms and you get the complete three-tier picture this unit has been building toward the whole time: the firewall decides what's allowed to start, the IDS matches what's already known to be bad, and monitoring catches everything left over, attacks using fully permitted ports and protocols, with no matching signature, that only give themselves away through volume, timing, or destination drifting off the baseline.

Key terms to lock in:

  • Packet capture (pcap): full-fidelity recording of every packet, including payload; precise but storage- and search-expensive at scale.
  • Flow record (NetFlow): a summarized record of a single conversation (endpoints, ports, protocol, byte/packet counts, duration) without payload; the common basis for large-scale monitoring.
  • Baseline: the recorded profile of "normal" traffic (volume, ports, timing, destinations) that current traffic is compared against.
  • Beaconing: regular, machine-timed outbound connections characteristic of malware command-and-control check-ins.
  • Data exfiltration (volume-based indicator): an abnormal spike in outbound data volume, often the clearest sign that data is actively leaving the network.
  • SIEM (Security Information and Event Management): a platform that aggregates and correlates logs and flow data from many sources for centralized analyst query and alerting.

Common misconceptions to watch for:

  • "You need full packet capture to detect an attack." → Most anomalies (volume spikes, beaconing, unusual ports) are visible in flow records alone, without ever inspecting payload content.
  • "A baseline is set once and never changes." → Legitimate traffic patterns shift (new applications, seasonal usage, staff changes); baselines must be re-established periodically or monitoring drifts into constant false positives.
  • "Any traffic on an unusual port is malicious." → Unusual-port traffic is a prompt for investigation, not automatic proof of compromise, new legitimate software can also introduce new ports. Volume, destination, and timing context matter together.

Learning objective(s): 3.5.B, 3.5.E
