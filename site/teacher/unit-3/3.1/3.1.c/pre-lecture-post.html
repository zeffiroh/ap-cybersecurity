Man-in-the-middle and ARP spoofing

Unit 3 · Securing Networks · 3.1 Network-Level Exploits

Before class today. A man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack is any attack where an adversary secretly positions itself between two communicating parties, relaying (and often reading or altering) traffic while both sides believe they are talking directly to each other.

The intuition to hold onto: The mental model worth having before lecture: ARP is a network's equivalent of everyone in a room shouting their own name whenever asked, with zero ID check, and everyone else just believing the loudest recent answer. That's not a metaphor stretched to fit, it's literally how a gratuitous ARP reply works, an answer nobody even asked for that devices accept anyway. Once you've internalized that ARP has no authentication at all, ARP spoofing isn't a clever exploit, it's just... using the protocol exactly as specified, except lying.

A myth worth busting before we start: "HTTPS fully protects you from a man-in-the-middle." → HTTPS encrypts and authenticates the content of the connection, preventing the attacker from reading or silently modifying it without detection, but it does not stop the attacker from occupying the MITM position in the first place, seeing which servers you connect to, or degrading service.

Today's goal: Explain how the Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) can be abused to insert an attacker between two hosts on a local network, trace the mechanics of an ARP spoofing attack step by step, and identify the technical controls that detect or prevent it. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
