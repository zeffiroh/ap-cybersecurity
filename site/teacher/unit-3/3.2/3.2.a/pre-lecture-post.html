3.2.a, Wi-Fi basics and wireless security protocols (WPA2/WPA3)

Before class today. Every modern office, school, and home relies on Wi-Fi, the family of wireless networking standards collectively known as IEEE 802.11. Radio signals are convenient, but they are also inherently public: anyone within range can receive the same frames your device sends. Without a strong security protocol layered on top, an attacker sitting in a parking lot can eavesdrop on every packet.

The intuition to hold onto: The frame worth carrying in: Wi-Fi has no cable, which means Wi-Fi has no privacy by default, every frame you send radiates in every direction to anyone with an antenna in range, full stop. WPA2 and WPA3 aren't optional polish on top of a private medium; they're the entire reason Wi-Fi is usable for anything sensitive at all, because without them you'd be broadcasting your traffic to the parking lot the same way you'd be shouting a phone call across a crowded room. Keep that mental picture, broadcasting into public space, and the rest of the atom's design decisions (why authenticate, why encrypt, why the handshake matters) stop being arbitrary standards and start being the obvious response to a genuinely public medium.

A myth worth busting before we start: "Hiding the SSID makes the network secure." → A hidden SSID is still broadcast in probe responses; any passive scanner finds it in seconds. SSID hiding is not a security control.

Today's goal: Compare WPA2 and WPA3 wireless security protocols, explain the vulnerabilities each addresses, and evaluate a Wi-Fi network configuration against current best practice. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
