Keep least privilege and privilege creep especially sharp for the exam: the technical enforcement is an ACL denying a specific file read, but the managerial control is the recurring review process that catches when someone's access should have been revoked months ago and wasn't, miss that distinction and you'll misclassify half of this skill's practice scenarios. This skill is also the hinge that connects Unit 3's very technical first half (protocols, spoofing, sniffing) to 3.2.c's layered-defense model, where you'll see the managerial layer named explicitly as the one that spans and reinforces every other layer rather than sitting in its own separate lane.

Key terms to lock in: - Managerial (administrative) control, a documented policy, procedure, or process governing human behavior and organizational process, enforced through training, review, and consequences rather than automatically by hardware/software. - Technical (logical) control, a control implemented in hardware or software that enforces policy automatically (e.g., a firewall, encryption, an access-control list). - Physical control, a control that restricts physical access to a space or device (e.g., a locked door, a badge reader). - Acceptable Use Policy (AUP), a policy defining permitted and prohibited use of organizational systems and data. - Least privilege policy, a policy requiring that access be limited to the minimum necessary for a role, with a documented request/review/revocation process. - Security awareness training, a recurring program that teaches employees to recognize threats (like social engineering) and follow security policy. - Privilege creep, the gradual, unreviewed accumulation of unnecessary access rights over time, which least-privilege review policies are designed to catch and correct.

Common misconceptions -- don't fall for these: - "A policy is not a real control unless it's enforced by software." → Managerial controls are a distinct, legitimate control family (alongside technical and physical). A signed AUP, documented access-review cadence, or completed awareness training all count as controls in their own right, even though none of them are software. - "Security awareness training is just a compliance checkbox." → When implemented well (recurring, reinforced with realistic phishing simulations, tied to measurable behavior change), awareness training measurably reduces successful social-engineering attacks, it is functioning as a control, not merely satisfying an audit requirement.
