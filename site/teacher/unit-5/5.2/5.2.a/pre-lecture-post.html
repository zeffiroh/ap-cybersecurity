5.2.a

Access control models (RBAC, DAC, MAC)

Unit 5 · Securing Applications and Data · 5.2 Linux Permission Controls

Before class today. Every application stores data that should not be readable or modifiable by everyone. An organization's payroll file should be visible to HR managers, not to interns. A classified government document should only reach employees with the correct clearance.

The intuition to hold onto: The frame to carry in: none of DAC, MAC, or RBAC is "the secure one", they're three different answers to "who gets to decide access," and the right answer depends entirely on whether you trust individual owners, need centrally enforced rules that nobody can override, or need something that scales past a handful of people. Resist the urge to rank them by strength; the AP exam is testing whether you can match a scenario to a model, not whether you can recite that MAC is "the most secure." A consumer photo-sharing app that used MAC would be unusable, and a intelligence agency that used DAC would be reckless, same three tools, opposite right answers.

A myth worth busting before we start: "DAC is weak, so only MAC is secure.", Each model fits different contexts. MAC overhead is impractical for consumer software; DAC is appropriate when owners are trusted to manage their own data correctly.

Today's goal: Explain what access control is, distinguish between the three primary access control models (DAC, MAC, RBAC), and select the appropriate model for a given organizational scenario. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
