5.2.c

Credential theft prevention and lateral movement

Unit 5 · Securing Applications and Data · 5.2 Linux Permission Controls

Before class today. Most large breaches do not end where they begin. An attacker rarely gets everything they want from the very first account they compromise, a phished marketing employee's laptop, for instance, usually has no access to the finance database or the domain controller.

The intuition to hold onto: The frame worth walking in with: almost no breach ends where it starts, so the interesting security question is rarely "how did they get in" but "how far did they get once they were in." A phished marketing laptop is a nuisance by itself; it only becomes a catastrophe if it can reach the finance database from there. That reframes least privilege and network segmentation from 5.2.a/b, they're not abstract hygiene, they're literally the walls that determine whether an attacker's first foothold stays a foothold or becomes the whole building.

A myth worth busting before we start: "A strong password alone stops credential theft." → Many lateral-movement techniques replay a stolen hash or ticket without ever needing the plaintext password, MFA and hygiene practices matter more than password strength alone.

Today's goal: Explain how stolen credentials enable an attacker to move laterally across a network, identify the detection signals that indicate lateral movement is happening, and describe the access-control practices (MFA, credential hygiene, network segmentation) that limit its spread.
