The big picture: As the final atom of the final unit, this is a genuinely fitting place to zoom out. Every detection tool this course has built, 5.6.a's log patterns, 5.6.b's DLP alerts, 5.2.c's lateral-movement flags, even 2.4's physical access-log anomalies from Unit 2, produces exactly the same kind of output: an indicator of compromise, a single observable fact that is rarely conclusive by itself. What separates a mature security program from an alert-driven panic is this skill's whole point, collecting multiple IOCs, weighing how strongly each one supports a given attack category, and reasoning to the best-supported classification rather than the first plausible one. Chain of custody matters for the same reason parameterized queries mattered back in 5.5.b: it's a structural guarantee (a hash taken at collection time, a documented access log) rather than a promise to be careful, which is exactly the pattern this whole course keeps returning to, trust the mechanism, not the good intentions. And notice the last thread this skill pulls taut: MITRE ATT&CK's shared vocabulary is what lets the risk-triage instinct from Unit 2's very first atom (name the threat, then reason about it) scale from one analyst's judgment call to an entire organization's, and an entire industry's, shared understanding of what actually happened.

Key terms to lock in:

  • Indicator of compromise (IOC): a single observable artifact (IP, file hash, unusual log entry) suggesting a security incident may be occurring; rarely conclusive on its own.
  • Attack classification: reasoning from a collection of IOCs to the attack category they collectively best support.
  • Chain of custody: a documented record of who collected, accessed, and stored evidence, so its integrity can be trusted (or challenged) later.
  • MITRE ATT&CK: a widely-used framework providing a shared vocabulary for attack techniques, letting teams classify and compare incidents consistently.
  • Hash verification: computing a cryptographic hash of evidence at collection time so any later modification is detectable.

Common misconceptions to watch for:

  • "One suspicious indicator is enough to declare the attack type." → A single IOC is rarely conclusive; classification should weigh the full set of collected evidence.
  • "Chain of custody only matters for evidence going to court." → Internal incident reports and post-mortems also depend on evidence integrity for their conclusions to be trusted.
  • "Classification frameworks are just paperwork." → A shared vocabulary (like MITRE ATT&CK) is what lets different analysts and teams compare and aggregate incidents meaningfully.

Learning objective(s): 5.6.B, 5.6.C
