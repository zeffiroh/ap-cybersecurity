5.6.c, Digital evidence analysis and attack classification

Before class today. Detecting that something happened is only the first half of incident response.

The intuition to hold onto: The habit worth building before this lecture, and really before the rest of your life in this field: an alert is a hypothesis, not a verdict. A burst of failed logins suggests brute-force, but it could just as easily be a broken script retrying a stale password, the analyst's job starts, not ends, when the alert fires, and jumping straight to a conclusion without checking the underlying evidence is the single most common way a real incident gets mishandled: wrong IP blocked, wrong password reset, real attack vector missed entirely.

A myth worth busting before we start: "One suspicious indicator is enough to declare the attack type." → A single IOC is rarely conclusive; classification should weigh the full set of collected evidence.

Today's goal: Explain the chain-of-custody principles that make digital evidence trustworthy, describe the difference between an indicator of compromise (IOC) and a conclusion about attack type, and classify a set of collected evidence into the attack category it most strongly supports.
