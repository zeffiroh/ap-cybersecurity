5.4.a, Public/private key pairs

Before class today. Every secure interaction on the internet, HTTPS, email signing, SSH login, ultimately depends on a single elegant idea: two mathematically linked numbers, one of which you can hand to anyone in the world, and one of which you keep to yourself forever. This is asymmetric cryptography, and the foundation of the whole system is the key pair.

The intuition to hold onto: The bootstrap problem worth sitting with before this lecture: symmetric cryptography (5.3) is strong once both sides share a key, but it never actually explains how two strangers agree on a secret over a network where anyone could be listening, asymmetric cryptography is the answer to that exact gap. The elegant part is that one of your two keys is designed to be handed to literally anyone, posted publicly, shouted across an open channel, and none of that weakens your security at all, because possessing the public key was never the thing protecting you.

A myth worth busting before we start: "If I encrypt with the private key, it is more secure." → Encrypting with the private key does not provide confidentiality; anyone holding the public key can decrypt. Sign-with-private-key is used for authenticity, not secrecy.

Today's goal: Explain how a public/private key pair is generated and used, describe the mathematical relationship between the two keys, and identify which key is used for encryption versus decryption in common asymmetric scenarios. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
