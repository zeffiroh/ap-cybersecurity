5.4.b

RSA algorithm

Unit 5 · Securing Applications and Data · 5.4 Asymmetric Cryptography

Before class today. 5.4.a introduced the key pair, (e, n) public, (d, n) private, both built from two primes p and q. This skill is about the actual algorithm that uses those numbers to scramble and unscramble a message: RSA, named for its inventors Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman. Everything RSA does boils down to one operation, applied twice: modular exponentiation.

The intuition to hold onto: Walk into this lecture ready to do actual arithmetic, RSA is one of the few places in this course where the security concept and the math are the same object, not just a metaphor for it. Everything reduces to modular exponentiation done twice, once with each exponent, and the entire security guarantee rests on one asymmetry: computing m^e mod n is fast in both directions if you know the right exponent, but recovering d from e and n alone requires factoring n, which is the one direction nobody has found a fast way to do at real key sizes. Small numbers like p=3, q=11 make the steps legible; they don't make RSA weak, the operations at 2048 bits are identical, just computationally hopeless to reverse without d.

A myth worth busting before we start: "Encryption and decryption use different algorithms." → They use the exact same operation, modular exponentiation; only the exponent (e vs. d) and which key it comes from differ.

Today's goal: Carry out the RSA encrypt and decrypt operations by hand on small numbers (including deriving the private exponent d), and explain why modular exponentiation with a public exponent is easy to compute but infeasible to reverse without the private key. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
