5.4.d recap, PKI and digital certificates

Nice work today. Here's what to lock in:

  • Recap handout: post-lecture-handout.docx
  • Simulation: SimLab
  • Self-check quiz: due before next class

The big picture: The concept worth keeping longest is that PKI just pushes the trust question up a level, again and again, until it hits a point you agreed to trust out-of-band, a root CA pre-installed in your OS's trust store, vetted by the OS vendor rather than by you personally. Every certificate in the chain is really just 5.4.c's sign/verify operation reused: the issuer hashes the certificate's fields and signs the digest with its own private key, and the next link up verifies it the same way you'd verify any signature. Keep the revocation-versus-expiration distinction sharp for the exam, expiration is scheduled and built in, revocation is an emergency withdrawal of trust issued when the situation changes unexpectedly, most often because a private key was compromised. And notice the full-circle moment this skill represents: it closes out the cryptography arc (5.3-5.4) by tying keys, hashes, and signatures together into the one system, HTTPS, that quietly runs underneath almost every interaction covered earlier in this course, from the login forms of 5.1 to the credential theft of 5.2.

Key terms to lock in: - Digital certificate, a signed document binding a public key to a subject identity, issued and signed by a Certificate Authority. - Certificate Authority (CA), an organization that verifies identities and signs certificates vouching for the binding between a subject and its public key. - Root CA, a CA whose self-signed certificate is pre-installed in an OS/browser trust store and trusted as the ultimate anchor of a chain. - Intermediate CA, a CA whose certificate is signed by a root (or another intermediate) and which signs end-entity (leaf) certificates. - Chain of trust, the sequence of signed certificates from a leaf up through intermediates to a trusted root, each link verified with the next certificate's public key. - Trust store, the curated set of root CA public keys an OS or browser trusts out of the box. - Certificate Revocation List (CRL), a CA-signed, periodically updated list of revoked certificate serial numbers. - OCSP, Online Certificate Status Protocol; a real-time query/response check of one certificate's revocation status against a CA-operated responder.

Common misconceptions -- don't fall for these: - "A certificate proves the website is trustworthy/legitimate in a business sense." → It only proves that a CA verified the binding between the stated subject (usually just a domain name) and the public key presented. It says nothing about the site's content, intent, or safety. - "Any self-signed certificate is inherently insecure." → Self-signed certificates use the same cryptography and can be perfectly secure once distributed and trusted through some other channel (e.g. manually installed on an internal network); the issue is that a random visitor's browser has no independent reason to trust it. - "Revocation and expiration are the same thing." → Expiration is a scheduled, built-in end to a certificate's validity window. Revocation is an unscheduled, active withdrawal of trust before that date, typically because the private key was compromised. - "The whole chain is checked with one signature." → Each certificate in the chain is signed and verified independently, using the public key of the certificate directly above it in the chain.

Learning objective(s): 5.4.B, 5.4.C
