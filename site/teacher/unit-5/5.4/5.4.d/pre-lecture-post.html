PKI and digital certificates

Unit 5 · Securing Applications and Data · 5.4 Asymmetric Cryptography

Before class today. 5.4.a ended with a bootstrap problem it deliberately left unsolved: asymmetric cryptography lets you hand your public key to anyone, but that only helps if the person receiving it can be sure the key really belongs to who it claims.

The intuition to hold onto: The unresolved thread worth carrying in from 5.4.a-c: you now know how to encrypt with someone's public key and verify their signature, but none of that math tells you the public key you received actually belongs to who it claims to. An attacker who swaps in their own key while claiming to be your bank makes every cryptographic guarantee from the last three atoms useless, you'd faithfully encrypt your password for the attacker, or faithfully "verify" a signature they forged with a key you never actually confirmed was your bank's. PKI is not new cryptography; it's the trust infrastructure that makes the cryptography you already learned actually mean something in the real world.

A myth worth busting before we start: "A certificate proves the website is trustworthy/legitimate in a business sense." → It only proves that a CA verified the binding between the stated subject (usually just a domain name) and the public key presented. It says nothing about the site's content, intent, or safety.

Today's goal: Explain what a digital certificate contains and why, describe how a client walks a certificate chain up to a trusted root to decide whether to trust a public key, and identify why and how certificates get revoked before their stated expiration. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
