Data breaches and their impact

Unit 5 · Securing Applications and Data · 5.1 Application Code Vulnerabilities

Nice work today. Here's what to lock in:

  • Recap handout: post-lecture-handout.docx
  • Simulation: SimLab
  • Self-check quiz: due before next class

The big picture: The connective tissue worth noticing is that this skill is the unifying finish line for everything in 5.1: SQL injection, XSS, and BOLA are three different causes, and a data breach is the consequence they all funnel toward, alongside plenty of non-technical causes like a lost laptop or a misconfigured bucket. That's worth sitting with, because it means the attack technique and the resulting harm are separate axes you have to reason about independently, the same breach severity can follow from a highly technical SQLi dump or a simple human mistake, and the response arc (contain, assess, notify, remediate) doesn't change based on how sophisticated the entry point was. Keep the time-to-detect factor especially close, because it's the bridge into 5.6: everything this unit builds toward, logging, DLP, evidence analysis, exists specifically to shrink that number before it becomes the deciding factor in how bad a breach turns out to be.

Key terms to lock in:

  • Data breach: unauthorized access, disclosure, or exfiltration of sensitive/protected data.
  • PII (personally identifiable information): data that can identify a specific individual (name, SSN, address, date of birth).
  • PHI (protected health information): health-related records protected by regulation (e.g., HIPAA in the U.S.).
  • Breach notification: the legal/regulatory requirement to inform affected individuals and authorities within a defined window after a confirmed breach.
  • Time to detect / contain: the elapsed time between a breach starting and it being discovered/stopped; a major driver of total breach impact.

Common misconceptions to watch for:

  • "A breach only matters if passwords were stolen." → Exposure of PII, financial, or health data alone (without passwords) can enable identity theft and fraud and often carries stronger legal notification obligations.
  • "Small breaches don't need a formal response." → Even a small breach of highly sensitive data (e.g., 40 patients' health records) can be more severe than a large breach of low-sensitivity data (e.g., 4 million newsletter emails); severity depends on data type and scale together.
