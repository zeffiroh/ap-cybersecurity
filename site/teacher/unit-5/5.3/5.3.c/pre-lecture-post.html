5.3.c, Integrity and authenticity (HMAC)

Before class today. 5.3.a established that a hash lets you detect whether data changed: store a digest, recompute it later, and compare. That is enough when the only threat is accidental corruption, a bad disk sector, a dropped byte in transit. It is not enough when the threat is an intelligent attacker who can intercept and rewrite the data.

The intuition to hold onto: The gap worth naming before this lecture starts: 5.3.a taught you that a hash detects tampering, but only if the attacker can't also update the hash after they tamper, and a plain hash is public and keyless, so any attacker who can rewrite your message can just as easily recompute a matching digest for their rewritten version. That's not a hash function being broken; it's a hash function doing exactly what it was designed to do, applied in a context where the design assumption (an untampered channel for the digest) silently failed. HMAC exists because "the data matches its digest" and "the data came from someone who knew a secret" are two completely different claims, and only the second one survives an active attacker.

A myth worth busting before we start: "A matching hash proves the message wasn't tampered with." → Only if the attacker could not also recompute the hash after tampering. A plain, keyless hash gives no such guarantee once an attacker can rewrite both the message and its accompanying hash.

Today's goal: Explain why a plain hash cannot prove who sent a message, describe how HMAC combines a secret key with a hash function to provide both integrity and authenticity, and classify a given integrity-check design as secure or vulnerable to tampering. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
