Skill 2.3.a

Physical access controls (locks, badges, biometrics, mantraps)

Instructor notes

Expected Scenario A gaps: no_tailgating_prevention, no_identity_verification. (Badge reader is present so no_key_control and no_electronic_access do not apply. CCTV is present so no_visual_monitoring does not apply. Guard is absent and biometric is absent, so no_identity_verification applies. Mantrap is absent, so no_tailgating_prevention applies.)

Expected Scenario B gaps: no_key_control, no_electronic_access, no_tailgating_prevention. (Guard present covers no_identity_verification. CCTV present covers no_visual_monitoring. No lock and no badge reader trigger no_key_control. No badge reader and no biometric trigger no_electronic_access. No mantrap triggers no_tailgating_prevention.)

Common stumbles: - Students apply no_key_control even when a badge reader is present. Remind them the rule requires BOTH keyed lock and badge reader to be absent. - Students confuse CCTV (detective) with prevention. Use it to reinforce the detective/preventive control distinction. - Students may not realize that a security guard present 24/7 satisfies the identity-verification requirement (the guard checks ID), so no_identity_verification does not apply in Scenario B.

Timing: Steps 1-2 typically take 8-9 minutes for pairs who read carefully. Step 3 usually surfaces the best discussion. If time is short, skip Step 4 and debrief Scenario A only.

Feed-forward to unit project: The gap-detection logic students practice here is exactly the solve() function they implement in problem/. Encourage fast finishers to open problem/starter.py and sketch pseudocode.

Skill 2.3.b

Surveillance systems (CCTV, monitoring)

Instructor notes

  • Timing: row 1 is a clean baseline (no issue) to test whether groups over-flag everything; rows 2 and 5 are meant to be contrasted (row 2 has a camera but narrow coverage, row 5 has no camera at all) so groups articulate the difference between "narrow FOV" and "true blind spot."
  • Row 3 is the intended teaching moment for compounding physical + logical vulnerabilities (low mount height enabling tampering, PLUS default credentials enabling remote takeover), push groups to name both issues, not just one.
  • Row 4 (PTZ blind spot) is the hardest row; if groups struggle, prompt them to reread the atom's "Camera types" section on PTZ cameras before revisiting the row.
  • Feeds forward to 2.4 (detecting physical attacks) and connects to 2.3.b's coding problem, which formalizes after-hours detection and "ghost motion" alerting logic from a camera event log.
Skill 2.3.c

Security guards and layered defense-in-depth

Instructor notes

  • Timing: rows 2 and 5 are the clean multi-category cases; rows 1 and 4 are the intended teaching moment for "redundant layers in one category do not raise depth score."
  • Common stumble: students count total layers instead of distinct categories, especially on row 4 (three CCTV feeds looks like "a lot of security" but is depth score 1). Push them to name the five categories explicitly and cross off which ones are covered.
  • Row 3 (single unlocked door, no controls at all) exists to test whether groups correctly assign a depth score of zero rather than assuming "at least it's a facility, so it must have some score."
  • Feeds the unit project (threat modeling a small network/facility) and the coding problem in problem/, which implements this exact depth-score and recommendation logic as solve(layers).
Skill 2.3.d

Environmental controls (HVAC, fire suppression, power)

Answer key (instructor only)

# Chosen control Justification (sample)
1 Clean-agent (FM-200 or equivalent) Water sprinklers destroy live electronics on contact; a clean agent extinguishes fire without residue or equipment damage.
2 UPS + generator (layered) UPS responds in milliseconds to bridge the outage; generator takes over after 10-30s for extended runtime. Neither alone is sufficient.
3 Hot aisle / cold aisle containment Physical separation stops hot exhaust air from mixing into cold intake air, which is the direct cause of rising inlet temperature.
4 N+1 redundant HVAC units A single HVAC unit is a single point of failure; N+1 means a failure of one unit does not cause a cooling outage.
5 Raised floor + water sensor Raised flooring limits how far shallow water spreads to reach equipment; a floor-level sensor gives early warning before visible flooding.
6 Water sprinkler Office space has no sensitive electronics at floor level in the way a server room does; standard sprinklers are appropriate and cheaper.
7 Managed PDU remote cycle A managed PDU lets staff remotely power-cycle a single outlet without a technician entering a restricted room.

Instructor notes

  • Timing: rows 1, 6, and 7 are unambiguous single-answer picks; row 2 and row 4 are the intended teaching moments for layered defense and the N+1 redundancy principle.
  • Common stumble: students pick "UPS only" for row 2 because it responds fastest, missing that UPS battery runtime is limited and a generator is required for anything beyond a few minutes.
  • Row 6 exists to test whether groups over-apply clean-agent suppression everywhere out of caution; a mature risk analysis matches the control to the actual hazard and asset value, since clean-agent systems are far more expensive than sprinklers.
  • Feeds forward into 2.4 (detecting physical attacks) and complements 2.2.b (environmental vulnerabilities), which covers identifying these hazards rather than mitigating them.
