Unit 2 · Securing Spaces · 2.2 Physical Security Breaches

Before class today. Unauthorized physical access (2.2.a) gets an attacker into a building. Once inside, or once a device leaves the building on an employee's backpack, the attacker's goal shifts to the device itself. Device theft is the straightforward case: a laptop, phone, external drive, or backup tape is physically taken.

The intuition to hold onto: The distinction to hold onto: a stolen laptop is a loss you already know about; a tampered laptop is a loss you don't know has happened yet. Theft interrupts your day. Tampering doesn't interrupt anything, it just quietly turns a device you trust into one you shouldn't, while it keeps behaving normally in every way you'd notice. That asymmetry is why tamper-evident seals and inventory audits matter even when nothing looks wrong: the whole point of a good tampering attack is that nothing looks wrong.

A myth worth busting before we start: "If a laptop is password-protected, a thief can't get the data." → A login password protects the operating system's normal boot path, but an attacker with physical possession can often boot from external media and read the disk directly unless the disk itself is encrypted.

Today's goal: Distinguish device theft from device tampering, identify the attack techniques each enables (offline password reset, disk imaging, hardware implants, evil-maid attacks), and evaluate whether a given device's configuration (encryption, boot protections, asset tracking) would contain the damage if the device were stolen or tampered with.
