Device theft and tampering

Unit 2 · Securing Spaces · 2.2 Physical Security Breaches

Nice work today. Here's what to lock in:

  • Recap handout: post-lecture-handout.docx
  • Simulation: SimLab
  • Self-check quiz: due before next class

The big picture: The evil-maid attack is worth remembering not because you'll personally be targeted by one, but because it's the cleanest illustration in this unit of a principle that recurs constantly in security: a control can be cryptographically perfect and still fail because of when it's applied. Full-disk encryption is genuinely strong, but only from the moment it's engaged. An attacker who gets brief unsupervised access before you've entered your password that session isn't breaking the encryption at all; they're intercepting the one narrow window where the protection hasn't started yet. That's a lesson worth generalizing: don't just ask "is this control strong," ask "is there a gap in time or scope where it isn't running."

Key terms to lock in:

  • Device theft: the physical removal of a device (laptop, phone, drive, tape) from its authorized location or owner.
  • Device tampering: a covert, typically brief physical alteration of a device (hardware, firmware, or peripheral) intended to compromise it without the owner's knowledge.
  • Evil-maid attack: a tampering attack in which an attacker with brief unsupervised access to a device (e.g., in a hotel room) modifies it, often to capture credentials on next use, then restores its appearance and leaves.
  • Full-disk encryption: encryption of an entire storage volume so its contents are unreadable without the decryption key, even if the physical drive is removed.
  • Tamper-evident seal: a sticker or mechanism that visibly and irreversibly shows evidence (tearing, a "VOID" pattern, discoloration) if a device enclosure has been opened.
  • Remote wipe: a mobile-device-management capability that erases a device's data remotely, typically once it is reported lost or stolen and reconnects to a network.

Common misconceptions to watch for:

  • "If a laptop is password-protected, a thief can't get the data." → A login password protects the operating system's normal boot path, but an attacker with physical possession can often boot from external media and read the disk directly unless the disk itself is encrypted.
  • "Tampering only matters for spy-movie scenarios." → Evil-maid attacks and rogue USB implants are documented techniques used against real targets (executives, journalists, researchers) whenever a device is left briefly unattended in a hotel, shared office, or conference venue.
  • "A locked laptop bag is enough physical security." → A cable lock or bag deters casual, opportunistic theft but does nothing against a prepared attacker with cutting tools, and does nothing at all against tampering, which does not require removing the device from the room.

Learning objective(s): 2.2.B, 2.2.C
