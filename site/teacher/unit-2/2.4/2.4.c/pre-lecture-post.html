2.4.c, Motion detectors and sensor-based detection

Before class today. A locked door stops someone from walking in cleanly. A camera records what happens if they don't. But neither a lock nor a camera actively notices an intruder who is already inside a room after hours, a badge reader only fires when a badge is presented, and nobody is watching every camera feed in real time.

The intuition to hold onto: The framing worth keeping: a motion sensor answers exactly one narrow question, did something change in this zone, and that narrowness is a feature, not a limitation. It doesn't know who moved, why, or whether they belong there; it hands a single yes/no signal to the alarm system covered in the last atom for a decision-maker (human or rule engine) to interpret with context the sensor itself doesn't have. Once you see the sensor as a narrow, fast tripwire rather than a smart observer, PIR's biggest quirk, that a person standing perfectly still can go undetected, stops being a flaw and starts being exactly what "detects change" should mean.

A myth worth busting before we start: "A motion sensor sees everything in the room." → Motion sensors have a limited detection cone and blind spots just like cameras; furniture, doorways, and mounting angle can all hide an intruder's movement from a single sensor.

Today's goal: Explain how PIR, microwave, and dual-technology motion sensors detect intrusions, evaluate sensor placement and sensitivity tradeoffs, and classify a given sensor trigger event as a true detection or a false positive. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
