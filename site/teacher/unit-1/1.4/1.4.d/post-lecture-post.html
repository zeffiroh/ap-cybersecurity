The big picture: The distinction between availability attacks (break it for everyone) and integrity/backdoor attacks (break it only in one specific, attacker-chosen way, while looking perfectly healthy everywhere else) is the part worth remembering longest, because backdoor attacks are unsettling precisely for how invisible they stay under normal evaluation. A model can pass every accuracy benchmark you throw at it and still contain a landmine that only detonates on a trigger the evaluator never thought to test. That's a sobering preview of why AI systems need the same kind of adversarial, not just functional, testing you'll apply to code throughout this course.

Key terms to lock in: - Data poisoning, intentionally corrupting training data to influence a model's behavior - Backdoor attack, embedding a trigger pattern in training data so the model misclassifies inputs containing that trigger - Label/content mismatch, a sample whose label contradicts the observable content - Outlier marker, a sample statistically anomalous relative to the rest of the dataset - Training integrity, the guarantee that training data has not been tampered with

Common misconceptions -- don't fall for these: - "Poisoning requires access to the model." → Poisoning targets the training data, which may be collected from public sources, crowd-sourced platforms, or user submissions, no model access needed. - "A small number of poisoned samples cannot change a large model." → Targeted backdoors have been demonstrated with less than 1% poisoning rate; availability attacks can succeed with slightly higher rates but still well under 10%.
