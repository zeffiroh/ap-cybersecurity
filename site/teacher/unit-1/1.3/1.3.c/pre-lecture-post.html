Jamming / denial-of-service attack

Unit 1 · Introduction to Security · 1.3 Public Network Sniffing

Before class today. Every Wi-Fi access point communicates by broadcasting radio waves on a shared spectrum. The 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands used by Wi-Fi are unlicensed, meaning any device is legally allowed to transmit on them, subject to power limits set by the FCC or equivalent regulator.

The intuition to hold onto: Jamming is the one attack in this unit that isn't really about deception at all, there's no fake network, no stolen credential, nothing to trick you into trusting. It's brute physical denial: flood the shared medium with noise until CSMA/CA politeness forces every legitimate device to back off. The intuition worth keeping is that Wi-Fi's cooperative design, devices listening before they transmit so they don't collide, is precisely the mechanism a jammer weaponizes. The same courtesy that makes Wi-Fi work efficiently among cooperating devices makes it exploitable by one that refuses to cooperate.

A myth worth busting before we start: "High noise always means jamming." → Transient interference from household devices is common on 2.4 GHz. A jamming attack is characterized by sustained, not momentary, elevated noise across several consecutive samples.

Today's goal: Explain how radio-frequency jamming causes a wireless denial-of-service, describe what a sustained jamming event looks like in signal-strength monitoring data, and implement a detector that flags sustained interference in a sample series. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
