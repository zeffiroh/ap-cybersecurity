Topic 1.2 Activity Answer Keys

Suspicious Website Logins · instructor-only · 3 entries

Skill 1.2.a

Password-attack signs (failed attempts, unusual times, unknown devices)

Instructor notes

  • Expected signals in the log excerpt: failure spike (6 in 5s), unusual time (02:13), unknown device (192.168.1.201 not in known list).
  • Common stumble: students fixate on the raw failure count and miss the time-of-day anomaly. Ask "What time is it in the log? Is that normal?"
  • Common stumble 2: students think the lockout prevents compromise. Point out the success event at 02:13:49, the attack succeeded before any lockout would have fired.
  • Feeds the unit scenario (1.2 stage: investigate the router admin login) and the coding problem.
Skill 1.2.b

Weak authentication patterns; adversary dictionary construction

Instructor notes

  • Expected stumble: students think P@ssword!2024 is strong because it has symbols. Point out that "password" is in the COMMON_WORDS list, and the checker flags in_dictionary regardless of the mangling appended, the base word match is enough.
  • Rule 4 (sequential_or_repeated) is often surprising: HelloWorld111 looks strong but fails because 111 is three identical characters. Use this to discuss entropy vs. apparent complexity.
  • Common Q: "Does the 12-character minimum mean passphrases are better?" Yes, a four-word random passphrase is typically 20+ characters, passes all rules, and is more memorable.
  • Feeds the unit project (building an authentication policy) and scenario stage 2 (adversary attempts credential stuffing with a dictionary attack against the school Wi-Fi portal).
Skill 1.2.c

Strengthening authentication: length/complexity, randomness, MFA

Instructor notes

  • The sim's TOTP uses a synthetic (non-SHA1) HMAC for offline reproducibility. The algorithm structure is identical to RFC 6238; only the underlying hash is replaced with an LCG for teaching. Students implementing the real solution in problem/ use Python's hmac + hashlib.
  • Common stumble: students confuse the time step (an integer) with the timestamp. Clarify: two timestamps 5 seconds apart in the same 30-second window share the same step number and would produce the same code.
  • Common Q: "Can't the attacker just try all million 6-digit codes?" In an online attack with rate limiting (5 attempts per minute), exhausting 1,000,000 codes would take 200,000 minutes. TOTP codes also change every 30 seconds, resetting the window. Rate limiting + TOTP together make online brute-force impractical.
  • Feeds the unit project (authentication policy design) and scenario stage 3 (implementing MFA for the school Wi-Fi portal to prevent the credential-stuffing attack from 1.2.b).
