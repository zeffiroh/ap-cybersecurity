Password-attack signs (failed attempts, unusual times, unknown devices)

Unit 1 · Introduction to Security · 1.2 Suspicious Website Logins

Before class today. When an attacker wants access to an account they do not own, the simplest path is to guess the password. Modern attackers do not guess slowly and manually, they use automated tools that can submit hundreds or thousands of login attempts per minute. The digital fingerprints those attempts leave in authentication logs are predictable, and recognizing them is one of the most fundamental skills in defensive security.

The intuition to hold onto: The professor's shortcut for this whole atom: a single anomaly is a coincidence; a stack of anomalies is an incident. Five failed logins alone might just be someone who forgot their password twice and fat-fingered it three more times. Five failed logins from a device you've never seen, at 3 a.m., ending in a success? Each individual fact is explainable on its own, it's the conjunction that collapses the space of innocent explanations. This is the same correlation principle real SIEM systems are built around, and you'll see it again every time this course asks you to combine weak signals into a strong verdict.

A myth worth busting before we start: "A few failed logins are not a big deal." → A few in an hour is noise. Five in sixty seconds is a strong indicator of automation; the rate, not the raw count, is what matters.

Today's goal: Read an authentication log, identify the three key signals of a password attack (repeated failed attempts, logins at anomalous times, and access from unknown devices), and write a rule that flags brute-force attempts automatically. Handout: pre-lecture-handout.docx.
