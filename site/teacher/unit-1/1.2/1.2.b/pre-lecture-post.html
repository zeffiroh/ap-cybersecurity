1.2.b, Weak authentication patterns; adversary dictionary construction

Before class today. A password is only as strong as the assumptions an attacker has to defeat in order to guess it. When an attacker sits down to brute-force an account, they do not usually start at aaaaaaa and try every possible combination, that is computationally expensive when the password space is large. Instead, they start with what works: a dictionary.

The intuition to hold onto: Here's the intuition that makes the whole math of password cracking click: an attacker is not trying to search the entire universe of possible passwords, they're trying to search the tiny universe of passwords a human would actually pick. Humans are bad random-number generators, we gravitate toward names, dates, keyboard patterns, and "clever" substitutions that feel unpredictable to us but are the very first thing every mangling rule tries. The dictionary attack isn't a brute-force fallback; for most real accounts, it's the primary attack, because it exploits human psychology, not computational limits.

A myth worth busting before we start: "Adding a number and symbol makes a password secure." → If the base word is in the dictionary, mangling rules will find the variant. Entropy comes from unpredictability and length.
