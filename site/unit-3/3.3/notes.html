I. From Castle-and-Moat to Segmented Networks

For much of the history of enterprise networking, defenders operated under a design philosophy commonly referred to as the castle-and-moat modelCastle-and-Moat ModelAn older security philosophy where a network is treated like a medieval fortress, build a strong outer wall (firewall) and assume everything inside is trustworthy. The problem: once an attacker breaches the wall, or an insider goes rogue, they can roam freely because there are no internal barriers. Also called the "perimeter security model." Formally deprecated in favor of Zero Trust by NIST SP 800-207 (2020).. Under this paradigm, an organization's perimeterNetwork PerimeterThe logical boundary between an organization's internal, trusted network and the untrusted external internet, typically enforced by a firewall or gateway router. Classical perimeter security assumes anything "inside" the boundary is inherently safe, an assumption invalidated by cloud computing, remote work, and supply-chain compromise., the firewall, the VPN concentrator, the border router, was treated as the primary and often sole line of defense. Once a device or user authenticated at the perimeter, they were implicitly trusted to communicate freely with nearly every other host on the internal network. This flat, unsegmented topology is architecturally efficient to deploy but catastrophically insecure: a single compromised laptop, a single phished credential, or a single unpatched IoT thermostat becomes a springboard from which an attacker can reach financial databases, domain controllers, or industrial control systems with no additional barriers in the way.

Network segmentationNetwork SegmentationThe practice of splitting a computer network into smaller, isolated sub-networks (segments) so that a compromise in one part cannot automatically spread to another. Analogous to a submarine's watertight compartments, a hull breach in one compartment does not sink the whole ship. Achieved via VLANs, subnetting, firewalls, and increasingly software-defined micro-segmentation policies enforced at the workload level. is the architectural countermeasure to the flat-network problem. Rather than relying on a single trust boundary, segmentation subdivides a network into discrete zones, separated by routers, firewalls, or logical VLAN boundaries, each governed by its own access control policy. The guiding academic principle is defense in depthDefense in DepthA layered security strategy in which multiple, independent controls are stacked so that if one fails, others still stand between the attacker and the target asset. Borrowed directly from military doctrine, where an invading force must fight through successive lines of defense rather than a single wall. NIST SP 800-53 treats defense in depth as a foundational control-selection principle rather than a single control itself.: no single control, however strong, should be the only thing standing between an attacker and a crown-jewel asset.

Segmentation also directly operationalizes the security concept of blast radiusBlast RadiusA borrowed demolition/military term describing the scope of damage a single incident can cause. In networking, the blast radius of a compromised host is the set of other systems it can directly reach and attack. Segmentation's entire purpose is to shrink this radius. Sometimes formalized as the "compromise scope" in incident response tabletop exercises; a small blast radius drastically reduces mean-time-to-containment (MTTC). reduction. Every additional segment boundary an attacker must cross after initial compromise imposes cost: additional reconnaissance, additional credential theft, additional exploitation of a distinct trust boundary, and, critically, additional opportunities for defenders to detect anomalous east-west trafficEast-West TrafficNetwork traffic that flows laterally between hosts inside a data center or internal network, as opposed to "north-south" traffic that flows in and out of the network to the internet. Historically under-monitored because perimeter-focused security tooling watches the border, not internal host-to-host chatter. Modern data center architectures deploy internal firewalls and network detection and response (NDR) sensors specifically to inspect east-west flows for lateral movement. before the objective (data exfiltration, ransomware detonation, OT sabotage) is achieved.

Watertight compartments, and why one open hatch sinks the ship Think of an unsegmented corporate network as a submarine with no internal bulkheads. One hull breach anywhere and the entire vessel floods and sinks. A properly segmented network is a submarine with sealed watertight compartments, a torpedo strike floods one compartment, the crew seals the hatch, and the ship stays afloat. Every VLAN boundary, every firewall rule between zones, every micro-segmentation policy is a watertight door. The goal of this chapter is to teach you how those doors are actually built at the packet level, and why leaving even one door open (as at Target in 2013) can flood the whole ship.

Historically, segmentation was implemented almost entirely at Layer 2 and Layer 3 using VLANsVLAN, Virtual Local Area NetworkA technique that lets a single physical switch behave as if it were several separate switches, by tagging traffic so that devices in different VLANs cannot see each other's broadcast traffic even though they share the same physical wiring. Standardized in IEEE 802.1Q (1998, revised 2011, 2014, 2018). VLANs are identified by a 12-bit VLAN ID (0–4095). and subnetting, with a firewall or Layer 3 switch mediating inter-VLAN traffic. This model assumed that network location was a reasonably reliable proxy for trust, "if you're on the finance VLAN, you must be a finance workstation." Modern academic and industry consensus, formalized by NIST Special Publication 800-207NIST SP 800-207, Zero Trust ArchitectureThe authoritative United States government publication (August 2020) defining Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA): a security model that assumes breach and verifies every request as though it originates from an untrusted network, regardless of physical or network location. Rose, S., Borchert, O., Mitchell, S., Connelly, S. (2020). NIST SP 800-207. Mandated for U.S. federal agencies under OMB Memorandum M-22-09 (2022)., rejects that assumption entirely: network location is not identity, and identity must be continuously verified regardless of which subnet a device happens to sit on. This chapter treats VLAN-based segmentation and Zero Trust micro-segmentation as complementary layers of the same underlying discipline, one operating at the wire/frame level, the other at the identity/policy level.

The economic argument for segmentation is equally important academically. PCI-DSSPCI-DSS, Payment Card Industry Data Security StandardA contractual security standard maintained by the PCI Security Standards Council (founded by Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Discover, and JCB) that any organization handling credit card data must follow. Segmentation is explicitly recognized as a compliance-scope-reduction technique. PCI-DSS v4.0 (2022), Requirement 1 and associated Segmentation guidance documents. Properly segmented cardholder data environments (CDE) reduce audit scope and cost. compliance guidance explicitly recognizes that isolating the cardholder data environment (CDE)Cardholder Data Environment (CDE)The subset of an organization's network that stores, processes, or transmits credit card data, plus any systems connected to it. PCI-DSS auditors only need to fully assess the CDE if it is properly segmented from the rest of the network, otherwise the entire flat network is in scope. PCI Security Standards Council, "Guidance for PCI DSS Scoping and Segmentation" (2017 rev.). into its own segment dramatically reduces the number of systems subject to the full battery of compliance controls, directly lowering audit cost and residual risk. This is a rare case where a purely technical control (a VLAN boundary or firewall rule) produces a measurable financial and regulatory benefit.

II. VLANs, 802.1Q Tagging, and Inter-Segment Routing

The dominant Layer 2 segmentation mechanism is the VLAN, standardized by IEEE 802.1QIEEE 802.1QThe IEEE standard that defines VLAN tagging on Ethernet networks. It inserts a 4-byte tag field into the Ethernet frame between the source MAC address and the EtherType/length field, allowing a single physical link to carry traffic for multiple logical VLANs simultaneously. First published 1998; current baseline is IEEE 802.1Q-2022. Also defines the Generic Attribute Registration Protocol (GARP) precursor to MVRP.. Each Ethernet frame traversing a trunk portTrunk PortA switch port configured to carry traffic for multiple VLANs simultaneously, typically used for switch-to-switch or switch-to-router links. Each frame is tagged with its VLAN ID so the receiving device knows which VLAN it belongs to. Cisco IOS: configured with "switchport mode trunk". The 802.1Q standard also defines a "native VLAN" that is carried untagged on the trunk, a frequent misconfiguration source. carries an inserted 32-bit tag field consisting of a 16-bit Tag Protocol Identifier (TPID)Tag Protocol Identifier (TPID)A fixed 16-bit value (0x8100) inserted into an 802.1Q-tagged Ethernet frame that signals to the receiving NIC or switch "this frame contains an 802.1Q tag, not a normal EtherType field." 0x8100 is reserved specifically for 802.1Q; 0x88A8 is used for 802.1ad Q-in-Q double tagging. set to 0x8100, a 3-bit Priority Code Point (PCP)Priority Code Point (PCP)A 3-bit field in the 802.1Q tag used for Class of Service (CoS), traffic prioritization such as marking VoIP frames as higher priority than bulk file transfers. Defined by IEEE 802.1p, now folded into 802.1Q. Values 0–7, with 7 typically reserved for network control traffic., a 1-bit Drop Eligible Indicator (DEI)Drop Eligible Indicator (DEI)A single bit signaling that a frame is eligible to be dropped first under network congestion, used for traffic engineering. Formerly called the Canonical Format Indicator (CFI) bit in older 802.1Q revisions. Renamed DEI in IEEE 802.1Q-2011., and a 12-bit VLAN Identifier (VID)VLAN Identifier (VID)A 12-bit field that identifies which of the 4,096 possible VLANs (0–4095) a frame belongs to. VID 0 and 4095 are reserved; VID 1 is typically the default/native VLAN on most switch platforms. 4,094 usable VLAN IDs (1–4094) per 802.1Q; large multi-tenant environments needing more use 802.1ad Q-in-Q or VXLAN (24-bit VNI, 16 million segments)., which determines VLAN membership. Ports configured as access portsAccess PortA switch port assigned to exactly one VLAN, used to connect end-user devices (workstations, printers, phones). Frames are untagged on the wire between the switch and the device; the switch internally associates the port with its configured VLAN. Cisco IOS: configured with "switchport mode access" and "switchport access vlan <id>". transmit and receive untagged frames, the switch silently applies the VLAN membership internally, while trunk ports carry tagged frames for many VLANs across a single physical link, most commonly between switches or between a switch and a router performing inter-VLAN routingInter-VLAN RoutingThe process of forwarding traffic between different VLANs, which by definition are separate broadcast domains and therefore separate IP subnets. Requires a Layer 3 device, either a router with sub-interfaces ("router-on-a-stick") or a Layer 3 switch with Switched Virtual Interfaces (SVIs). Router-on-a-stick uses one physical interface split into 802.1Q sub-interfaces; enterprise designs increasingly prefer Layer 3 switches for lower latency..

Switch(config)# vlan 10

Switch(config-if)# switchport trunk native vlan 99

Note the deliberate hardening choices in the example above: switchport nonegotiate disables Dynamic Trunking Protocol (DTP)Dynamic Trunking Protocol (DTP)A Cisco proprietary protocol that automatically negotiates whether a link should become a trunk port. Convenient for administrators, but dangerous, an attacker plugging in a rogue device can send DTP frames to trick a switch into forming a trunk with them, gaining access to every VLAN on that trunk. DTP is enabled by default on many Cisco switch models ("dynamic auto" / "dynamic desirable"). Disabling it is a standard hardening baseline in CIS Cisco benchmarks., and the native VLAN is deliberately reassigned to an unused VLAN (99) rather than the default VLAN 1. Both choices directly defend against VLAN hoppingVLAN HoppingAn attack technique that allows a host on one VLAN to send or receive traffic on a different VLAN it should not have access to, bypassing the isolation VLANs are supposed to provide. The two primary methods are switch spoofing and double tagging. Documented extensively in Cisco's own security hardening guides; classified under MITRE ATT&CK as a network-boundary-bypass technique used post-initial-access..

The first VLAN hopping technique, switch spoofingSwitch SpoofingA VLAN hopping attack where a malicious host sends DTP negotiation frames to convince a switch it is another switch, causing the switch to automatically form a trunk link with the attacker's device. Once trunked, the attacker's device receives tagged traffic from every VLAN carried on that trunk. Mitigated entirely by disabling DTP auto-negotiation on all access-facing ports ("switchport nonegotiate")., exploits ports left in DTP auto-negotiation mode: an attacker's NIC or a small embedded device (e.g., a Raspberry Pi with the right software) sends DTP frames claiming to be a switch, and a misconfigured access port dutifully becomes a trunk, handing the attacker tagged access to every VLAN permitted on that trunk. The second and more insidious technique, double taggingDouble Tagging (Double Encapsulation) AttackA VLAN hopping attack that only works when an attacker's access port shares the same VLAN as the trunk's native VLAN. The attacker crafts a frame with two stacked 802.1Q tags: the first switch strips the outer tag (matching the native VLAN, so it is not re-tagged) and forwards the frame with its inner tag still present, causing a downstream switch to deliver the frame to the wrong (victim) VLAN. This is a one-way attack, no return traffic path exists. Requires both the attacker port to be on the native VLAN and the trunk to carry the native VLAN untagged, hence the CIS hardening recommendation to explicitly change the native VLAN away from VLAN 1 and never place hosts on it., exploits the fact that native VLAN traffic on a trunk is carried untagged: an attacker on the native VLAN crafts a frame with two nested 802.1Q headers, the outer tag matching the native VLAN and the inner tag matching a victim VLAN. The first switch strips the outer (native) tag as expected and forwards the frame along the trunk still carrying the inner tag; a second switch downstream reads that inner tag as legitimate and delivers the frame into the victim VLAN. This attack is unidirectional, the attacker can inject frames into a foreign VLAN but cannot easily receive a return path, yet it is sufficient to inject spoofed traffic, trigger denial-of-service conditions, or in some scenarios exfiltrate broadcast/multicast data.

The native VLAN is the double-tagging attack’s prerequisite Nearly every VLAN hopping mitigation checklist boils down to two rules: (1) disable DTP negotiation on every access port with switchport nonegotiate, and (2) never leave the native VLAN as the factory-default VLAN 1, and never place production hosts on the native VLAN at all. VLAN 1 on most switch platforms is also the default VLAN for management traffic, CDP/LLDP, and STP BPDUs, leaving it as the native VLAN combines the double-tagging weakness with an unnecessarily privileged broadcast domain. A scenario in which an attacker sends traffic into the finance VLAN without holding a port assigned to it is double tagging, and the fix is to change the native VLAN away from VLAN 1 and place no host on it.

VLANs divide a network between broadcast domains, but a great deal of unwanted reachability lives within one. Every device on a VLAN can, by default, address every other device on it directly at Layer 2, which is exactly what an intruder on a guest or server VLAN exploits to move sideways. Private VLANsPrivate VLAN (PVLAN)An 802.1Q refinement that subdivides one VLAN and its single IP subnet into a primary VLAN and secondary isolated or community VLANs. Isolated ports can reach only the promiscuous port (the gateway or firewall) and not one another; community ports can reach their own community and the promiscuous port. Standardised in RFC 5517 as an informational description of the widely deployed switch feature. address this without spending additional subnets: the VLAN is split into a primary and one or more secondary domains, so that isolated ports can reach the gateway but not each other, and community ports can reach their community and the gateway but not other communities. A hotel or dormitory guest network is the canonical use, every room reaches the internet through the promiscuous uplink and no room can reach the room next door, and the same pattern isolates servers in a DMZ that have no legitimate reason to talk among themselves. Private VLANs are described in RFC 5517; note that they are a containment control, not an authentication one, and can be undone by a device that ignores the subnet and forges Layer 2 frames unless port security is also enforced.

Beyond VLAN mechanics, the modern segmentation discipline is increasingly defined by Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA)Zero Trust ArchitectureA security model built on the principle "never trust, always verify", no user, device, or network segment is implicitly trusted, even if it is inside the traditional perimeter. Every access request is evaluated on a per-session basis using identity, device posture, and context. NIST SP 800-207 defines the formal architecture: a Policy Decision Point (PDP) and Policy Enforcement Point (PEP) mediate every resource access request. as codified in NIST SP 800-207. The publication enumerates seven foundational tenets, among the most exam-relevant being: (1) all data sources and computing services are considered resources; (2) all communication is secured regardless of network location; (3) access to individual enterprise resources is granted on a per-session basis; (4) access is determined by dynamic policy, including the observable state of client identity, application, and the requesting asset, and may include behavioral attributes; and (5) the enterprise monitors and measures the integrity and security posture of all owned and associated assets. Zero Trust operationalizes segmentation at a far finer grain than VLANs ever could through micro-segmentationMicro-SegmentationApplying granular, identity- and workload-based security policy at the level of individual hosts, containers, or even single application processes, rather than at the level of entire VLANs or subnets. Enforced by software-defined controls (e.g., hypervisor-level firewalls, service mesh sidecars) rather than physical switch configuration. Common implementations: VMware NSX, Illumio, Cisco Tetration/Secure Workload, Kubernetes NetworkPolicy objects, and cloud-native security groups., in which every workload, down to an individual container or virtual machine, carries its own explicit allow-list of permitted communication partners, enforced independent of IP address or VLAN membership by a Policy Enforcement Point (PEP)Policy Enforcement Point (PEP)The component in a Zero Trust architecture that actually grants, denies, or revokes access to a resource, based on a decision handed down by the Policy Decision Point. Conceptually the "gatekeeper" that sits directly in front of every protected resource. NIST SP 800-207 §3.3. In practice implemented as a reverse proxy, software-defined perimeter gateway, or host-based firewall agent. acting on instructions from a central Policy Decision Point (PDP)Policy Decision Point (PDP)The centralized "brain" of a Zero Trust architecture that evaluates an access request against policy, identity signals, and device posture, then instructs the PEP to permit or deny it. Composed of a Policy Engine (which computes the trust decision) and a Policy Administrator (which executes it). NIST SP 800-207 §3.2, sometimes implemented via products like Google BeyondCorp, Zscaler Private Access, or Cloudflare Access..

Isolation strategy also encompasses coarser, purpose-built zones. A demilitarized zone (DMZ)DMZ, Demilitarized ZoneA network segment that sits between the untrusted internet and the trusted internal network, hosting public-facing services (web servers, mail relays, DNS) so that if one of those services is compromised, the attacker still cannot directly reach the internal network without crossing another firewall boundary. Classic "three-legged firewall" or dual-firewall DMZ designs are described in NIST SP 800-41 Rev. 1, Guidelines on Firewalls and Firewall Policy. isolates externally reachable services behind two firewall boundaries (or a single firewall with three interfaces) so a compromised web server cannot pivot directly into internal file shares or domain controllers. In operational technology (OT) and industrial control system (ICS) environments, air-gappingAir-GappingA physical isolation strategy in which a critical system or network has no direct network connection whatsoever to any other network, especially the internet, the only "gap" data can cross is physically carried across (historically via removable media), not routed. True air-gaps are increasingly rare in modern OT environments due to remote monitoring needs; the Stuxnet worm (2010) famously crossed an air gap via infected USB drives, illustrating that air-gapping reduces but does not eliminate risk. represents the most extreme form of segmentation, used to protect safety-critical systems such as programmable logic controllers (PLCs) and supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems where even the delay-tolerant risk of a firewall misconfiguration is unacceptable. The Purdue Enterprise Reference ArchitecturePurdue Model (Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture)A widely adopted reference model for segmenting industrial control networks into hierarchical levels, from Level 0 (physical process/sensors) up through Level 5 (enterprise IT), with firewalled zones between each level to prevent IT-side compromises from reaching OT-side physical processes. Originally developed at Purdue University in the 1990s; formalized in ISA-95 and referenced heavily in ICS-CERT and CISA segmentation guidance. formalizes this by defining discrete levels (Level 0 physical process, Level 1 basic control, Level 2 supervisory control, up through Level 4/5 enterprise IT) with mandatory firewalled boundaries between IT and OT zones, a model whose absence, as discussed below, directly enabled the precautionary shutdown during the Colonial Pipeline incident.

III. Case Studies: Target and Colonial Pipeline

Case Study 3.3.1: The 2013 Target Corporation Breach

In November–December 2013, attackers exfiltrated approximately 40 million credit and debit card records and 70 million customer records from Target Corporation, one of the largest retail breaches on record at the time. The initial initial access vectorInitial Access VectorThe specific technique or entry point an attacker uses to first gain a foothold inside a target environment. Classified under MITRE ATT&CK Tactic TA0001. Common initial access vectors include phishing, exposed remote services, and, as in the Target case, trusted third-party vendor connections. was not a direct attack on Target's core systems at all. Attackers first compromised Fazio Mechanical ServicesFazio Mechanical ServicesA third-party HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) contractor granted remote network access to Target's systems for billing, project management, and contract submission, access that, due to insufficient network segmentation, ultimately provided a pathway into Target's point-of-sale environment. Documented in the U.S. Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation report, "A 'Kill Chain' Analysis of the 2013 Target Data Breach" (March 2014)., a small HVAC vendor with legitimate remote-access credentials to Target's vendor portal for billing and project management purposes, via a phishing email that delivered the CitadelCitadel MalwareA banking trojan derived from the leaked Zeus malware source code, used in the Target breach to steal Fazio Mechanical's vendor-portal credentials via keylogging. Citadel was widely distributed on underground forums beginning in 2011; it is a variant descendant of the Zeus banking trojan family. credential-stealing trojan. With those stolen credentials, the attackers accessed Target's externally facing vendor portal, a system that, according to the subsequent U.S. Senate Commerce Committee investigation, was not adequately segmented from Target's internal corporate network.

From that vendor-portal foothold, the attackers moved laterally through Target's internal network, traversing systems that should have been separated by distinct trust zones, and ultimately reached the point-of-sale (POS)Point-of-Sale (POS) SystemThe hardware and software that processes in-store credit and debit card transactions at checkout registers. In the Target breach, attackers deployed memory-scraping malware directly onto POS terminals to capture card track data before it was encrypted. Used in both the Target (2013) and later Home Depot (2014) breaches.) onto thousands of registers to harvest card track data directly from memory before encryption occurred. Investigators concluded that had Target's network enforced strict segmentation, isolating the vendor-facing billing systems from the cardholder data environment, consistent with PCI-DSS segmentation guidance, the HVAC vendor compromise would have been contained to a low-value corporate segment and never reached the POS estate at all. The breach ultimately cost Target over $200 million in direct settlement and remediation costs and remains one of the canonical case studies for why third-party vendor access must be segmented independently of production payment systems.

The failure at Target was architectural, not the air conditioning The Target breach is frequently misremembered as "hackers broke in through the air conditioning", which is not quite right. The HVAC vendor's system was never physically connected to POS registers; the failure was that a vendor billing portal and the cardholder data environment lived on the same flat, poorly segmented internal network, so a credential theft anywhere on that network became a credential theft everywhere on that network. The lesson is architectural, not physical: any third party with any remote access, no matter how unrelated their business function seems to your core assets, must be segmented as though they are a hostile network, because to your CDE, they effectively are one until proven otherwise.

Case Study 3.3.2: The 2021 Colonial Pipeline Ransomware Attack

In May 2021, Colonial Pipeline, operator of the largest refined petroleum pipeline system in the United States, supplying roughly 45% of fuel consumed on the East Coast, suffered a ransomware attack attributed to the DarkSideDarkSideA ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) criminal group, believed to be Russian-speaking and Russia-based, that developed and leased out the ransomware strain used against Colonial Pipeline. DarkSide operated under an affiliate model, splitting ransom proceeds with the affiliates who conducted individual attacks. The U.S. Department of the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) sanctioned entities associated with DarkSide in the aftermath of the incident. ransomware-as-a-service group. The intrusion vector was a single compromised VPNVPN (in this context)Colonial Pipeline's legacy VPN account, which lacked multi-factor authentication and used a password that had been reused and previously exposed in an unrelated data breach, allowing DarkSide affiliates to log in with a stolen credential alone. Per Colonial Pipeline CEO Joseph Blount's testimony before the U.S. House Committee on Homeland Security (June 2021). account with a leaked password and no multi-factor authentication protecting a legacy remote-access system, a purely IT-side compromise. Investigators, including CISA and the FBI, found no evidence that the attackers ever directly breached or manipulated the operational technology (OT) systems that physically control pipeline valves and pumps.

Despite the attack remaining confined to Colonial's IT network, including billing, invoicing, and corporate business systems, the company made the precautionary decision to proactively shut down the entire 5,500-mile pipeline for approximately six days. The company's stated rationale, corroborated by subsequent Congressional testimony, was that Colonial could not be confident that its IT/OT segmentationIT/OT SegmentationThe practice of maintaining a hardened, monitored boundary (per the Purdue Model) between an organization's information technology (IT) network, email, billing, corporate applications, and its operational technology (OT) network, which directly controls physical industrial processes such as pipeline valves, turbines, or manufacturing equipment. CISA's "Layering Network Security Through Segmentation" (2022) guidance was published partly in direct response to lessons learned from Colonial Pipeline. was robust enough to guarantee the ransomware could not spread into, or that the compromised billing systems could not be used to falsify data feeding into, the OT environment responsible for safe pipeline operation. In other words: the company lacked sufficient confidence in its own segmentation boundary to *know* the OT network was safe, so it treated the entire operation as compromised out of caution. This event triggered a regional fuel supply disruption, panic buying across the southeastern United States, and a nearly $4.4 million ransom payment (later partially recovered by the Department of Justice).

A boundary you cannot verify during an incident is not a boundary you can rely on Colonial Pipeline is the textbook example of a subtle but exam-critical point: segmentation is only a defense if you can verify and trust its integrity during an active incident. The ransomware never technically "crossed" into OT, but because Colonial's IT/OT boundary was not rigorously validated, monitored, and documented under a Purdue-style architecture, the company could not prove a negative fast enough to keep the pipeline running safely. A segmentation boundary that exists on a network diagram but isn't continuously monitored and tested is a false sense of security. The question worth asking is why a purely IT-side ransomware attack caused a physical-infrastructure shutdown, and the answer is the absence of verifiable IT/OT isolation, not the ransomware directly reaching OT.

IV. Trunk Hardening, Zero Trust, and Micro-Segmentation

Effective segmentation programs combine hardened Layer 2/3 configuration, identity-centric Zero Trust policy, and continuous validation. The following controls represent the current industry and academic consensus baseline.

1. VLAN and Trunk Hardening

All access-facing switch ports should have DTP negotiation disabled and be explicitly configured as access ports; unused ports should be administratively shut down and assigned to an unused, non-routed black-hole VLANBlack-Hole VLANAn unused VLAN with no Layer 3 gateway and no other member ports, deliberately assigned to every unused or disabled switch port so that if a rogue device is ever plugged into a live but unconfigured port, it lands in an isolated segment with no route anywhere. A widely cited Cisco and SANS Institute hardening recommendation for unused-port security. rather than left in the default VLAN. The native VLAN on every trunk must be changed away from VLAN 1 and should carry no live host traffic. Access control lists (ACLs) restricting inter-VLAN traffic should default-deny and explicitly allow-list only required flows:

! --- from reaching the FINANCE VLAN (10) or any internal RFC1918 space ---
Switch(config)# ip access-list extended GUEST-ISOLATION
Switch(config-ext-nacl)# deny ip 192.168.20.0 0.0.0.255 192.168.10.0 0.0.0.255
Switch(config-ext-nacl)# deny ip 192.168.20.0 0.0.0.255 10.0.0.0 0.255.255.255
Switch(config-ext-nacl)# deny ip 192.168.20.0 0.0.0.255 172.16.0.0 0.15.255.255
Switch(config-ext-nacl)# permit ip 192.168.20.0 0.0.0.255 any
Switch(config-ext-nacl)# exit

! --- Apply inbound on the guest VLAN's routed SVI ---
Switch(config)# interface Vlan20
Switch(config-if)# ip address 192.168.20.1 255.255.255.0
Switch(config-if)# ip access-group GUEST-ISOLATION in
Switch(config-if)# exit

2. Zero Trust Policy Enforcement

Consistent with NIST SP 800-207, organizations should migrate from implicit network-location trust toward continuous, per-session authorization enforced at a PEP in front of every resource, regardless of whether the requester is "inside" or "outside" the traditional perimeter. This typically involves a software-defined perimeter (SDP)Software-Defined Perimeter (SDP)An architecture (sometimes called a "black cloud") in which network resources are made invisible by default, cryptographically hidden from unauthenticated devices via mutual TLS and single-packet authorization, and only become reachable after the requesting device and user have both been verified. Standardized by the Cloud Security Alliance (CSA); a practical implementation pattern for the ZTA principles in NIST SP 800-207. or identity-aware proxy replacing traditional site-to-site VPN, coupled with device posture checks (patch level, disk encryption status, endpoint detection and response agent health) evaluated on every access request rather than once at login.

3. Micro-Segmentation and East-West Inspection

Within data centers and cloud virtual networks, host- or hypervisor-level firewalling should enforce least-privilege, workload-to-workload allow-lists rather than relying solely on perimeter or VLAN-level controls. Internal network detection and response (NDR) sensors should be deployed to inspect east-west traffic for anomalous lateral movement patterns, such as a workstation suddenly initiating SMB connections to dozens of servers, a classic ransomware propagation signature.

4. IT/OT and DMZ Boundary Enforcement (Purdue Model)

Organizations operating industrial control systems should implement a documented, tested, and continuously monitored boundary between IT Levels 4/5 and OT Levels 0–3, typically via a dedicated OT DMZ hosting historian replicas and jump servers so that no direct IT-to-OT protocol traffic is ever permitted. Public-facing services must reside in a DMZ isolated by at least one additional firewall hop from internal systems, and DMZ hosts should never hold direct trust relationships (shared credentials, unrestricted database connectivity) with internal domain infrastructure.

Naming the layer that failed, and the control that belongs there Asking how a breach could have been prevented is best answered by naming the specific layer that failed and the specific control that would have stopped lateral movement at that layer: (1) Layer 2, VLAN hardening, DTP disabled, native VLAN reassigned; (2) Layer 3/4, default-deny inter-VLAN/inter-zone ACLs and firewall rules; (3) Identity/Zero Trust, per-session PEP authorization regardless of network location, phishing-resistant MFA on remote-access accounts (the missing control at Colonial Pipeline); (4) Physical/OT, Purdue-model IT/OT boundary enforcement and, where justified, true air-gapping. Always tie your answer back to blast-radius containment and lateral movement, not just "add a firewall."

Self-Check Review Questions

Question 1: Explain how the double-tagging VLAN hopping attack works at the frame level, and identify the two specific switch misconfigurations that make it possible.

The double-tagging attack exploits the fact that a trunk port carries its native VLAN's traffic untagged, forwarding it as though it were plain, non-802.1Q Ethernet. An attacker positioned on an access port that shares the same VLAN as the trunk's native VLAN crafts a frame containing two stacked 802.1Q headers: an outer tag matching the native VLAN and an inner tag matching the victim VLAN the attacker wishes to reach. When this frame arrives at the first switch, the switch strips the outer tag, because it matches the native VLAN and is therefore not expected to be re-tagged on egress, and forwards the frame onward across the trunk still carrying its inner tag. A second switch downstream reads that remaining inner tag as a legitimate VLAN identifier and delivers the frame into the victim VLAN, even though the attacker's port was never a member of it. This attack requires two specific misconfigurations to succeed: first, the trunk's native VLAN must remain the factory default (typically VLAN 1) or otherwise be a VLAN that also hosts live, reachable end devices; and second, the attacker's own access port must be a member of that same native VLAN. The standard mitigation is to explicitly reassign the native VLAN on every trunk to an unused VLAN ID that carries no host traffic at all, which eliminates the untagged-forwarding behavior the attack depends on. Note that the attack is inherently unidirectional, it allows frame injection into the victim VLAN but does not establish a return path, making it primarily useful for spoofing, denial-of-service, or broadcast/multicast interception rather than full bidirectional access.

Question 2: According to NIST SP 800-207, what distinguishes Zero Trust Architecture from the traditional castle-and-moat perimeter model, and how does micro-segmentation operationalize this distinction?

The castle-and-moat model treats the network perimeter (typically a border firewall) as the primary trust boundary: once a user or device authenticates at that boundary, it is implicitly trusted to communicate broadly with internal resources based on its network location alone. NIST SP 800-207 rejects this assumption outright, formalizing the principle of "never trust, always verify." Under Zero Trust Architecture, network location is explicitly not treated as a proxy for trust; every individual resource access request is authenticated and authorized on a per-session basis using dynamic policy that considers requester identity, device security posture, and behavioral/contextual signals, regardless of whether the request originates from inside or outside the traditional perimeter. Micro-segmentation operationalizes this distinction at the enforcement layer. Rather than relying on coarse VLAN or subnet boundaries, which still implicitly trust any host that happens to reside within a given broadcast domain, micro-segmentation applies granular, workload-specific allow-lists at the level of individual hosts, containers, or processes, enforced by a Policy Enforcement Point acting on instructions from a centralized Policy Decision Point. This means that even two workloads sitting in the identical VLAN or subnet have no default communication path unless a specific, identity-verified policy explicitly permits it, eliminating the flat-network lateral-movement risk that VLAN-only segmentation cannot fully address.

Question 3: Compare the segmentation failures in the 2013 Target breach and the 2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack. Why did Colonial Pipeline shut down its pipeline even though the ransomware never technically reached its operational technology systems?

Both incidents illustrate segmentation failures, but of different character. In the Target breach, the failure was a concrete absence of a trust boundary: attackers who compromised a third-party HVAC vendor's remote-access credentials were able to traverse Target's internal network directly into the point-of-sale cardholder data environment because the two zones were not adequately separated by firewall or access-control boundaries, consistent with a classic flat-network lateral-movement scenario. The remediation is straightforward in principle, enforce a hard segmentation boundary between third-party vendor access and the cardholder data environment, consistent with PCI-DSS scoping guidance. Colonial Pipeline represents a subtler failure: a legacy VPN account without multi-factor authentication was compromised using a leaked, reused password, giving DarkSide affiliates access to Colonial's IT network. Investigators found no evidence the ransomware reached the OT systems that physically control pipeline valves and pumps. However, Colonial Pipeline proactively shut down the pipeline as a precaution because it could not sufficiently verify, in real time during an active incident, that its IT/OT segmentation boundary (as conceptualized in the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture) was robust and monitored enough to guarantee the ransomware, or corrupted data originating from the compromised IT systems, could not affect safe physical pipeline operation. This illustrates that segmentation is only a genuine defense when its integrity can be continuously verified and trusted during an incident; a boundary that exists only as a network diagram, without rigorous monitoring and testing, forces defenders to assume the worst case and can produce severe operational consequences even when the segmentation technically held.

8. 3.3.A 3.3.B Free response. A manufacturer runs office IT and a plant floor of programmable logic controllers on one flat network reachable from a vendor remote-access account. Recommend a segmentation design using the Purdue model, identify at least two techniques and the layer each operates at, and explain the limit of segmentation, what it does not protect against and what must accompany it.

Answer Key

1. B3.3.A

The techniques differ precisely in where they are enforced: a VLAN is a Layer 2 construct realised by the switch tagging and segregating frames, while microsegmentation is applied per workload by a host or hypervisor agent and does not depend on IP address or VLAN membership. A miscategorises the VLAN as an identity control. C is impossible, since an air gap is the absence of any connection and cannot be created by an access control list on a connection that exists. D confuses the two, attributing microsegmentation to the 802.1Q tag, which is the VLAN mechanism.

2. B3.3.A

A private VLAN with isolated ports gives exactly this shape: every isolated port reaches the promiscuous uplink to the gateway but no other isolated port, and it does so inside one subnet, so no per-room addressing is needed. A is the opposite of secure, VLAN 1 is the default management VLAN and places every room in one unsegmented domain. C separates nothing at Layer 2; a shared passphrase still leaves every device on one subnet able to address every other. D would hand each room a trunk carrying all VLANs, which is a severe exposure rather than an isolation.

3. B3.3.A

Double tagging works only when the attacker's access port shares the trunk's native VLAN, because the native VLAN is carried untagged: the first switch removes the outer tag matching the native VLAN and forwards the frame with its inner tag intact, which a downstream switch then honours. A is false, the attack needs no switch credentials, which is what makes it dangerous. C is backwards, since the untagged-native behaviour is an 802.1Q property and the mitigation is to move the native VLAN off VLAN 1 and put no host on it. D is irrelevant to the tag-stripping mechanism.

4. B3.3.B

Segmentation does not touch what an intruder can do on the host they already hold; its value is denying reachability beyond that host's zone, so each further objective requires crossing another default-deny boundary, which costs time and generates east-west traffic a defender can detect. A, C, and D all describe actions confined to the already-compromised host, which no network boundary constrains, the point of segmentation is precisely that it limits spread, not local capability.

5. B3.3.B

A boundary is a defence only if its integrity can be verified during an incident. Colonial could not confirm that its IT/OT segmentation was intact and monitored, so it could not prove the OT network was uncompromised and shut down out of caution, the segmentation existed on paper but not as a validated, monitored control. A contradicts the investigators' finding that the ransomware never reached OT. C misstates air-gapping, which does not preclude monitoring and was not in place. D is unrelated; PCI-DSS governs cardholder data, not pipeline operations.

6. B3.3.A, 3.3.B

The two controls answer different failure modes. A VLAN separates broadcast domains at the wire but still equates network location with trust, so an intruder already inside the finance VLAN faces no further barrier. A Zero Trust microsegmentation policy authorizes each session on identity and device posture regardless of location, which is exactly the case the VLAN leaves open. A is wrong because they enforce different rules, not the same one twice. C overstates Zero Trust, which in practice layers on top of network segmentation rather than removing the need for it. D inverts the layers, VLANs are Layer 2, not Layer 7.

7. Free response3.3.A, 3.3.B

Model answer. The absent boundary at Target was a segmentation barrier between the third-party vendor-access environment and the cardholder data environment. The attackers entered through a stolen HVAC-vendor credential to a billing portal that shared a flat internal network with the point-of-sale estate, and from that foothold moved laterally until they reached the POS registers and installed memory-scraping malware. A default-deny boundary between vendor systems and the CDE would have denied that lateral movement: the vendor compromise would have been contained to a low-value corporate segment with no route to the payment network, so the blast radius would have fallen from the entire internal estate to one billing zone, and the card data, the objective, would have stayed out of reach. Segmentation increases security here by converting one credential theft from a network-wide event into a single-zone event. VLAN separation alone would not have been a complete answer, because a VLAN treats network location as trust: once the attacker held a credential valid inside the trusted network, VLAN membership did not re-verify who they were, and an inter-VLAN routing path or a shared service could still bridge zones. The additional technique that addresses the gap is identity-centric microsegmentation under a Zero Trust policy, in which access to the CDE is authorized per session on identity and device posture rather than on being present on an internal subnet, backed by phishing-resistant MFA on all vendor remote access so a stolen password alone is insufficient.

A grader should look for: (i) the specific missing boundary named as vendor-access to CDE, not merely "more firewalls"; (ii) the lateral movement from billing portal to POS identified as what the boundary would deny; (iii) blast radius stated as a reduction from the whole internal network to one zone; (iv) the recognition that VLAN location-trust is insufficient and that Zero Trust per-session authorization plus MFA closes it. An answer that stops at "they should have segmented the network" without naming the boundary or the lateral path has not met 3.3.B.

8. Free response3.3.A, 3.3.B

Model answer. The design places office IT at Purdue Levels 4 and 5 and the programmable logic controllers at Levels 0 through 2, with a hardened, monitored OT DMZ between them so that no direct IT-to-OT protocol traffic is ever permitted; historian replicas and a jump server live in that DMZ, and the plant floor is reached only through it. At least two techniques with their layers: routed subnets and default-deny firewall rules at Layer 3/4 enforce the IT/OT boundary and the DMZ; VLANs at Layer 2 separate control-network segments on the plant floor, refined with private VLANs so controllers that need not talk to one another cannot; and, for the most safety-critical controllers, physical air-gapping where remote monitoring is not required. The vendor remote-access account must be moved behind the DMZ with phishing-resistant MFA and per-session authorization, never a flat route to the plant. The limit of segmentation is that it constrains reachability but not the actions available once a boundary is legitimately crossed or a trusted path is abused: a boundary that is not continuously monitored and tested becomes a paper control, as at Colonial Pipeline, and an air gap can still be bridged by removable media, as Stuxnet demonstrated. Segmentation must therefore be accompanied by monitoring of the boundaries themselves, strong authentication on every crossing, and detection of anomalous east-west traffic; it reduces blast radius but does not by itself detect or stop an adversary who holds valid credentials for a permitted path.

A grader should look for: (i) a Purdue-structured IT/OT split with an OT DMZ and no direct IT-to-OT traffic; (ii) at least two techniques each tied to a correct layer (routed/firewall at 3/4, VLAN or private VLAN at 2, air gap physical); (iii) the vendor account re-architected with MFA and per-session authorization; (iv) an explicit limit, segmentation constrains reachability, not misuse of a permitted path or local action, and must be monitored and tested, with the air-gap-is-not-absolute point credited. An answer that recommends segmentation without stating its limit or the need for monitoring has not fully met the objective.
