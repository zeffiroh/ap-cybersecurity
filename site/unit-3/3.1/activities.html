3.1 Activities

Network-Level Exploits · 4

Skill 3.1.a · pairs · 25 min · Offline: yes

TCP/IP fundamentals and network topology flaws

Goal

Students trace a network connection through all four TCP/IP layers, identify which header fields are forgeable, and explain why a flat network topology amplifies risk. Ties to LO 3.1.A (describe TCP/IP stack structure and topology-based attack surfaces).

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. Each pair opens the simulation and steps through the simulated TCP connection. For each packet displayed, they label: (a) which layer's header is shown, (b) which field could be forged by an attacker and why.
  2. Pairs draw a simple network diagram showing two scenarios: a flat network and a segmented network. For each, they mark where a compromised host could reach without crossing a firewall.
  3. Using the packet log in the simulation, pairs identify which packets show evidence of IP spoofing and write one sentence describing the defense that would block each one.

Collaborate

Assign roles: one partner is the "network analyst" (describes what each header field means legitimately), the other is the "attacker" (explains how to forge or abuse that field). Swap roles halfway through. For the topology diagram step, both partners must agree before writing.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits: the name of the TCP/IP layer targeted by IP spoofing, the name of one network topology defense, and one sentence explaining why switches alone do not prevent all sniffing attacks.

Skill 3.1.b · small group (3-4) · 25 min · Offline: yes

DNS attacks (spoofing, cache poisoning)

Goal

Groups act as SOC analysts reviewing a resolver's reply log to decide, for each outstanding query, whether the resolver ends up caching a legitimate answer, gets poisoned, or correctly rejects a mismatched reply, then propose one concrete mitigation. Ties to LO 3.1.A, 3.1.B and the course skills Analyze Risk, Detect Attacks.

Materials

Reply log (print or project)

# qname query tx_id/port reply 1 (from, tx_id, port, qname, arrival) reply 2 (from, tx_id, port, qname, arrival) Verdict?
1 pay.example 4410 / 51000 attacker, 4410, 51000, pay.example, arrival 1 authoritative, 4410, 51000, pay.example, arrival 2 ?
2 app.example 8821 / 60010 attacker, 1111, 60010, app.example, arrival 1 authoritative, 8821, 60010, app.example, arrival 2 ?
3 id.example 2290 / 33221 attacker, 2290, 40000, id.example, arrival 1 (none) ?
4 cdn.example 7710 / 12000 authoritative, 7710, 12000, cdn.example, arrival 1 attacker, 7710, 12000, cdn.example, arrival 2 ?

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. (5 min) Each group reads rows 1-2 individually: for each, check whether tx_id AND port AND qname all match the query for every reply, in arrival order, and decide the verdict (WIN / POISON / REJECT).
  2. (5 min) Group discusses row 3: only one reply arrived, and it has the wrong port. What happens to the query? Is this REJECT the "safe" outcome or does the resolver just retry?
  3. (7 min) Group works row 4: the legitimate reply arrived first this time. Discuss why arrival order, not just "does the attacker eventually send a matching reply", is what determines the winner.
  4. (5 min) Each group opens the simulation and checks their calls against the sim's scoring for analogous scenarios; groups revise any incorrect calls.
  5. (3 min) Report-out: each group proposes one mitigation (source-port randomization, DNSSEC, or DoH/DoT) and states specifically which field(s) in the log that mitigation would have changed.

Collaborate

Assign roles: one member is the "resolver" (applies the tx_id/port/qname matching rule literally, in arrival order), one is the "attacker" (explains what the attacker would have needed to guess correctly to win each row), and remaining members are "auditors" (check the resolver's and attacker's reasoning against the three-field matching rule from the lesson). For row 1, the group must specifically explain why a wide open source port (guessable) made the poisoning possible, and what changes if source-port randomization is added.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student writes one sentence: "Row X is [WIN / POISON / REJECT] because [tx_id / port / qname] [matched / did not match] and [reasoning about arrival order]."

Skill 3.1.c · small group (3-4) · 25 min · Offline: yes

Man-in-the-middle and ARP spoofing

Goal

Groups act as network analysts reviewing ARP-watch logs for a small office LAN, decide which IP addresses show the ARP spoofing signature, and design one mitigation for the network. Ties to LO 3.1.B and the course skill Analyze Risk.

Materials

ARP-watch log (print or project)

# Target IP Reply sequence (MAC @ t=sec) Verdict?
1 192.168.1.1 (gateway) aa:01 @0, aa:01 @30, aa:01 @60 ?
2 192.168.1.1 (gateway) aa:01 @0, aa:01 @30, ff:99 @32 ?
3 192.168.1.40 12:34 @0 ?
4 192.168.1.60 bb:02 @0, cc:03 @600 ?

(Row 4: the second reply arrives 10 minutes after the first, discuss whether the same rule applies regardless of the time gap.)

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. (5 min) Each group reads rows 1-2 individually and decides LEGIT or SPOOFED using the trusted-binding rule from the lesson (first reply establishes trust; a later reply with a different MAC for the same IP is spoofing).
  2. (5 min) Group discusses row 3: with only one reply ever observed, is there enough evidence to call this SPOOFED? Why must a baseline exist first?
  3. (7 min) Group works row 4 together: the MAC changes, but only after a 10-minute gap. Does a long time gap make the change more or less suspicious than a change seconds later (as in row 2)? Discuss legitimate reasons a MAC might change (e.g., a NIC replacement, DHCP lease to a new device) versus the spoofing explanation.
  4. (5 min) Each group opens the simulation and checks their calls against the sim's scoring for analogous scenarios; groups revise any incorrect calls.
  5. (3 min) Report-out: each group proposes one mitigation (Dynamic ARP Inspection, static ARP entry for the gateway, or VLAN segmentation) and explains which row it would have stopped.

Collaborate

Assign roles: one member is the "arpwatch operator" (applies the trusted-binding rule literally), one is the "attacker" (explains what a real attacker would need to do to reproduce each row), and remaining members are "auditors" (challenge whether the binding-change evidence is strong enough to call SPOOFED). For row 2 (the gateway), the group must specifically discuss why attackers usually target the default gateway's IP rather than a random host, and what an attacker gains by doing so.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student writes one sentence: "Row X is [LEGIT / SPOOFED] because [the MAC did / did not] change for [IP], and [reasoning about trusted binding or timing]."

Skill 3.1.d · small group (3-4) · 25 min · Offline: yes

Packet capture and traffic sniffing

Goal

Groups act as SOC analysts triaging a batch of captured packet log entries, decide which ones leak a plaintext credential, and propose one mitigation per protocol involved. Ties to LO 3.1.B, 3.1.C and the course skill Detect Attacks.

Materials

Capture log (print or project)

# Protocol/Port Destination Payload snippet Verdict?
1 TCP/80 intranet.example:80 POST /login ... user=alice&password=Pass123! ?
2 TCP/443 payroll.example:443 (TLS encrypted application data) ?
3 TCP/23 switch.local:23 login: netadmin\nPassword: Sw1tchAdmin ?
4 TCP/80 wiki.example:80 GET /docs/onboarding.html HTTP/1.1 ?
5 TCP/21 ftp.example:21 USER backup\nPASS Backup#2024 ?

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. (5 min) Each group reads rows 1-2 individually and decides LEAK or SAFE using the protocol/port + payload-content rule from the lesson.
  2. (5 min) Group discusses row 3 (Telnet): why is the entire login exchange visible even though this is an "administrative" connection to network equipment, not a public website?
  3. (7 min) Group works rows 4-5 together: row 4 is cleartext HTTP but the payload holds no credential; row 5 is a different cleartext protocol (FTP) with a different credential syntax (USER/PASS). Decide both, and discuss why the port/protocol matters as much as the payload content.
  4. (5 min) Each group opens the simulation and checks their calls against the sim's scoring for analogous scenarios; groups revise any incorrect calls.
  5. (3 min) Report-out: each group proposes the encrypted replacement protocol for each LEAK row (e.g., HTTPS for HTTP, SSH for Telnet, SFTP/FTPS for FTP).

Collaborate

Assign roles: one member is the "capture analyst" (applies the protocol/port + payload rule), one is the "network admin" (explains what legitimate business reason each connection might have), and remaining members are "auditors" (challenge whether a payload really contains a credential or just looks like one). For row 2 (HTTPS, encrypted), the group must specifically explain why a capture analyst cannot determine anything about the login credentials even though they can see the connection is happening.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student writes one sentence: "Row X is [LEAK / SAFE] because [protocol/port] is [cleartext / encrypted] and the payload [does / does not] contain a credential."
