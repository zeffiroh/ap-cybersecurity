Device and Endpoint Vulnerabilities · 3

Skill 4.1.a · pairs · 25 min · Offline: yes

Malware types (viruses, worms, trojans, ransomware)

Goal

Students learn to distinguish the four malware categories by behavioral signature and identify the most effective defensive response for each. Ties to LO 4.1.A (identify malware categories) and 4.1.B (explain how each causes harm and spreads).

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.

  1. Classify the incidents (10 min). Each pair reads the five incident reports below and writes the malware category (virus, worm, trojan, ransomware) and one-sentence justification for each.

  2. Incident A: A hospital database server is scanning the entire internal network for open port 1433. No administrator started this scan. The server's CPU is at 97%.

  3. Incident B: A student downloaded a "free Adobe Photoshop crack." Their computer now sends screenshots to an unknown IP address every 60 seconds.
  4. Incident C: Every .docx file on a machine now has an extra copy with a slightly different name. The copies appeared after the user opened an email attachment.
  5. Incident D: All files on a shared drive have been renamed to .locked extensions. A text file on the desktop reads: "Send 0.5 BTC to this address to recover your data."

  6. Incident E: A USB drive brought from home infected three workstations when plugged in. Each infected machine then infected others when their shared folders were opened.

  7. Compare with the simulation (5 min). Open the simulation and work through its incident stream to check your classifications. Note any disagreements and discuss why.

  8. Build a defense table (10 min). As a pair, fill in the table:

Malware type Primary spread method One technical defense One policy defense
Virus
Worm
Trojan
Ransomware

Collaborate

Assign roles: one partner is the "incident analyst" (explains which category and why), the other is the "defender" (proposes the most effective countermeasure). Swap roles for the defense table. Compare your completed tables with another pair, identify any defense they listed that you missed.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits: their classification of Incident B + a one-sentence explanation of why user education alone is insufficient to prevent all trojan infections.

Skill 4.1.b · small group · 30 min · Offline: yes

Device tampering and physical compromise

Goal

Students identify physical security vulnerabilities in a clinic's office layout and devise a comprehensive physical-hardening strategy to protect workstations, servers, and network devices against tampering. Ties to LO 4.1.B.

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.
  1. Analyze the Scenario (10 min): Read the following description of a medical clinic's setup. Identify at least four distinct physical vulnerabilities that expose the clinic's devices to physical tampering or offline access:
    • Scenario: A local medical clinic's reception desk is in the main lobby, where patients wait. The receptionist's workstation sits on a counter, with its back facing the waiting area, exposing the USB ports directly to visitors. A network printer sits in a hallway that patients use to reach exam rooms. The main server cabinet is located in a repurposed storage closet; the door has a standard key lock, but the keys are kept in a drawer at the reception desk, which is left unlocked during business hours. Staff laptops are left open on exam room desks while doctors step out to consult with colleagues.
  2. Devise Hardening Countermeasures (10 min): For each vulnerability identified, specify at least one physical and one logical mitigation. Use terms such as port blockers, BIOS/UEFI hardening, Full Disk Encryption (FDE), Kensington cable locks, and chassis intrusion detection.
  3. Refine the Defense (10 min): Present your group's top two recommendations to the class. Discuss why logical security alone (like screen-lock passwords) fails if an attacker has physical access.

Collaborate

Assign roles within each group: - Physical Security Architect: Focuses on structural barriers, locks, and placement. - Systems Administrator: Focuses on logical policies (FDE, BIOS/UEFI settings, device timeouts). Work together to create a unified recommendation.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits: One reason why Full Disk Encryption (FDE) is necessary even if a server room has a physical door lock.

Skill 4.1.c · pairs · 25 min · Offline: yes

IoT device vulnerabilities

Goal

Students learn to identify the dominant vulnerability category in a described IoT device and propose the matching defense. Ties to LO 4.1.A (identify vulnerability categories) and 4.1.C (analyze IoT-specific risk).

Materials

Steps

Check off all materials above to unlock the steps.

  1. Classify the devices (10 min). Each pair reads the five device descriptions below and writes the dominant vulnerability category (default credentials, outdated firmware, exposed port, unauthenticated API, unencrypted traffic, or secure) with a one-sentence justification.

  2. Device A: A smart doorbell camera is still running the username/password it shipped with from the factory. Its firmware was updated last month and its app traffic is encrypted.

  3. Device B: An office network-attached printer has Telnet (port 23) open for "remote diagnostics," left on since installation three years ago. Its password was changed at setup.
  4. Device C: A smart thermostat's manufacturer stopped releasing firmware updates two years ago. A known vulnerability in its old firmware version was published last month. No default password, no open management ports.
  5. Device D: A fitness tracker's companion app talks to a cloud API that returns any user's step data if you simply guess their numeric account ID, no login token required.

  6. Device E: A smart plug sends its on/off commands over the local network in plain, unencrypted text, readable by anything else connected to that Wi-Fi network.

  7. Compare with the simulation (5 min). Open the simulation and work through its device audit to check your classifications. Note any disagreements and discuss why.

  8. Build a defense table (10 min). As a pair, fill in the table:

Vulnerability category Why it's dangerous One technical defense One policy defense
Default credentials
Outdated firmware
Exposed port
Unauthenticated API
Unencrypted traffic

Collaborate

Assign roles: one partner is the "auditor" (identifies which vulnerability category and why), the other is the "remediator" (proposes the most effective fix). Swap roles for the defense table. Compare your completed tables with another pair, identify any defense they listed that you missed, and discuss whether any device in Step 1 could realistically fail more than one category at once.

Exit Ticket

AP

Certificate of Completion

Each student submits: their classification of Device D + a one-sentence explanation of why a botnet like Mirai is dangerous even though each individual compromised device seems unimportant.
