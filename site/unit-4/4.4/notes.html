I. Logs as the Endpoint's Memory

Topics 4.1–4.3 examined how endpoints are attacked, authenticated, and hardened. This final chapter of Unit 4 addresses what happens after every prior control has already failed and an attacker has gained a foothold on an endpoint: endpoint threat auditingEndpoint Threat AuditingThe practice of systematically recording, protecting, and analyzing security-relevant events generated by an endpoint's operating system, applications, and security tooling, in order to detect intrusions, reconstruct attacker activity, and support forensic investigation after the fact. The device-level equivalent of the physical intrusion detection concepts covered in Topic 2.4, assuming prevention will eventually fail and asking how quickly and completely the failure can be reconstructed. NIST SP 800-53 control family AU (Audit and Accountability) governs this domain, most notably AU-2 (Event Logging) and AU-9 (Protection of Audit Information)., the discipline of recording, protecting, and analyzing the trail of evidence an endpoint generates during normal operation and during an attack.

Why Logs Exist: The Endpoint's Memory of Itself

Every modern operating system maintains an internal audit logAudit Log (Security Event Log)A structured, chronological record of security-relevant events generated by an operating system or application, logon attempts, privilege escalations, file access, process creation, configuration changes. Functions as the endpoint's own memory of what has happened to it, and is frequently the only surviving evidence of an attack once the attacker's live session has ended. Windows records these events in the Security event log (accessible via Event Viewer); Linux systems typically use auditd alongside syslog for equivalent functionality. of security-relevant activity: who logged in and when, which processes were created, which files were accessed, which privileges were escalated. This log is, in a very real sense, the endpoint's own memory of what has happened to it, and for an investigator arriving after an attack has concluded, it is frequently the only surviving evidence of what the attacker actually did, since the attacker's live session, network connection, and any in-memory artifacts are typically gone by the time an incident is discovered.

The Attacker's Incentive to Erase the Record

Because logs are often the only durable evidence of an intrusion, a rational attacker has a strong incentive to tamper with or delete them as one of the final steps of an attack, a technique classified under the MITRE ATT&CKMITRE ATT&CK FrameworkA publicly maintained, community-sourced knowledge base cataloging real-world adversary tactics and techniques observed across documented intrusions, organized into a matrix used widely across the security industry for threat modeling, detection engineering, and red-team/blue-team exercises. Log tampering and deletion is cataloged specifically under ATT&CK technique T1070 (Indicator Removal), sub-technique T1070.002 (Clear Linux or Mac System Logs) and T1070.001 (Clear Windows Event Logs). technique category "Indicator Removal." An attacker who has gained local administrative privileges on a Windows endpoint can, for example, clear the Security event log entirely using built-in operating system tools, and an attacker on a Linux endpoint can truncate or delete local syslog files, in both cases attempting to erase the very evidence that would otherwise reveal how they got in, what they did, and where they went next.

Clearing a log is itself an event, and an unusual one A crucial insight this chapter builds toward: the act of clearing or deleting a security log is, itself, a loggable and highly suspicious security event. A legitimate administrator essentially never has a routine business reason to clear the entire Security event log on a production endpoint. When a SOC sees an event indicating the log was cleared, that event itself should be treated as a near-certain indicator of compromise, not dismissed as routine maintenance.

The Core Defense: Get the Logs Off the Endpoint

The foundational architectural principle of endpoint threat auditing follows directly from the attacker incentive described above: if logs are only ever stored locally on the endpoint itself, an attacker who compromises that endpoint can simply delete the evidence. The defense is to continuously forward every log event, in as close to real time as possible, to a centralized, secure log serverCentralized Secure Log Server (Syslog / SIEM)An architecture in which every monitored endpoint forwards its security event data, typically in near-real-time, to a separate, hardened, centrally managed server, a Syslog collector or a full Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) platform, so that a copy of every log record exists outside the reach of an attacker who compromises any single endpoint. Even if the attacker deletes the local copy after the fact, the centrally forwarded copy remains intact and available for investigation. This "forward first, delete second" race is why security architects treat log-forwarding latency as a meaningful security metric: the shorter the delay between event generation and off-host forwarding, the smaller the window in which a fast-acting attacker could delete a local event before it is copied off-host., physically and logically separated from any single endpoint an attacker might compromise. If the log has already left the endpoint by the time an attacker attempts to clear it, deleting the local copy accomplishes nothing; the centrally forwarded copy is untouched. This is the single most important architectural decision in endpoint threat auditing, and it is the device-level parallel to the "assume prevention will eventually fail" philosophy introduced with physical detection in Topic 2.4.

II. Windows Event IDs, auditd, and Tamper-Evident Logging

This section examines specific log event types, the mechanics of log forwarding, and the techniques used to make audit data tamper-evident.

Key Windows Security Event IDs

Windows records security events using numeric Event IDsWindows Event IDA numeric identifier assigned to a specific category of event recorded in the Windows Security event log, allowing analysts and detection tooling to filter and correlate on precise event types rather than parsing free-text descriptions. Event IDs are stable across Windows versions for the same event category, making them a reliable basis for detection rules and SIEM correlation logic shared across an entire fleet of heterogeneous endpoints. that allow analysts and automated tooling to filter and correlate on precise event categories. Event ID 4625 ("An account failed to log on") records every failed authentication attempt and is one of the most heavily monitored event types in enterprise security operations, since a burst of 4625 events against a single account in a short window is a classic indicator of a password-guessing or brute-force attack. Event ID 4624 records a successful logon and, when correlated against a preceding burst of 4625 failures for the same account, can indicate a successful brute-force compromise. Event ID 1102 specifically records that the Security event log itself was cleared, directly implementing the detection principle described in Section I, since this event is generated by the very act of an attacker attempting to erase their tracks.

Correlated Brute-Force Detection Rule (SIEM Pseudo-Config) 
# SIEM correlation rule -- detect brute-force followed by success
[detection_rule]
name            = "Brute-force-then-success"
condition_1     = EventID=4625 COUNT>=10 WITHIN 5m SAME account,source_ip
condition_2     = EventID=4624 WITHIN 2m AFTER condition_1 SAME account
severity        = HIGH
action          = ALERT-SOC + AUTO-DISABLE-ACCOUNT-PENDING-REVIEW

# Rationale: 10+ failed logons in 5 minutes followed by a success for
# the same account is a strong indicator that a brute-force or
# credential-stuffing attempt eventually guessed correctly, rather
# than a legitimate user simply mistyping their password repeatedly.

Linux Auditd and Syslog

Linux systems achieve equivalent auditing through two complementary subsystems: auditdauditd (Linux Audit Daemon)The Linux kernel's built-in auditing subsystem, configurable to record specific security-relevant kernel events, file access, system calls, privilege changes, according to a set of audit rules. Provides far more granular, kernel-level visibility than syslog alone, at the cost of higher configuration complexity and log volume. Common Criteria and many U.S. federal compliance frameworks (e.g., systems requiring FIPS 140 or DISA STIG hardening) mandate specific auditd rule sets as a baseline requirement., which provides granular kernel-level event auditing, and syslogSyslogA standardized logging protocol and message format (RFC 5424) used by Unix/Linux systems, network devices, and many applications to generate and, critically, forward log messages to a remote collector over the network. The forwarding capability built into the syslog protocol itself is what makes centralized, off-host log aggregation straightforward across heterogeneous device types. Syslog messages carry a standardized severity level (0–7, from Emergency to Debug) and facility code, allowing a central collector to apply consistent filtering and alerting logic across many different device types and vendors., the standardized protocol most commonly used to forward those events (and other application log data) to a remote collector. A local attacker with root privileges can, in principle, disable auditd, delete `/var/log/auth.log`, or clear the systemd journal, precisely the local file log deletion threat vector this chapter centers on, which is exactly why production Linux fleets are configured to forward syslog output to a remote server continuously, rather than relying on local retention alone.

Tamper-Evident Logging: Write-Once and Cryptographic Chaining

Centralizing logs off-host defends against local deletion, but a sufficiently privileged attacker who also compromises the central log server itself could still tamper with historical records. Advanced log architectures defend against this second-order risk using write-once storageWrite-Once, Read-Many (WORM) StorageA storage medium or configuration that permits data to be written once but never subsequently modified or deleted, even by an administrator with otherwise full privileges over the storage system, typically enforced at the hardware or filesystem level. Prevents even a fully compromised log-server administrator account from retroactively altering historical audit records. Many regulatory and forensic chain-of-custody frameworks explicitly require WORM-compliant storage for audit logs subject to legal or compliance retention requirements. and cryptographic log chainingCryptographic Log Chaining (Hash Chaining)A tamper-evidence technique in which each log entry includes a cryptographic hash of the previous entry, forming a chain analogous in principle to a blockchain: altering any single historical entry breaks the hash chain for every subsequent entry, making tampering mathematically detectable even if the underlying storage itself is not physically write-once. This is conceptually identical to the block-linking mechanism used in blockchain technology, applied here purely for tamper-evidence rather than for distributed consensus., in which each new log entry incorporates a cryptographic hash of the prior entry, meaning any retroactive alteration of a historical record breaks the hash chain for every subsequent entry and becomes immediately, mathematically detectable during an integrity verification pass.

Centralisation moves the target rather than removing it Forwarding logs off-host defeats an attacker who only compromises a single endpoint. It does not, by itself, defeat an attacker who additionally gains administrative access to the central log server. Tamper-evident techniques (WORM storage, hash chaining) exist specifically to close this second-order gap, and a complete answer describing endpoint audit defense should mention both layers rather than treating "we send logs to a central server" as a complete solution.

II-A. Reading a Password Attack Out of a Log

Every credential attack leaves the same primitive record: an authentication failed. What separates one attack from another is not the individual line but the shape of the set, how many accounts were touched, how deeply each was pressed, over what interval, from how many places, and whether the usernames were real. Two numbers do most of the work, and it is worth naming them before looking at any log:

  • Breadth, the number of distinct accounts a source attempted.
  • Depth, the largest number of attempts aimed at any single account.

The three attacks in Topic 4.2 occupy different regions of that plane, and they occupy them for reasons that follow from what the attacker is trying to do.

Brute force: narrow and deep

The attacker has one account and is exhausting its password space. Depth is high, hundreds or thousands of attempts, breadth is one or two, and the interval is short because the attacker is limited only by the endpoint's response time. This is the easiest of the three to detect and, against any account with a lockout policy, the least likely to succeed. In practice it survives mainly against services that have no lockout at all, which is why exposed SSH and RDP endpoints see it constantly.

Password spraying: wide and shallow

The attacker inverts the loop: for each of a handful of very likely passwords, try it against every account in the directory. Depth stays at two or three, deliberately, because the attacker has read the same lockout policy the defender wrote. Breadth is enormous. The interval is long, hours or days, and the source addresses are often numerous, because per-source rate limits are the other control being evaded. No per-account counter will ever fire. Detection has to look at the directory as a whole and ask how many distinct accounts failed per unit time, and how many distinct accounts a single source touched.

Credential stuffing: shaped like spraying, sourced differently

Stuffing replays username and password pairs recovered from someone else's breach. Its footprint in an authentication log is close to indistinguishable from a spray: wide, shallow, distributed. The difference is provenance, and provenance is not a field any authentication log contains. There are secondary hints, a stuffing run works from a list of real accounts obtained elsewhere, so its usernames tend to exist and to include addresses that are not in the local directory at all, while a spray drawn from a generic wordlist produces a high proportion of accounts that were never valid. Those are hints and not proof.

Name what the evidence supports, and no more Calling a wide, shallow pattern "credential stuffing" in an incident report asserts something the log does not show: that the attempted passwords came from a breach of another service. The honest finding is "distributed low-and-slow guessing across the directory, consistent with spraying or stuffing," followed by the evidence that would separate them, matching attempted credentials against a breach corpus, or the ratio of valid to invalid usernames. This is not pedantry. The two attacks call for different responses: a spray is answered by password policy and breach screening, while confirmed stuffing means a specific population of users has reused a credential elsewhere and needs targeted resets.

Windows: the fields that matter in a 4625

The event identifiers listed in Section II are the coarse filter; the useful detail is inside the record. A 4625 carries a Status and a Sub Status code, and the pair distinguishes failure modes that look identical in a summary count. The two that matter most for credential attacks are 0xC000006A, meaning the account exists and the password was wrong, and 0xC0000064, meaning no such account exists. A run dominated by 0xC0000064 is an attacker enumerating usernames from a wordlist; a run dominated by 0xC000006A against real accounts means the attacker already knows who works there.

The Logon Type field says how the attempt arrived. Type 3 is a network logon (SMB, and most lateral movement), type 10 is RemoteInteractive (RDP), type 2 is a person at the physical keyboard, and type 5 is a service starting. A burst of type 10 failures against many accounts from one external address is a spray against exposed RDP; the same volume at type 2 would mean somebody is standing at the machine, which is a different investigation entirely.

Three further identifiers complete the picture. 4624 is a successful logon and carries the same Logon Type and source-address fields, which is what makes it correlatable with the failures that preceded it. 4740 records that an account was locked out, useful, but note what it implies: a run that generates 4740 events was too deep to be a spray, and a competent spray generates none at all. 1102 records that the Security log itself was cleared. On a domain controller, Kerberos pre-authentication failures appear as 4771 rather than 4625, with failure code 0x18 for a wrong password; a detection rule written only against 4625 will miss an attack against a domain account authenticating over Kerberos, which is a common and expensive gap.

A quiet lockout log is not evidence that nothing is happening It is tempting to monitor 4740 as the credential-attack signal, because a lockout is unambiguous and rare. The inference runs the wrong way. Lockouts are generated by attacks that failed to respect the threshold; the attacks worth worrying about are specifically the ones that respected it. An estate with a five-attempt lockout policy and no 4740 events in a month has demonstrated that nobody ran a naive brute force. It has demonstrated nothing whatever about spraying.

Linux: the same reasoning in auth.log

OpenSSH writes a verdict line per attempt to /var/log/auth.log on Debian-family systems and /var/log/secure on Red Hat-family systems. The lines carry every field the analysis needs: outcome, account, source address, and, through the phrase invalid user, whether the account existed at all, which is the direct analogue of the Windows sub-status distinction.

II-B. Detecting the Shape Programmatically

The reasoning above can be mechanised, and doing so exposes exactly where judgement is still required. The script below reads an OpenSSH auth.log, computes breadth and depth per source address, classifies the shape, and reports any successful logon from a source that was also failing. It is a teaching implementation: it reads one file into memory and uses fixed thresholds. Production detection belongs in a streaming pipeline with thresholds tuned per environment and an allowlist for scanners and monitoring accounts, and the script says so in its own docstring.

Python 3 (standard library only), spray_detect.py 
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Flag password-attack patterns in an OpenSSH auth.log.

Teaching implementation. It reads one file, holds every parsed event in
memory, and applies fixed thresholds. That is fine for a class exercise and
wrong for production, where the same logic belongs in a streaming pipeline
with per-environment thresholds and an allowlist for scanners and monitoring
accounts. Do not deploy this as written.

Usage:
    python spray_detect.py auth.log --year 2026
"""

import argparse
import re
from collections import defaultdict
from datetime import datetime

# Matches the two OpenSSH lines that carry a password verdict. "invalid user"
# appears when the account does not exist at all, which is itself a signal:
# an attacker working from a leaked employee list produces mostly valid
# usernames, while one working from a generic wordlist produces mostly
# invalid ones.
SSHD = re.compile(
    r"^(?P<ts>\w{3}\s+\d+\s+\d{2}:\d{2}:\d{2})\s+"
    r"(?P<host>\S+)\s+sshd\[\d+\]:\s+"
    r"(?P<result>Failed|Accepted)\s+password for\s+"
    r"(?P<invalid>invalid user\s+)?(?P<user>\S+)\s+"
    r"from\s+(?P<ip>\S+)\s+port\s+\d+\s+ssh2"
)


def parse(path, year):
    """Yield (timestamp, result, user, ip, user_existed) for each verdict line."""
    events = []
    with open(path, encoding="utf-8", errors="replace") as fh:
        for line in fh:
            m = SSHD.match(line)
            if not m:
                continue
            # syslog timestamps carry no year, so the caller supplies one.
            ts = datetime.strptime(
                "%s %d" % (m.group("ts"), year), "%b %d %H:%M:%S %Y"
            )
            events.append(
                (
                    ts,
                    m.group("result"),
                    m.group("user"),
                    m.group("ip"),
                    m.group("invalid") is None)
            )
    events.sort(key=lambda e: e[0])
    return events


def summarise(events):
    """Per source address, measure the breadth and depth of its failures.

    Breadth  = how many distinct usernames the source touched.
    Depth    = the most failures it aimed at any single username.

    Those two numbers separate the attack shapes. A spray is wide and
    shallow because the attacker is staying under a per-account lockout
    counter. Brute force is narrow and deep because the attacker is
    exhausting one account's password space.
    """
    per_ip = defaultdict(lambda: {"fail": defaultdict(int), "ok": [], "first": None,
                                  "last": None, "invalid": 0, "total": 0})
    for ts, result, user, ip, existed in events:
        rec = per_ip[ip]
        if rec["first"] is None or ts < rec["first"]:
            rec["first"] = ts
        if rec["last"] is None or ts > rec["last"]:
            rec["last"] = ts
        rec["total"] += 1
        if result == "Failed":
            rec["fail"][user] += 1
            if not existed:
                rec["invalid"] += 1
        else:
            rec["ok"].append((ts, user))

    out = []
    for ip, rec in per_ip.items():
        if not rec["fail"]:
            continue
        out.append(
            {
                "ip": ip,
                "breadth": len(rec["fail"]),
                "depth": max(rec["fail"].values()),
                "failures": sum(rec["fail"].values()),
                "invalid_user_failures": rec["invalid"],
                "successes": rec["ok"],
                "span": rec["last"] - rec["first"],
            }
        )
    return sorted(out, key=lambda r: r["failures"], reverse=True)


def classify(row, min_breadth, max_spray_depth, min_brute_depth):
    if row["breadth"] >= min_breadth and row["depth"] <= max_spray_depth:
        return "PASSWORD SPRAYING"
    if row["depth"] >= min_brute_depth and row["breadth"] <= 2:
        return "BRUTE FORCE"
    if row["breadth"] >= min_breadth and row["depth"] > max_spray_depth:
        return "BROAD GUESSING (mixed shape - review manually)"
    return "low-volume failures"


def main():
    ap = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__)
    ap.add_argument("logfile")
    ap.add_argument("--year", type=int, default=datetime.now().year)
    ap.add_argument("--min-breadth", type=int, default=10,
                    help="distinct usernames before a source looks like a spray")
    ap.add_argument("--max-spray-depth", type=int, default=5,
                    help="attempts per username a spray stays under")
    ap.add_argument("--min-brute-depth", type=int, default=10,
                    help="attempts against one username that indicate brute force")
    args = ap.parse_args()

    events = parse(args.logfile, args.year)
    if not events:
        print("no sshd password verdicts found in %s" % args.logfile)
        return

    rows = summarise(events)
    print("%-16s %8s %6s %9s %9s %9s  %s" %
          ("SOURCE", "FAILURES", "DEPTH", "USERNAMES", "INVALID", "SPAN",
           "ASSESSMENT"))
    for row in rows:
        verdict = classify(row, args.min_breadth, args.max_spray_depth,
                           args.min_brute_depth)
        print("%-16s %8d %6d %9d %9d %9s  %s" %
              (row["ip"], row["failures"], row["depth"], row["breadth"],
               row["invalid_user_failures"], str(row["span"]), verdict))
        for ts, user in row["successes"]:
            # A success from a source that was also guessing is the finding
            # that matters. Everything above it is only context.
            print("    ** SUCCESS from this source: %s as '%s' from %s"
                  % (ts.isoformat(sep=" "), user, row["ip"]))

    total_breadth = len({e[2] for e in events if e[1] == "Failed"})
    sources = len({e[3] for e in events if e[1] == "Failed"})
    if total_breadth >= args.min_breadth and sources > 1:
        print("\nFleet view: %d distinct usernames failed across %d source "
              "addresses. A spray distributed over many sources will not "
              "exceed the per-source thresholds above; judge it here."
              % (total_breadth, sources))
    print("\nNote: these logs cannot tell you where the passwords came from. "
          "Distinguishing credential stuffing from spraying requires matching "
          "the attempted credentials against a known breach corpus, which is "
          "evidence this file does not contain.")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

against a known breach corpus, which is evidence this file does not contain.

Two limitations in that output are the point of the exercise rather than defects to be fixed. The per-source thresholds are defeated by an attacker who distributes the same spray across a hundred addresses, each staying under the breadth threshold, which is why the script reports a fleet-wide view that aggregates regardless of source, and why real detections for spraying are usually written against the directory rather than against a host. And no threshold anywhere in the script can determine provenance, so the tool declines to call anything credential stuffing. A detection that reports what it cannot know is worse than one that reports less.

The corresponding query on the Windows side does the same aggregation over the Security log. The following is a working PowerShell one-liner for a single host, useful for triage before a SIEM query is written:

PowerShell, breadth-first view of 4625 failures on one host 
# Group failed logons by source address, then count DISTINCT target accounts.
# High account count with low per-account depth is the spray signature.
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{ LogName = 'Security'; Id = 4625;
                                 StartTime = (Get-Date).AddHours(-24) } |
    ForEach-Object {
        $x = [xml]$_.ToXml()
        [pscustomobject]@{
            Time    = $_.TimeCreated
            Account = ($x.Event.EventData.Data | Where-Object Name -eq 'TargetUserName').'#text'
            Source  = ($x.Event.EventData.Data | Where-Object Name -eq 'IpAddress').'#text'
            Type    = ($x.Event.EventData.Data | Where-Object Name -eq 'LogonType').'#text'
            SubStat = ($x.Event.EventData.Data | Where-Object Name -eq 'SubStatus').'#text'
        }
    } |
    Group-Object Source |
    Select-Object Name,
        @{ n = 'Failures';  e = { $_.Count } },
        @{ n = 'Accounts';  e = { ($_.Group.Account | Sort-Object -Unique).Count } },
        @{ n = 'MaxDepth';  e = { ($_.Group | Group-Object Account |
                                   Measure-Object Count -Maximum).Maximum } } |
    Sort-Object Failures -Descending | Format-Table -AutoSize

III. Case Studies: Log Clearing and Correlated Logon Failures

Case Study 4.4.1: Post-Intrusion Log Clearing as a Standard Attacker Playbook

Publicly documented incident-response case studies across numerous ransomware and targeted-intrusion investigations consistently report the same late-stage attacker behavior: after achieving their primary objective (data exfiltration, ransomware deployment, or establishing persistent access), attackers frequently attempt to clear Windows Security event logs, delete PowerShell command history, and remove or truncate relevant syslog files on any Linux systems they touched, precisely the T1070 "Indicator Removal" technique described in Section I. Incident responders investigating these cases have repeatedly found that organizations with centralized log forwarding in place before the intrusion were able to fully reconstruct the attacker's timeline from the forwarded copies despite local log deletion, while organizations relying solely on local log retention lost significant forensic visibility into exactly how the initial compromise occurred, directly validating the "forward first, delete second" architectural principle from Section I as the decisive factor in investigative outcome.

Detection assumes prevention has already failed Just as Topic 2.4 argued that physical detection assumes prevention will eventually fail and exists to minimize dwell time, endpoint threat auditing assumes an intrusion will eventually succeed and exists to preserve the evidence needed to understand and remediate it after the fact. Neither replaces prevention (Topics 4.1–4.3); both are the necessary complement to it.

Case Study 4.4.2: Brute-Force Detection via Correlated 4625/4624 Events

Enterprise SOC case studies documenting credential-stuffing and brute-force campaigns against exposed remote-access services (RDP, VPN gateways) consistently describe the same detection pattern implemented in production SIEM platforms: a correlation rule flagging any account that accumulates a high volume of Event ID 4625 failures in a short window, followed by an eventual Event ID 4624 success for that same account, as a high-confidence indicator of a successful brute-force compromise rather than routine mistyped-password activity. Organizations that implemented this specific correlation rule (rather than alerting on 4625 failures alone, which generates excessive false-positive volume from ordinary user error) reported meaningfully faster detection of successful brute-force compromises, directly illustrating the value of correlated, multi-signal detection logic over single-event alerting, the same principle introduced for physical sensors in Topic 2.4.

Correlation is what turns an ordinary event into evidence A single Event ID 4624 is the most ordinary line in a Windows Security log; several thousand of them are generated on an average morning. The same 4624 becomes the most important line in the file when it follows a burst of 4625 failures from the same source address against the same account. Nothing about the event itself changed, what changed is what surrounds it. This is why detection engineering is largely the work of specifying relationships between events rather than cataloguing individual ones, and why a rule that fires on 4625 alone is answering a question nobody asked.

IV. An Endpoint Auditing Program: Coverage, Forwarding, Correlation

A mature endpoint threat-auditing program combines complete event coverage, immediate off-host forwarding, tamper-evident storage, and disciplined correlation logic.

1. Comprehensive, Baseline-Aligned Event Logging

Endpoints should be configured to log the full baseline of security-relevant events recommended by a recognized hardening standard (such as a DISA STIG or CIS Benchmark audit policy configuration) rather than relying on default, often minimal, out-of-the-box logging settings, directly closing detection gaps that would otherwise exist simply because a relevant event type was never being recorded in the first place.

2. Immediate Centralized Forwarding

Every monitored endpoint should forward its security event stream continuously to a centralized log server or SIEM with minimal latency, directly implementing the "forward first, delete second" defense against local log tampering described in Section I. Forwarding latency should itself be monitored as a security metric, a sudden gap in received events from a given endpoint is itself a suspicious indicator worth investigating.

3. Tamper-Evident Storage at the Central Collector

The central log server should itself be hardened using write-once storage configurations and/or cryptographic log chaining, closing the second-order risk that a sufficiently privileged attacker who reaches the central collector could retroactively alter historical records without detection.

4. Correlated Detection Rules Over Single-Event Alerting

SOC detection logic should prioritize correlation rules, multiple related signals within a defined time window, over alerting on any single log line in isolation, directly reducing both false-negative risk (a single suspicious-but-ambiguous event being dismissed) and alert fatigue (the same phenomenon documented for physical sensors in Topic 2.4) from high-volume, low-fidelity individual events.

Where auditing sits in the rest of Unit 4 The four topics of this unit are not four alternatives. Topic 4.1 establishes what is wrong with the device, Topic 4.2 makes the credential expensive to defeat, Topic 4.3 removes attack surface and closes known defects, and this chapter assumes all three were insufficient on some particular morning. That assumption is not pessimism; it is what makes the investment in auditing rational. An organisation that believes its preventive controls will hold has no reason to fund detection, and an organisation with no detection cannot know whether its preventive controls held.

Self-Check Review Questions

Question 1: Explain why simply logging security events locally on an endpoint is an insufficient defense against a determined attacker, and describe the architectural principle used to address this weakness.

An attacker who gains sufficient privileges on a compromised endpoint (local administrator on Windows, root on Linux) can directly delete or clear the local security log, erasing the primary evidence of how they gained access and what they did afterward, this is the local file log deletion / "Indicator Removal" threat vector this chapter centers on. The architectural defense is to continuously and immediately forward every log event off the endpoint to a centralized, secure log server (Syslog collector or SIEM) that the attacker does not control. Because the event has already been copied off-host by the time an attacker attempts to erase local evidence, deleting the local copy does not remove the centrally forwarded copy, preserving the evidence needed for later investigation.

Question 2: A SIEM alert fires because a user account accumulated 15 Event ID 4625 records within 5 minutes, followed by a single Event ID 4624 two minutes later. Explain what this correlated pattern likely indicates and why correlating both event types is more useful than alerting on 4625 alone.

Event ID 4625 records a failed logon attempt, and Event ID 4624 records a successful one. A burst of 15 failures in a short window followed by a success for the same account is a strong indicator of a successful brute-force or credential-stuffing attack: the attacker made repeated guesses (generating the failure burst) and eventually guessed correctly (generating the success). Alerting on 4625 events alone would generate excessive false-positive volume, since ordinary users mistype their passwords occasionally without being attackers. Correlating the failure burst with a subsequent success produces a much higher-confidence detection, since a legitimate user who eventually logs in successfully after a few mistyped attempts does not typically generate this specific volume-and-timing pattern.

Question 3: An organization forwards all endpoint logs to a central Syslog server, but a security review notes the server allows any administrator to freely edit or delete historical log files. Identify the remaining risk and describe two techniques that would address it.

Centralizing logs off individual endpoints defends against an attacker who compromises only a single endpoint, but it does not defend against an attacker (or a malicious/compromised insider) who additionally gains administrative access to the central log server itself, that privileged access would allow retroactive tampering with the very records meant to preserve forensic integrity. Two techniques that address this second-order risk are: (1) write-once (WORM) storage, which prevents any historical record from being modified or deleted after being written, even by an account with full administrative privileges over the storage system; and (2) cryptographic log chaining, in which each log entry incorporates a hash of the previous entry, so that altering any historical record breaks the hash chain for every subsequent entry and becomes mathematically detectable during an integrity check, even on storage that is not physically write-once.

Practice Questions

Question 1 4.4.D, A domain's Security logs for one weekend contain 4,900 Event ID 4625 records spread across 1,180 distinct accounts, a maximum of four failures against any single account, and zero Event ID 4740 records. What is the correct reading?
  1. Nothing occurred; the absence of 4740 lockout events shows that no account was under attack.
  2. A password spray. Depth was held under the lockout threshold on purpose, which is precisely why no lockout event was generated, the missing 4740s are a consequence of the attack's design, not evidence against it.
  3. A brute-force attack that was stopped by the lockout policy before any account could be reached.
  4. Ordinary user error, since four failures per account is within normal mistyping behaviour.
Question 2 4.4.D, Two 4625 events arrive from the same source. One carries Sub Status 0xC0000064; the other carries 0xC000006A. What does the difference tell an analyst?
  1. The first attempt used Kerberos and the second used NTLM.
  2. The first targeted an account that does not exist, the second an account that does exist with the wrong password, so the ratio across a run indicates whether the attacker is guessing usernames from a wordlist or already holds a real account list.
  3. The first was a network logon and the second was interactive.
  4. The first indicates the account was locked out and the second that it was disabled.
Question 3 4.4.A, Why is forwarding endpoint logs to a central collector in near real time the foundational control for endpoint detection, rather than simply enabling more local logging?
  1. Central collectors compress logs, so more history can be retained per endpoint.
  2. An attacker with administrative privileges on the endpoint can clear or truncate the local record; a copy that has already left the host is outside their reach, so the forwarding latency is the width of the window in which evidence can still be destroyed.
  3. Local logs are written in a proprietary format that cannot be analysed without a SIEM.
  4. Forwarding is required in order to generate Event ID 1102.
Question 4 4.4.B, An organisation wants to detect password spraying against its directory. Which control will actually do it?
  1. Lower the account lockout threshold from five failures to three.
  2. Alert on every Event ID 4625, so that no failure goes unexamined.
  3. A detection that aggregates across accounts, distinct accounts failing per unit time directory-wide, and distinct accounts touched per source address, because the attack is defined by breadth and every per-account counter is below its threshold by design.
  4. Alert on Event ID 4740 lockouts, which is the authoritative record of failed authentication.
Question 5 4.4.C, A team proposes alerting on every single failed logon across a 4,000-user estate. Evaluate the impact of that detection method.
  1. It is optimal: maximum sensitivity means no attack can be missed.
  2. It has a high true-positive rate and therefore few false positives.
  3. It produces an alert volume dominated by ordinary user error, and its practical effect is to reduce detection capability, analysts triage a queue they cannot clear, and genuine findings are lost in it or the rule is switched off.
  4. It is acceptable provided the alerts are stored rather than reviewed.
Question 6 4.4.B, All endpoint logs are forwarded to a central collector, but any administrator on that collector can edit historical files. What risk remains, and what addresses it?
  1. None; centralisation is the complete defence for audit integrity.
  2. An attacker or insider who reaches the collector can retroactively alter the record; write-once storage and hash chaining, in which each entry incorporates a digest of the previous one, make such alteration impossible or detectable.
  3. The collector may run out of disk; log rotation addresses it.
  4. Forwarded logs lose their original timestamps; NTP synchronisation addresses it.
Free Response 1 4.4.D Detect Attacks, You are handed one week of auth.log from a manufacturer's jump host. It contains: 2,100 failures from 198.51.100.23 against 700 distinct accounts, three attempts each, spread across five nights; 380 failures from 203.0.113.66 against the single account root, all marked invalid user, within nine minutes; and one Accepted password for tnovak from 198.51.100.23 at 02:51, followed four minutes later by a sudo ... USER=root ; COMMAND=/bin/bash line for the same account. (a) Classify each of the two failure patterns and give the specific log evidence for each classification. (b) State which of the three findings you would escalate first and justify the ordering. (c) The manufacturer's lockout policy is five failures in fifteen minutes. Explain what it did and did not do here. (d) Name two pieces of evidence outside this log file that you would need before writing "credential stuffing" in your report, and say what each would establish.
Free Response 2 4.4.C Detect Attacks, A hospital's security team is choosing between three detection methods for its 6,000 Windows endpoints: (i) alert on every 4625; (ii) alert only on 4740 account lockouts; (iii) alert when the count of distinct accounts generating 4625 events directory-wide exceeds a rolling baseline, and separately when a 4624 success follows failures from the same source. (a) Evaluate the impact of each method on detection capability and on the analysts who must consume it. (b) Explain the specific attack each method would miss. (c) Recommend a combination and justify it, including what you would do to keep the chosen method's alert volume sustainable. (d) The team notes that method (iii) requires logs from all 6,000 endpoints to be centrally available within seconds. Explain why that requirement is itself a security control and what an unexplained gap in a single endpoint's log stream should be treated as.

Answer Key

1. B4.4.D

Breadth of 1,180 with depth of four is the definitional shape of a spray, and four is below five. The absence of 4740 events is the expected consequence: an attacker who has read the lockout policy paces beneath it, so lockouts never occur. A inverts the inference and is the error the attack is designed to produce, treating a quiet lockout log as an all-clear. C contradicts the data on both axes; brute force is deep and narrow, and 1,180 accounts is not narrow. D ignores scale: four failures on one account over a weekend is unremarkable, but 1,180 accounts doing it simultaneously is not a property any population of users produces spontaneously, and the correct unit of analysis is the directory rather than the account.

2. B4.4.D

Within status 0xC000006D, the sub-status separates a non-existent account (0xC0000064) from a valid account with a wrong password (0xC000006A). The ratio across a run is diagnostic of the attacker's preparation: mostly invalid names indicates a generic wordlist, while mostly valid names indicates a real roster obtained from a directory dump, a breach, or public sources. A confuses the sub-status with the Authentication Package field. C confuses it with Logon Type. D assigns meanings that belong to other codes entirely; a lockout is recorded as its own event, 4740.

3. B4.4.A

Clearing the local record is a documented late-stage technique, MITRE ATT&CK T1070, and it works against any evidence still resident on the compromised host. Forwarding converts the problem into a race the defender can win by shortening the interval, which is why forwarding latency is measured as a security property rather than an operational one. A names a storage benefit that has nothing to do with the threat. C is false; both the Windows Event Log and syslog are readable locally with built-in tools. D reverses the mechanism: 1102 is generated by the local log service when the log is cleared, whether or not forwarding is configured, forwarding is what ensures the 1102 survives.

4. C4.4.B

A spray is defined by breadth, and every per-account counter it produces is deliberately sub-threshold, so detection must operate at a granularity the attack cannot stay under: the directory as a whole, and the source address. A tightens a counter the attack already avoids, and buys a denial-of-service lever and a heavier help-desk load in exchange for nothing. B is a volume problem masquerading as sensitivity; the resulting queue is dominated by user error and the real pattern is invisible inside it. D monitors the events a competent spray never generates, which is the same mistake as A expressed as a detection rather than a prevention.

5. C4.4.C

Evaluating a detection method means evaluating what happens to its output. Across 4,000 users the base rate of benign failed logons, mistyped passwords, stale cached credentials on phones, expired service accounts, is high enough that the alert stream is almost entirely noise, and the predictable outcomes are an unclearable queue, desensitised analysts, and eventual suppression of the rule. Sensitivity that is not actionable is not detection. A mistakes sensitivity for capability. B is simply wrong on the arithmetic; when the benign base rate is high, even an accurate test produces mostly false positives. D describes retention, which is valuable for investigation and is not a detection method, and the question asked about detection.

6. B4.4.B

Centralisation defeats an attacker who holds one endpoint. It does not defeat one who reaches the collector, and the collector is a high-value target precisely because it holds the evidence. Write-once storage removes the ability to alter a written record even with full administrative rights; hash chaining makes alteration detectable by breaking the digest linkage for every subsequent entry. A stops the analysis one layer short. C and D name genuine operational concerns, capacity and clock synchronisation, but neither is the integrity risk the question describes, and neither would prevent an administrator from editing a file.

FR 14.4.D

Model answer. (a) The activity from 198.51.100.23 is a password spray: breadth of 700 accounts against a depth of three, paced over five nights. Each of those facts is the evidence, the breadth rules out brute force, the depth of three shows deliberate pacing beneath the five-failure policy, and the multi-night spread shows evasion of any short-window rate limit. The activity from 203.0.113.66 is a brute-force run from a generic wordlist: depth of 380 against a single account within nine minutes, and every attempt marked invalid user, which establishes that root does not exist as a login on this host and therefore that the attacker had no knowledge of the environment. (b) The tnovak success is escalated first, and by a wide margin. It is the only finding that is not an attempt: a success from the same address that had just failed against 700 accounts is a compromise, and the sudo ... USER=root four minutes later shows privilege escalation and moves this from an authentication finding to an intrusion requiring containment. The spray is second, because it is ongoing and other accounts may already have fallen. The brute force against a non-existent account is third; it is loud, unskilled, and cannot have succeeded. (c) The policy did exactly what it was written to do and nothing more. It bounded depth, which is why the brute force from 203.0.113.66 could not have reached a real account. It was irrelevant to the spray, because three attempts per account over five nights never approaches five in fifteen minutes, the attacker read the threshold and stayed under it. A per-account counter cannot express a directory-wide pattern; that requires an aggregate detection. (d) Two external pieces of evidence. First, the attempted passwords matched against a known breach corpus: if the credentials that were tried appear as previously breached pairs for these specific users, that establishes provenance and therefore stuffing rather than spraying. Second, the composition of the target account list, whether it corresponds to a real roster including accounts that do not exist in this directory, which suggests an externally sourced list, or to a generic wordlist. Corroboration from the identity provider that the same account list was attempted against unrelated services would also serve.
Look for: both patterns classified by breadth and depth with the numbers cited, not by volume; invalid user read as evidence about the attacker's knowledge; the success plus sudo escalated above both attack patterns with a stated reason; an explicit statement in (c) that the lockout policy operates at the wrong granularity for a spray; and, in (d), evidence that establishes provenance rather than restating the log.

FR 24.4.C

Model answer. (a) Method (i) has near-total coverage of failed authentication and near-zero usable output: across 6,000 endpoints the benign base rate produces an alert stream dominated by mistyped passwords and stale cached credentials, so analysts cannot clear the queue, genuine findings are buried, and the rule is eventually suppressed, a method whose net effect on detection capability is negative. Method (ii) has the opposite profile: very low volume, essentially no false positives, and each alert is meaningful, but it only fires for attacks that breached a per-account threshold. Method (iii) matches the granularity of the detection to the granularity of the attack, and the correlated success-after-failures rule converts a low-value event into a high-value one by context; volume is moderate and each alert carries a specific hypothesis. (b) Method (i) misses nothing in principle and everything in practice, because the finding is present in a queue nobody can read. Method (ii) misses password spraying entirely and by construction, since a competent spray generates no lockouts, and it misses any successful compromise, because success is not a lockout. Method (iii) misses a slow attack that stays within the rolling baseline, and it depends on that baseline having been established during a period that was itself clean. (c) Deploy (iii) as the primary detection and retain (ii) as a low-volume secondary, since a lockout burst is cheap to monitor and does indicate a naive attack in progress. Do not deploy (i) as an alert, but retain all 4625 records for investigation, the distinction between what is alerted on and what is retained is the one that makes the volume sustainable. Keep the volume manageable by baselining per business unit rather than globally, allowlisting known scanners and service accounts, suppressing the known-benign sources of repeated failure, and reviewing thresholds on a schedule rather than after a flood. (d) Sub-second central availability is a control because it bounds the window in which an attacker who has taken an endpoint can destroy the record of how they took it; the shorter the interval, the less there is to lose. An unexplained gap in one endpoint's stream should be treated as a detection in its own right, not as a monitoring fault: the plausible causes include an agent stopped, logging disabled, or the host taken offline, all of which are attacker actions as readily as operational ones, and the absence of evidence is the one signal an attacker cannot produce without producing it.
Look for: impact evaluated in terms of what analysts can act on rather than sensitivity alone; the specific claim that a spray generates no 4740 events; a recommendation that separates alerting from retention; and, in (d), the gap treated as a finding rather than a data-quality issue.
